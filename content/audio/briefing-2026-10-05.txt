At least eighty-two people have died in a gold mine collapse in Sudan's West Kordofan... and survivors say paramilitaries standing by with two excavators refused to dig the trapped miners out. It's the country's fourth major mine collapse this year, and local miners say the Rapid Support Forces helped run the site at Al-Zaraa. Both sides in Sudan's war pay for it with gold, so the men underground were financing a conflict that wouldn't rescue them.

In politics. <short pause>
Washington has answered Iran's seven-day plan to end the war, passing its reply through Qatar, and Tehran says it's reviewing the response without publishing a single term. The plan trades seven days of ceasefire, at least twelve billion dollars in frozen assets and a lifted blockade for a reopened Strait of Hormuz, the channel that carries roughly a fifth of the world's oil. That same weekend Iran's oil minister resigned with no reason given, and the head of the state oil company now also runs the ministry that has to sell crude through an American naval blockade.
<long pause>
In Ethiopia, federal troops and the allied Tigray Peace Force entered Mekelle on Saturday after the Tigray People's Liberation Front withdrew, taking the airport and other key sites in the regional capital. The last war there, from twenty twenty to twenty twenty-two, killed hundreds of thousands by most estimates, and the Pretoria peace deal that ended it never settled who actually governs Tigray. Residents now fear they're watching that war begin again.
<long pause>
Taiwan's presidential office is calling it transnational repression... after the FBI arrested a thirty-four-year-old woman in Los Angeles accused of tracking the president's son. Wanying Zhang allegedly sent officials in China photos, video and licence plates belonging to his family, then headed for a flight to Shanghai. She's accused, not convicted, and the case will test how far one state's reach extends over the families of another's leaders when they live abroad.
<long pause>
Brazil may be heading for a second round, with Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, son of the former president, ahead of President Lula by forty-nine percent to forty-two in early returns. Only about two percent of ballots had been counted, though, and polls before the vote had put Lula in front. If nobody clears fifty percent of valid votes, the two meet again on the twenty-fifth of October, in an election that's also filling all five hundred and thirteen seats of the lower house.
<long pause>
Across India, anger at the Election Commission is spreading: police detained nearly five hundred people on Ashoka Road in New Delhi, a journalist among them, and about six thousand marched in Mumbai on Sunday. Their target is the Special Intensive Revision of the voter rolls, a process that drops names from the lists, overseen by Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar. In a democracy of nearly a billion voters, who gets removed before polling day can matter as much as who campaigns.
<long pause>
Egypt's Supreme State Security Prosecution has ordered a journalist from the fact-checking outlet Matsadaqsh held for fifteen days on terrorism and false-news charges. The Egyptian Initiative for Personal Rights says the National Security Agency kept him incommunicado before he surfaced, and five of his colleagues are still missing. Checking facts is being prosecuted as spreading false news... and nobody's said where the other five are.
<long pause>
In Valencia, Spanish police fired rubber bullets at protesters gathered outside a property summit. Rents have almost doubled in ten years, according to the listings site Idealista, and on Friday parliament rejected two tenant-protection decrees, the closer of them by just six votes. Pedro Sánchez's minority government doesn't have the numbers to try again, so the argument over housing has moved from the chamber to the street.
<long pause>
Volodymyr Zelenskyy says Ukraine's ready for three-way talks with Russia and the United States, and that Washington wants technical-level meetings by the end of October. American ceasefire efforts have been paused since the war on Iran began, and Kyiv's offering to meet in the United Arab Emirates or anywhere else Washington proposes. Moscow's been contacted, he says... its answer hasn't been made public.

On the economy. <short pause>
Middle East crude exports peaked at twenty-two and a half million barrels a day in late September, according to the data firm Kpler, well above the eighteen million average before the war. That's despite at least seven attacks on tankers around Hormuz, so the oil is moving while the danger of carrying it keeps climbing. Drivers are still paying for the risk: American gasoline averaged a record four dollars thirty-three a gallon in September, and about a third of US states have cut fuel taxes just weeks before November's midterms.
<long pause>
Off Yemen's coast, blasts erupted beside a tanker near Mokha, according to Iran's state-affiliated Mehr news agency, and the UK's maritime trade operations office says the crew are safe without naming the ship or a cause. Yemen's government says it's launched an offensive against the Houthis, who, the Russian state agency TASS reports, seized Mokha and the Bab el-Mandeb coast in mid-September. About twenty-seven ships a day now cross that strait against a normal twenty-nine, a small gap that would widen quickly if the fighting reaches the water.

In science. <short pause>
In China's Zhejiang province, weekly flu vaccinations flipped from growing seven point three percent to shrinking nine percent after vaccine-safety events tied to misinformation. The study, in the journal N-P-J Digital Public Health, used real vaccination records rather than surveys, comparing weekly trends before and after three such events. It shows association in time, not proof of cause, but it's rare evidence of what people actually did rather than what they told a pollster they'd do.
<long pause>
On Australia's Great Barrier Reef, a single giant clam shell has given researchers about thirty years of daily records, written in nearly ten thousand nine hundred growth lines. Oxygen isotopes in the shell preserve sea temperature and carbon isotopes preserve rainfall, read from seven hundred and eighty-four drilled samples. They show summers from about seventeen eighty-five to eighteen twenty-seven ran cooler than today's while winters were much the same... a baseline from decades before anyone was measuring.

In technology. <short pause>
The crypto exchange OKX and Intercontinental Exchange, owner of the New York Stock Exchange, have filed to offer tokenized stock trading around the clock. A tokenized share is a blockchain version of a stock that carries the same dividends and voting rights, and the venue would open with more than sixty US-listed companies. Those companies didn't ask for this, they've got thirty days to object, and a filing isn't yet a launch.
<long pause>
Iran has summoned Norway's ambassador over Starlink, according to the state-affiliated Mehr news agency. Tehran's argument is that Norway is the registry state for the satellites at the International Telecommunication Union, and that the US and Israel use the network for what it calls interventionist actions. No reply from Norway or the company's been reported, but the complaint shows how little a government can do about internet that arrives from orbit, beyond protesting to another capital.
