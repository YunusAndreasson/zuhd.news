This is your briefing for the third of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo has now killed four thousand and eighteen people... with eight thousand three hundred confirmed cases. The World Health Organization says it's on track to pass the eleven thousand deaths of the West African epidemic a decade ago, the worst ever recorded. And this week, the UN says, soldiers burned a transit centre for patients near Bunia, forcing nineteen thousand people to flee at the very moment responders need them within reach.

In politics. <short pause>
In Iran, an investigation by Drop Site News says US Tomahawk missiles killed at least one hundred and fifty-six people at a school in Minab on the twenty-eighth of February, the first day of the war. Its count includes one hundred and twenty children and twenty-six teachers, built from two months of interviews with survivors, parents, doctors and first responders. Iranians remember nineteen eighty-eight, when a US warship shot down a civilian airliner and killed two hundred and ninety people, and the families in Minab are asking whether anyone will answer this time.
<long pause>
Tigrayan fighters have seized three airports held by Ethiopia's federal government, and Ethiopia and Eritrea expelled each other's diplomats on Thursday. That's the unravelling of the twenty twenty-two Pretoria agreement, which ended a war that killed hundreds of thousands of people. Eritrea has moved toward Tigray to block Ethiopia's ambitions for a Red Sea port, and with Egypt among Eritrea's allies, a regional conflict could become a Horn of Africa war.
<long pause>
Thousands gathered in Mumbai's Shivaji Park despite a police ban, called by a youth movement that named itself the Cockroach Janta Party. They accuse India's Election Commission of purging voters to favour the ruling party, after a special revision of the rolls struck one hundred and thirty million names, which the commission says were duplicate or ineligible. Organiser Abhijeet Dipke has given the chief election commissioner until the tenth of October to resign, or face a march on Delhi.
<long pause>
From Nigeria's Benue State, villagers have told Premium Times that police shot dead seven mourners at a funeral vigil in twenty twenty-four. Residents accuse a unit called Operation Zenda, set up to fight banditry, of killings, disappearances and burned homes... six years after nationwide protests forced Nigeria to disband another police squad, known as SARS, over the same pattern. A court ordered police to pay fifty million naira over the death of a detainee, Terna Chado, and his family says it hasn't received any of it.
<long pause>
French pupils disrupted five hundred and sixty-four high schools on Friday, and riot police met them in the streets. About two thousand people have been detained since the movement began, and the justice minister says ninety percent of them are minors. What the students want is ordinary... classes smaller than thirty-five, teachers who turn up, rooms that aren't overheated, and no cuts to the education budget.
<long pause>
On the Caribbean coast of Honduras, officials abandoned a court-ordered survey of Garífuna land after receiving threats. The Garífuna descend from Africans and Indigenous islanders exiled by the British from Saint Vincent in seventeen ninety-seven, and in twenty fifteen the Inter-American Court of Human Rights ordered Honduras to title their land and remove outsiders. InSight Crime reports the state has instead moved to accusing Garífuna leaders of organised crime, on a coast that traffickers want empty.
<long pause>
Brazil votes for a president on Sunday, and two days before the polls the United States suspended consular services and Australia closed its embassy, both citing security. The contest is between Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Flávio Bolsonaro, whose father's supporters stormed Congress, the Supreme Court and the presidential palace in January twenty twenty-three. Federal police carried out searches in São Paulo and the Federal District on the same day, and Brazilians now go to vote with foreign governments openly betting on trouble.
<long pause>
Yemen may be heading back to full-scale war. Six people briefed on the plan told Reuters that Saudi Arabia is preparing an offensive against the Houthis, with Yemeni forces leading on the ground, Saudi air strikes behind them, and possibly more than one hundred thousand troops. The prize is Bab el-Mandeb, the strait guarding the Red Sea route that replaced the closed Strait of Hormuz, in a country where the UN estimates the last Saudi-led campaign cost several hundred thousand lives.

On the economy. <short pause>
Vietnam wants to make semiconductors, but liquefied natural gas supplies just one point six percent of its electricity. Chip plants need power that never flickers, a lesson the north learned in twenty twenty-three when summer blackouts shut down factories, so Hanoi aims to get ten percent of its electricity from gas by twenty thirty. Since February the Iran war has lifted import prices by one hundred and forty-six percent, and the consultancy BRG puts the cost of that target at fourteen to eighteen billion dollars a year.
<long pause>
Peru has had nine presidents in a decade, and its economy still grew three point four percent last year. An autonomous central bank held inflation to one and a half percent while Brazil, Mexico, Chile and Uruguay all ran at more than twice that, in a country that lived through hyperinflation above seven thousand percent in nineteen ninety. Copper sold to China carries the boom whoever governs... but poverty is rising, which means the stability hasn't reached Peruvian households.
<long pause>
The first project under the minerals agreement between Ukraine and the United States has been signed, and it's worth about thirty million dollars. Washington pressed that agreement on Kyiv in April twenty twenty-five, and the new venture with Ukraine's BGV Group targets rare earths, beryllium and zirconium. It's a small sum, but it tests a large question, which is who controls a country's resources when its security guarantor is also its investor.
<long pause>
Colombia's riverbed gold mining grew four and a half percent to more than one hundred and eighteen thousand hectares, according to a UN report. Sixty-one percent of that growth fell on the collective lands of Afro-Colombian communities, descendants of the enslaved people Spain forced to pan those same rivers. Three-quarters of the output is illegal, and the mines minister says nearly half the mined area also grows coca, so gold and cocaine now share ground.

In science. <short pause>
Corals treated with probiotics kept their colour through a twenty twenty-two heat wave in the Red Sea. Researchers dosed them eight times in fifteen days with bacteria taken from healthy local coral, and it's the first sign the method works on a real reef rather than in a tank. With the world's reefs living through the most widespread bleaching ever recorded, the open question is whether a treatment applied by hand can ever reach the scale of an ocean, in sha'Allah.

In technology. <short pause>
Reports in Washington say Donald Trump will name his Director of National Intelligence, Jay Clayton, as the official in charge of artificial intelligence. The intelligence director's office was created after the September eleventh attacks to coordinate the spy agencies, and this would put the same person over the development of a civilian industry. None of the reports say whether Clayton keeps the intelligence post, and the appointment hasn't been announced.
<long pause>
Journalists at El Faro, the Salvadoran news outlet, were targeted with Pegasus spyware at least two hundred and twenty-six times between June twenty twenty and November twenty twenty-one. A California judge dismissed their lawsuit against the maker, Israel's NSO Group, on Wednesday, ruling they hadn't shown the state had jurisdiction. NSO says it sells only to governments... so the reporters still can't establish in court who was reading their phones.
<long pause>
Portugal's Omnidea has bought the rocket designs and brand of Orbex, the Scottish launch company that collapsed in February. Britain is still the only country to have built its own satellite launcher and then abandoned it, in nineteen seventy-one, and Orbex was meant to end that story. Omnidea is simplifying the Prime rocket for easier manufacture and dropping its larger sibling, so a launcher designed in Scotland now depends on Portuguese owners to fly.
