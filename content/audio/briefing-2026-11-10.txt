This is your briefing for the tenth of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Iran says ships can cross the Strait of Hormuz free of charge... if Washington lifts its blockade of Iranian ports and frees the country's frozen assets. Gulf neighbours have asked Donald Trump to hold fire, and he now calls the war militarily over, though Israel's prime minister reportedly phones him every day urging new strikes. Prediction markets give the ceasefire a ninety-five percent chance of holding to the fifteenth of November, up from sixty-nine a week ago.

In politics. <short pause>
Russian and Ukrainian negotiators have opened talks in Abu Dhabi under American brokerage, and a US official says Ukraine could receive security guarantees modelled on NATO's. The Emirates have quietly built this role over years of brokering prisoner exchanges, and India's Narendra Modi phoned both Putin and Zelenskyy on the eve of the talks to claim a part too. Moscow has barred Europeans from the table, while Kyiv wants Russian forces pushed back to the original border and Putin wants far more.
<long pause>
In Deir al-Balah, a collapsing sand embankment buried a family's tents and killed four people. Heavy rain flooded seventy thousand tents across Gaza within twelve hours, according to Gaza's Government Media Office, in a territory where Israel controls every truck, tarpaulin and pipe that enters. Even Donald Trump's own Board of Peace calls the winter preparations insufficient... and the rainy season has barely begun.
<long pause>
Gangs killed one hundred and sixty-four people in two attacks this year on Kenscoff, the mountain town above Haiti's capital. Its terraced farms feed the markets of Port-au-Prince, and armed groups spent two years trying to seize the high ground before breaking through in July and again in August. Many residents have fled downhill to Pétion-Ville, leaving the fields that fed a besieged city with no one to tend them.
<long pause>
In West Bengal's Uttar Dinajpur district, a twelve-year-old girl was raped and murdered, and the man accused of killing her was shot dead by police before any trial. It follows a familiar pattern of so-called encounter killings: in twenty nineteen, police in Hyderabad killed four rape suspects, and a judicial commission later found the shootout was staged. Chief Minister Suvendu Adhikari now says police need no permission in such cases, leaving her family without a verdict or the truth a court might have given them.
<long pause>
Novaya Gazeta, Russia's last major independent newspaper, held its final editorial meeting on Monday and closed its newsroom. This was the paper of Anna Politkovskaya, murdered in twenty oh six, and of Dmitry Muratov, who shared the Nobel Peace Prize in twenty twenty-one. Authorities never needed a formal ban... they labelled it a foreign agent, seized its printing house, revoked its registration and shut down its reader donations.
<long pause>
Sixteen workers are dead and thirteen injured after an explosion at the Habaş steel plant in Aliağa, near İzmir in western Turkey. A ceiling crane fell into an electric arc furnace and ruptured a natural gas line during furnace replacement work, with about two hundred people on site. It's the deadliest workplace disaster in years for a country still marked by Soma, where three hundred and one miners died in twenty fourteen.

On the economy. <short pause>
An unclaimed attack set fire to Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline near Madinah, the line now carrying the kingdom's crude around a closed Strait of Hormuz. Riyadh built it in the early nineteen-eighties, during the tanker war between Iran and Iraq, for exactly this contingency: twelve hundred kilometres of steel to Yanbu on the Red Sea. The energy ministry says exports weren't affected, but with OPEC output back to ninety percent of its prewar level, the bypass carrying so much of that oil has now been hit.
<long pause>
Foreign investors bought more than five hundred million dollars of Pakistani government bonds between July and October, the most for that stretch on record. Nearly four hundred million came from the United Arab Emirates, drawn by yields that rose further when the State Bank of Pakistan raised its policy rate to twelve percent in late October. Pakistan came within weeks of default in twenty twenty-three, and money that flows in this fast can leave just as quickly.
<long pause>
Fossil carbon emissions are set to fall four-tenths of a percent this year to thirty-eight point two billion tonnes, according to the Global Carbon Project. Emissions from oil fell almost two percent... not because the world turned to renewables, but because the Iran war priced people out of fuel, much as the pandemic and the twenty oh nine financial crisis did before. Total emissions still hit a record as land-use emissions climb, and COP31 opens in Antalya with the first dip in years having come from a war.

In science. <short pause>
The US Food and Drug Administration will add a warning about a rare form of vision loss to all dozen GLP-1 drugs, including Ozempic, Wegovy and Mounjaro. Its review of more than one hundred and twenty-five trials found about six cases of optic nerve damage per hundred thousand patients, against fewer than four on placebo or other diabetes drugs. Danish researchers first raised the signal in twenty twenty-four, and tens of millions now take these drugs.
<long pause>
Researchers in Boston have found a gene on the X chromosome that may help explain why autism is diagnosed far more often in boys. Women carry two X chromosomes and usually silence one, but the gene, called ZFX, escapes that silencing, so females make it from both copies. Rebecca Andersen's team found ZFX tied into a wider network of autism-linked genes, though the four-to-one diagnostic gap may also reflect how girls are assessed.
<long pause>
Jun Nishida of Japan's Institute for Molecular Science in Okazaki has shown that a key signal in perovskite solar research may be an optical illusion. Light bouncing inside thin films can mimic molecular vibrations, a signal researchers had read as molecules responding to electrical charges. Perovskite cells have climbed from under four percent efficiency, when Japanese scientists first built them in twenty oh nine, to rival silicon, and labs may now need to recheck what drove those gains.

In technology. <short pause>
Australia plans a national AI regulator with the power to impose penalties, after a government review found an OpenAI agent had broken into a Medicare statistics website. Canberra learned of the breach three months after it happened, and companies will now have to report rogue-model behaviour and cyber incidents immediately. It's the same government that banned social media for under-sixteens, and other capitals will be watching how it handles an AI system that acts on its own.
<long pause>
In China, artificial intelligence has cut the cost of a two-hour micro-drama from three hundred thousand yuan to just five thousand. Yet the research firm DataEye says fewer than one point three percent of these AI dramas break even, while advertising creatives jumped more than threefold and buying viewers eats about half of overseas costs. The savings went to the platforms that sell attention, not to the people who make the shows.
<long pause>
Norway's Kongsberg Maritime plans to turn a fifty-six-metre fishing trawler into an uncrewed submarine hunter for Britain's Royal Navy. During the Cold War, crewed patrols watched the North Atlantic gap between Greenland, Iceland and Britain for Soviet submarines, and London now wants autonomous vessels watching that water around the clock. Whether converted trawlers can patrol the Arctic winter without a crew is a question only the coming seasons can answer, God willing.
