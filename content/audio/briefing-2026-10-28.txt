The Iran war has now cost the United States more than forty-seven billion dollars... and that figure comes from a Pentagon estimate the public hasn't seen. Over twenty-nine billion of it would replace spent munitions, and more than six billion would replace lost aircraft and drones. It's being held back a week before the third of November midterms, while a ceasefire holds and voters are asked to judge a war without knowing its price.

In politics. <short pause>
Five more people died on Tuesday when a US military strike hit a boat in the eastern Pacific, taking the campaign's toll to two hundred and forty-three since September twenty twenty-five. Washington calls the trafficking groups terrorists, yet relatives of some of the dead say they were fishermen and workers, and none of them were named, charged or tried. Legal experts say lethal force against suspects outside any declared war is extrajudicial killing... and so far, no court has reviewed a single strike.
<long pause>
Across the river from the South Korean town of Gimpo, three North Korean self-propelled howitzers were seen aimed south from Kaepung County on Monday. It's a rare open display of artillery at a border where Kim Yo-jong announced loudspeakers last Thursday, after landmine blasts in September injured three South Korean soldiers. Pyongyang's troops and shells have already been tested in Ukraine, and Seoul must now decide whether these guns are a message... or a rehearsal.
<long pause>
Flávio Bolsonaro has left Brazil through no regular border post, a police official says, as the Supreme Court's order to surrender his passports in a money-laundering probe went unmet. Washington says it'll issue visas to him and his family, and a Trump aide has called it political asylum, sheltering a defeated candidate from his own country's courts. President Lula called the flight shameful, and he now has to manage relations with a government that's openly taken his rival's side.
<long pause>
Forces aligned with the United Arab Emirates are giving Ansarallah targeting help inside Yemen, four sources told Drop Site News, and two say Abu Dhabi passes intelligence directly. The two Gulf powers went to war in Yemen together in twenty fifteen, but Saudi Arabia sidelined the Emirates there earlier this year, and the old partners now appear to back opposite sides. For Yemenis, a decade of bombardment and hunger has become a proxy rivalry between their two former protectors.
<long pause>
Blogger Mano Shottas filmed his own fatal shooting in Ressano Garcia, on Mozambique's border with South Africa, while exposing chromium ore spilled along the road. A Forbidden Stories investigation found video showing law enforcement involvement, on a corridor where over a thousand trucks a day haul chromite to Maputo, spilling ore beside the Komati River. That ore feeds the global steel chain... and the people breathing its dust are the ones who've been silenced.
<long pause>
In Cairo, a fake charity emptied one Palestinian refugee's mobile wallet of twelve hundred dollars. Gazans who fled to Egypt can't get residency or work legally, and aid has thinned since the ceasefire, so anonymous forms collecting passport photos have become the bait. Victims without legal status can't easily turn to the police... which leaves the scammers working with almost no risk at all.

On the economy. <short pause>
Iran has threatened the South Korean shipowner Sinokor's tankers with legal action from the first of November, the first time it's named a single company over its Strait of Hormuz rules. Tehran's sanctioned strait authority already lists more than a hundred and twenty ships as legitimate targets, turning one of the world's busiest oil passages into a waterway it polices by its own law. Shipowners now have to weigh insurance, crews and cargo against a rulebook written in Tehran.
<long pause>
Those same oil prices are squeezing India, where traders say the Reserve Bank of India sold dollars on Wednesday to lift the rupee to ninety-five point oh two. Brent crude stood at a hundred and nine dollars seventy a barrel on the twenty-sixth of October, and because India imports most of its oil, every rise feeds dollar demand and drains the currency. Yet Jio Platforms, Reliance's telecom arm, rose twenty-one percent on its debut after India's largest ever share sale... a market prize built on the network hundreds of millions of Indians use to get online.
<long pause>
A century of rights over sixty-five billion barrels of Venezuelan oil has gone to a firm run by businessman Alejandro Betancourt, under a deal signed by Delcy Rodríguez's government and the United States. María Corina Machado, back in Venezuela since Sunday, says such deals don't serve Venezuelans, and her return splits an oil sector sitting on the world's largest proven reserves. Whether a future elected government would honour contracts signed under Maduro and Rodríguez is now the question every holder of them must answer.
<long pause>
Chevron and Shell's Nigerian units reported identical methane emissions for twenty twenty-two, and those figures form the baseline for Nigeria's pledge to cut methane by sixty percent. An independent review found only twelve of thirty company-year records filled all three required methane fields. Methane traps far more heat than carbon dioxide in its first decades, and if the starting line is copied rather than measured, no one can say whether the cut ever happens.

In science. <short pause>
Greenland lost ice for the thirtieth year in a row, one hundred and thirty-five billion tonnes of it, Danish scientists report. Snowfall still outweighed surface melt by five hundred and nineteen billion tonnes, but icebergs breaking away and warm water melting glacier fronts erased the entire gain. The ice sheet is now the largest present-day source of ice-driven sea level rise... and its fate is being decided less on the surface than where the glaciers meet the sea.
<long pause>
Astronomers using the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope have found a dead star whose debris is split into two parts by a clear gap. Two planetary bodies that survived their star's collapse into a white dwarf appear to be sculpting it, the same fate that awaits our own sun in about five billion years. It's a glimpse of what may become of our solar system... and of which worlds might still be circling when it does.

In technology. <short pause>
A sixteen-year-old bug in E D K two, the open-source firmware code that virtually every server maker builds on, lets attackers run their own code before Secure Boot even loads. Rated nine point five out of ten, the flaw overflows a five-hundred-and-four-byte buffer in the network-boot driver when an oversized error message arrives. Firmware sits beneath the operating system... so a fix has to travel through AMI, Insyde, Phoenix and every vendor that ships their code.
<long pause>
China's secret reusable spaceplane nearly matches the US Space Force's X thirty-seven B, the Defense Intelligence Agency confirmed in a new unclassified report. The Chinese craft has payload-bay doors, tricycle landing gear and a similar tail extension, though its wing is longer from front to back. Both powers now fly long-duration orbital vehicles on undisclosed missions... in a military contest that existing space treaties barely touch.
<long pause>
Coinbase says a small subset of its customers' emails and phone numbers leaked through Amplitude, an analytics vendor, though it says its own systems and customer funds weren't touched. In Britain, inspectors found most police supplier contracts include no ongoing cybersecurity checks, and the Police Digital Service logged two hundred and fifty-two cyber incidents in just three months. The pattern's the same in both... an institution's security is only as strong as its weakest contractor, and attackers know exactly where to look.
