Oxfam has found that sources of contamination now cover more than ninety-nine percent of Gaza... in the air, the water, the food and the soil. Israel's bombardment destroyed all six of the strip's wastewater plants, so forty thousand cubic metres of raw sewage pour into the sea every day. Under a ceasefire, Israeli forces moved the yellow line again on the twenty-fifth of October, pushing families west into a smaller and more poisoned space.

In politics. <short pause>
In a federal courtroom in Abuja, a farmer named Ukeyima David testified that gunmen killed his wife and three sons in the Yelwata massacre, which reportedly left more than a hundred and fifty people dead in Benue State last year. Nine men face fifty-seven terrorism counts, and he told the court that police and soldiers stationed at the market said they were outnumbered during a four-hour attack. Killings in Nigeria's Middle Belt, rooted in decades of competition between farmers and herders over shrinking land, rarely reach a courtroom at all... so this trial tests whether they can.
<long pause>
Ukraine says seven naval drones sank the Russian corvette Merkury in a harbour in eastern Libya, nearly eighteen hundred kilometres from Crimea. The warship and a cargo vessel were berthed in territory held by Khalifa Haftar, whose ports have anchored Russia's military supply line into Africa since the Wagner group arrived there in twenty nineteen. Libya isn't party to this war, and the government in Tripoli has demanded that Kyiv explain itself, while Libyans now live beside a front line that was never theirs.
<long pause>
At least two thousand one hundred and ninety-one people born in Kyrgyzstan have died in Russia's war on Ukraine, according to a classified database of death records compiled by Rosstat, Russia's statistics agency, and obtained by the investigative outlet Kloop. Only two hundred and twenty-eight of them were Kyrgyz citizens, and Moscow has courted Central Asian labour migrants with fast-track Russian passports in exchange for military service. The records stop in August, so the full toll remains uncounted.
<long pause>
Armenia's prime minister, Nikol Pashinyan, has staked his job on removing the constitution's claim to Nagorno-Karabakh, and Azerbaijan's foreign minister says that's the last obstacle to a peace treaty. The clause traces back to Armenia's nineteen ninety declaration of independence, and it outlived Azerbaijan's twenty twenty-three offensive that sent more than a hundred thousand Armenians fleeing the region. His party holds sixty-four of a hundred and five seats, but calling a referendum takes seventy votes, so he'll need the opposition to get there.
<long pause>
Qatar says it's working with Washington and Tehran to restart talks, according to Turkish state broadcaster TRT World and Iran's Mehr news agency. A memorandum signed in Islamabad in June stalled over sanctions and the American naval blockade, and Qatar's prime minister said the region can't bear another round of escalation. Gulf states that host US bases would absorb the first blows of any new conflict... which is why Doha, a mediator from Afghanistan to Gaza, keeps the channel open.
<long pause>
Pakistan's electronic media regulator has ordered infant formula advertising off every channel it licenses, after the health ministry demanded enforcement of a twenty oh two law that already barred formula promotion. That law grew out of the World Health Organization's nineteen eighty-one code on marketing breast-milk substitutes, written after campaigners accused multinationals of selling formula to mothers who lacked clean water to mix it. The order holds until advertisers obtain permission, and the real test is whether a law ignored for twenty-four years will now be enforced.

On the economy. <short pause>
Brazil's Finance Ministry has cut its growth forecast for twenty twenty-seven from two point three percent to one point eight, and raised its inflation estimate to four and a half percent, well above the three percent target. Private economists are gloomier still, expecting just one percent growth, and stubborn inflation keeps the central bank's benchmark rate high, which slows lending and swells the cost of public debt. Brasília is betting the economy can slow without stalling... a bet the market isn't yet willing to take.
<long pause>
Gold rebounded two point four percent on Monday to about three thousand six hundred and seventeen dollars an ounce, after falling nearly nine percent last week, its steepest weekly drop since March. Analysts say the tech selloff pushed traders to reprice American interest rates, and lower expected rates favour an asset that pays no yield. Central banks from Beijing to Ankara have been steady buyers of bullion for years, so whether this was a bottom or a pause now rests on the next US inflation figures.
<long pause>
The Federal Reserve's inspector general is examining whether Michelle Bowman, its top bank supervisor, broke the central bank's blackout rules by addressing a private Bank of America dinner the day after a rate vote. Those rules silence officials from the second Saturday before a meeting until it's over, so that no private audience hears policy signals before the public does... and this audience was a bank she regulates. Senator Elizabeth Warren pushed for the probe, and its findings will show whether the Fed holds its own officials to the standard it asks markets to trust.

In science. <short pause>
America's Food and Drug Administration has approved Welgemda, known in development as ribitol, the first drug for any form of limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, a family of inherited diseases that waste the muscles of the hips and shoulders. It's cleared only for the subtype known as two-i, and its maker, BridgeBio, estimates just two to three thousand American patients qualify... exactly the tiny market the nineteen eighty-three Orphan Drug Act was written to make worth serving. The company hasn't disclosed a price, and for drugs this rare, that number often decides who actually gets treated.
<long pause>
Researchers at Pioneer Labs have designed a twenty-tonne system that would use bacteria to grow habitats on Mars, needing nothing from Earth once it lands. A forty-kilowatt reactor would melt ice, and a strain of the microbe Cupriavidus necator, bred to survive the toxic perchlorates in Martian soil, would feed on nutrients drawn from the ground itself. The same bacterium has long been studied on Earth for turning carbon dioxide into plastic, and while the design is sized to fit a single SpaceX Starship, for now it exists only on paper.

In technology. <short pause>
Colombia's information ministry has asked X to ban former President Gustavo Petro, accusing him of inciting violence against President Abelardo de la Espriella's government after he posted a video of protesters in Cali on Saturday. Florida Congresswoman María Elvira Salazar then petitioned Elon Musk to do the same. When a government asks a foreign-owned platform to silence its predecessor, one company's moderation call ends up weighing more than Colombia's own courts.
<long pause>
Germany's states want camera glasses that can film people unnoticed restricted, a rule that would probably ban many popular models. Hamburg's data regulators found the recording light on Ray-Ban Meta glasses hard to see, and current law bans only devices designed for covert recording. It's the same unease that drove Google Glass off the consumer market more than a decade ago... except this time the product is already on sale everywhere.
<long pause>
Xiaomi's SU7 electric car has opened for pre-orders in the United Arab Emirates at forty-six thousand two hundred and sixty dollars, the Chinese company's first sale through an authorised overseas dealer, ahead of any official launch abroad. The dealer, Gargash Prime, lists three models, with the YU7 at about fifty thousand dollars and the Skynomad at about fifty-seven thousand, and the Gulf has none of the tariffs the EU imposed on Chinese electric vehicles in twenty twenty-four. A phone maker that sold its first car only last year is testing export demand in the Gulf before it faces Europe.
<long pause>
Virginia and Oregon have sued SpaceX, now the parent of xAI, alleging its Grok chatbot was built and promoted without safeguards. The Center for Countering Digital Hate estimates Grok posted about three million sexualised images in eleven days... roughly twenty-three thousand of them appearing to depict children. The states are suing under consumer-protection law, a route around the nineteen ninety-six statute that shields platforms from liability for what users post, and the case will test whether a company answers for what its own machine creates.
