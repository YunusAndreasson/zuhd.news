This is your briefing for the twenty-first of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
At least twenty-three sailors are missing or dead... killed by drone strikes on merchant ships off Romania and Bulgaria, the latest spillover from Russia's war on Ukraine. NATO is now sending patrol aircraft, radars, drones and forensic teams to the Black Sea to trace the explosives. The people dying are commercial crews, not navies, and every strike near two alliance members pulls NATO closer to a war Washington is pushing Kyiv to end.

In politics. <short pause>
Volodymyr Zelenskyy has thanked Washington for restoring the early warnings that send Ukrainian civilians to shelters before Russian missiles and drones arrive. Kyiv says it missed those warnings three times last week, which the United States denies, and the dispute came days after Donald Trump eased sanctions on Moscow for a three-hundred-thousand-tonne diesel deal. Envoy Steve Witkoff now heads to Moscow, while Zelenskyy says Patriot interceptors that Europe has already paid for still await Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth's signature.
<long pause>
Turkey has sent a sixteen-article defence pact with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan to its parliament, the step that would make it a binding ten-year commitment. Like NATO's Article Five, it treats an attack on one as an attack on all three, joining an alliance member, the Gulf's largest military spender and the Muslim world's only nuclear power. Article Two, though, lets each state decide its own support, and Turkey's constitution still requires a separate parliamentary vote to deploy forces.
<long pause>
Ebola has reached Abuja, Nigeria's capital, where forty-four contacts, including health workers, are isolated for three weeks... and Kenya has confirmed a ninth case. Nigeria hasn't faced the virus since it contained an outbreak in twenty fourteen, and this strain, Bundibugyo, has no licensed vaccine or specific treatment. Health Minister Muhammad Pate calls the Abuja case imported rather than community spread, but one case in a city of three million will test surveillance across East and West Africa.
<long pause>
Drone attacks in Sudan have killed more than one hundred and sixty people, eighty of them in a single strike. The reports name no attacker, no town and no supplier, a measure of how little scrutiny reaches a war now in its fourth year and among the world's largest displacement crises. Meanwhile, the United States has granted Sudanese nationals protection from deportation... for now.
<long pause>
Floods, erosion and drying channels are forcing Bangladesh's Bede, river nomads who've lived on boats for generations, off the Jamuna and onto land. Estimates of their number range from seventy-five thousand to eight hundred thousand, because clinics, schools and civil registers assume a fixed address, and without one there's no national ID, no school place and no vote. It's climate displacement colliding with paperwork built for settled citizens, and it lands on one of the country's most marginalised Muslim communities.
<long pause>
The Palestinian Journalists Syndicate now counts more than two hundred and sixty reporters killed in Gaza. With Israel barring foreign reporters from the territory, the world's outlets rely on Palestinian freelancers paid by the story, who rarely get a safety net and cover their own medical treatment. What the world knows about Gaza depends on the people least protected while gathering it.
<long pause>
Brazil's runoff is a dead heat, with a new poll showing Flávio Bolsonaro at fifty point three percent of valid votes against Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's forty-nine point seven. Four years ago Lula beat Jair Bolsonaro, Flávio's father, by under two points, and the son now promises to use the conservative Congress elected on the fourth of October to change the Constitution. A Bolsonaro win would hand the farm lobby more sway over one of the world's top food suppliers.
<long pause>
Hundreds of Garífuna, an Afro-Indigenous people of the Caribbean coast, are camped outside the Supreme Court in Tegucigalpa. Five of them face usurpation charges over their own ancestral land after a violent eviction in San Juan on the sixth of July, land that drug traffickers use as cocaine corridors and businessmen want for palm plantations and hotels. Honduras's courts must now decide whether a people who've lived on that coast for more than two centuries can be called trespassers there.

On the economy. <short pause>
Sergey Lavrov went to Tehran offering to broker talks between Iran and the United States, with Brent crude above one hundred and nine dollars a barrel. Reuters calculations show Russia's biggest oil tax revenue doubled to about nine billion dollars by April, as buyers shut out of the Gulf switched to Russian barrels. Iran's Revolutionary Guards now say a third tanker has hit a mine in the Strait of Hormuz, a two-million-barrel supertanker left burning... so the mediator is also the war's biggest winner.
<long pause>
Japan's finance minister, Satsuki Katayama, told parliament that inflation driven by import costs is nearing a shift to inflation driven by wages. For a country that spent most of three decades fighting deflation, that's a historic turn, and the Bank of Japan's rate already sits at one point two five percent, a thirty-one-year high. Yet Tokyo is cutting the tax on food to cushion households from a weak yen, so fiscal relief and monetary tightening are pulling in opposite directions.
<long pause>
France's ten-year borrowing cost has hit four point nine six percent, its highest since twenty oh two, the year euro notes and coins first entered wallets. Investors doubt a fragmented National Assembly can pass a budget with fifty-four billion euros in savings, so they're charging more to lend. Every rise in interest leaves less for pensions, schools and health, and every failed budget makes the next one costlier to fund.

In science. <short pause>
Less snow on the Tibetan Plateau dries South Asia's monsoon and wets tropical Africa's, according to model runs published in a Nature climate journal. A bare plateau heats faster, speeding the tropical easterly jet, which carries moisture toward Africa... an echo of the link between Himalayan snow and Indian rainfall that colonial meteorologists first proposed in the eighteen eighties. If the mechanism holds, forecasters could anticipate harvests in the Sahel and South Asia together, two monsoons that sustain billions of people.
<long pause>
China's Zhejiang Lab trained an AI model on fossil depths from tens of thousands of sites, and it recasts three mass extinctions as slow declines rather than sudden cataclysms. Since the asteroid theory for the dinosaurs' end took hold in nineteen eighty, the field has leaned toward catastrophe, and its developers say this finding could force a rethink. Whether palaeontologists accept a machine's reading of the rock record is the next test.

In technology. <short pause>
Eight web security certificates belonging to the India-based authority eMudhra had private keys just three to eight bits long, until browsers blocked all eight on Tuesday. Transport Layer Security, the encryption behind every padlock in a browser, relies on keys of two thousand bits or more, and keys this short meant anyone could have forged trusted credentials for any website. Nobody has yet explained how keys that weak passed the audits meant to catch them.
<long pause>
The Federal Bureau of Investigation, with cyber agencies in Britain and Germany, says the SafePay ransomware gang has hit more than six hundred organisations since twenty twenty-four. Its attack on the contractor Conduent disrupted Medicaid, child support, food assistance and toll payments across the United States. And the way in is mundane: guessed passwords, known flaws left unpatched and phishing, then ordinary remote-access tools like AnyDesk to move inside.
