This is your briefing for the fifth of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
In Sudan, the staple grain sorghum now costs twice what it did a year ago... and the UN's coordinator there, Denise Brown, is warning that children will die of hunger. The aid plan meant to prevent that is less than half funded, at forty-six point seven percent, while the war that began in twenty twenty-three grinds on. Both the Rapid Support Forces and the army are blocking aid to areas the other holds, and striking the markets and power grids that keep people alive.

In politics. <short pause>
At his first press conference since Democrats won both chambers of Congress, Donald Trump said the Iran war will end whenever he wants it to. Vice President JD Vance says that same war cost Republicans their majorities, and the American Israel Public Affairs Committee spent fifty-five million of its eighty-four million dollars on races its candidates lost, against an eighty-seven percent win rate in twenty twenty-four. From January, Congress holds the purse and can pass war powers resolutions... but overriding a veto still takes two-thirds of both chambers.
<long pause>
Israel's President Isaac Herzog has accepted the certified election results and opened talks on who should form a government. Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc won fifty-three seats and Gadi Eisenkot's forty-eight, so nineteen seats held by Arab parties now decide who reaches the sixty-one needed to govern. The incumbent's answer is that letting those parties in would dismantle Israel — an argument that one in five citizens can't share power, made after the elections commission found no evidence of irregularities.
<long pause>
Across Hebron, in the occupied West Bank, Israel's army has displaced nearly five thousand Palestinians in eleven days. Soldiers sealed the city's entrances, went door to door taking identity cards, and ordered families out with only their essential belongings, while schooling stopped for eighty thousand students. It's a city already carved up by the nineteen ninety-seven Hebron Protocol, and no one has said when those families can go home.
<long pause>
In Berlin, Hubert Aiwanger, the fifty-five-year-old leader of Germany's Free Voters, has been found dead in his hotel room. He served as deputy to Bavaria's premier, Markus Söder, and his party is the junior partner in Bavaria's coalition government. Police and prosecutors have opened an inquiry to establish the cause of death and rule out the involvement of anyone else.
<long pause>
Colombian lawmakers have voted ninety to forty-four to end the legal path to peace talks, so armed groups will now be treated as criminal organisations. The new Submission Act replaces the nineteen ninety-seven Public Order Law — the framework that made possible the twenty sixteen accord with the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia, after more than half a century of war. Security forces gain a freer hand, but it's rural communities in the conflict zones who'll live with what replaces negotiation.
<long pause>
South Africa's local elections are projected to leave a hundred and ten councils hung, with metros like Johannesburg, Tshwane and Nelson Mandela Bay among those at stake. The African National Congress and the Democratic Alliance, partners in the national unity government since the ANC lost its majority in twenty twenty-four, are now running talks from head office and trading positions across councils. That means water, power and municipal budgets for millions will be settled in deals that residents never voted on.
<long pause>
San Salvador's government is quietly emptying the mesones of its Historic Centre — old shared tenements where families have lived and worked for years. The outlet Focos follows three women facing displacement, and says the official revitalisation plans simply ignore the people they remove. Who ends up with the land once the tenements are cleared is the question those plans don't answer.

On the economy. <short pause>
China's central bank is reining in a rallying yuan: on the fifteenth of November it raises the foreign-exchange reserve ratio by two points to six percent, locking up about twenty-four billion dollars. That rally follows a record trade surplus of nine hundred and thirty-eight billion dollars through September, and the People's Bank is also taking its digital yuan offshore, letting overseas banks, starting with Hong Kong branches, settle payments around the clock. How many foreign lenders join that rail will show how far Beijing can build payments that run outside the dollar system.
<long pause>
India now buys crude from about forty-three countries, rebuilding the supply chain of one of the world's largest oil importers around the war with Iran. Before the war, the Strait of Hormuz carried a fifth of the world's oil and gas; today the United Arab Emirates supplies fifteen percent of India's imports, and fuel prices at home have risen just seven percent while crude climbed more than forty-five. Petroleum secretary Neeraj Mittal says what worries him isn't the price... it's the volatility.
<long pause>
Farmers around Dera Ghazi Khan and Taunsa, in Pakistan's Punjab, are losing their sowing season after the Chashma Right Bank Canal ran ninety-six percent short in August. Then, on the fourteenth of September, a breach upstream in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa cut their supply to zero — and the groundwater beneath their two hundred and forty thousand acres is too brackish to fall back on. Punjab officials blame water theft upstream, and neither the provincial nor the federal government has said when the water will return.
<long pause>
Lagos's water regulator is threatening to seal unlicensed boreholes, in a city where the state water corporation has just over thirty-four thousand connections for twenty million people... about one percent coverage. A civic coalition says the state's own twenty seventeen environmental law exempts household boreholes from licensing. Without piped water to replace them, it isn't clear what those households would drink instead.

In science. <short pause>
Skeletons from a centuries-old English cemetery link the rickets that bent children's bones during the Industrial Revolution to climate shocks. Between roughly seventeen sixty and eighteen forty the disease was commonplace, and it's long been blamed on crowded cities, northern latitude and poverty — a span that also took in the cold, dim year without a summer, eighteen sixteen. The finding adds weather to that list... a reminder that climate stress shows up first in the bodies of poor children.

In technology. <short pause>
Hackers tried to push the storage temperature of plasma past boiling point at France's national blood service. They got in through a refrigeration contractor's control systems at three cold-storage sites, but monitoring alarms flagged the anomalies, and the French Blood Establishment says no blood products were lost. It's an attack built to destroy medical supplies rather than steal data, and it shows how a health system's weakest point can sit in a supplier's software.
<long pause>
Ukraine's English-language outlet, the Kyiv Independent, was hacked, and its subscribers were emailed a false message about a nuclear strike on Kyiv. The attackers had entered the site's management system and its newsletter service, reaching an audience of nearly three million readers a month, most of them outside Ukraine. When a newsroom's own channels can be turned against it, readers have to wonder how they'll know the next alert is real.
<long pause>
Six Asian airlines say passenger data was stolen through a single supplier — Amadeus, the Spanish company whose systems handle bookings across much of the industry. Japan Airlines alone counts ninety-two thousand customers affected, and Cathay Pacific says the attackers used login credentials assigned to its own system account. Amadeus says there's no evidence payment data was taken, but with one supplier behind so many carriers, a single stolen login can reach travellers across a continent.
