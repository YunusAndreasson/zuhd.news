This is your briefing for Tuesday, the sixth of October, twenty twenty-six.

Pakistan and Turkey have agreed to send troops to Saudi Arabia... after an emergency meeting of the Mecca Defence Alliance voted to activate what it calls collective deterrence measures against Yemen's Houthi movement. The statement gives no troop numbers and no dates, and it doesn't say whether those soldiers will stay on Saudi soil or cross into Yemen, where government forces say they've taken Dhubab, beside the Bab al-Mandab strait, and have launched an operation to retake a capital the Houthis have held since twenty fourteen. With Washington now promising to honour its own defence pact with Riyadh, a Yemeni ground war is becoming one with several foreign armies standing behind it.

In politics. <short pause>
Brazil is heading to a runoff after Flávio Bolsonaro finished ahead of President Lula, forty-seven percent to forty-five point one, overturning polls that had Lula leading by three points. Analysts say Lula lost ground on crime, and on interest rates that punish the eighty-two percent of Brazilian families carrying debt. The second round is on the twenty-fifth of October, and Bolsonaro has pledged to seek an amnesty... in a country where his father was convicted last year of plotting a coup.
<long pause>
In Bosnia and Herzegovina, Savo Minić has won fifty-five of Republika Srpska's sixty-four municipalities, and Milorad Dodik, who's barred from office himself, says his party and its allies now hold two-thirds of the entity's assembly. Preliminary results also show a Croat nationalist, Darijana Filipović, replacing the civic-minded Željko Komšić on the country's three-member presidency. The Dayton agreement that ended the war in nineteen ninety-five divided power by ethnicity, and thirty years on, each nationalist bloc has come out of this vote stronger.
<long pause>
Egyptian journalist Rasha Azab has been questioned on terrorism charges over a single tweet, then released on bail of ten thousand Egyptian pounds. Her post asked how long it takes to go from calling the Interior Ministry thugs to demanding the fall of the government, and the security prosecution summoned her without first telling her what she was accused of. Bail doesn't close the case, it leaves it hanging over her... in the same week six journalists from a fact-checking platform appeared before the same prosecutors, with their lawyers alleging torture.
<long pause>
Pakistan has confirmed its sixth polio case of the year, a seven-year-old boy in Khyber, less than a week after the fifth. Five of the six are in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, four of them in Bannu division, where insecurity keeps vaccination teams from reaching every child. Only Pakistan and Afghanistan still have the wild virus circulating, so the final stretch of a decades-long global eradication effort runs through these few districts.
<long pause>
A sitting judge of India's Supreme Court, Ujjal Bhuyan, has publicly called the deletion of names from the country's voter rolls arbitrary. Draft rolls in several states have lost large numbers of voters under what's called a special intensive revision, and petitions against it are waiting before Bhuyan's own court. Former Chief Justice Gavai has called the remarks judicial impropriety, which leaves the country arguing about who spoke rather than who can still vote.
<long pause>
Mexico has charged an alleged cartel commander known as R One over the murder of Carlos Manzo, the mayor of Uruapan. Officials say he ran a cell of the Jalisco New Generation Cartel called Los Rs, and that his son requested the on-camera killing of Valeria Márquez, a twenty-three-year-old influencer. Authorities already say a man called R Two has taken over the cell, so the arrest maps the network without yet dismantling it.
<long pause>
New York has declared a measles emergency, with ninety-two of the state's one hundred and eight cases arriving since the fifteenth of July. Next door, Pennsylvania has passed a thousand cases, the largest outbreak in the United States in three decades, in a country that declared the disease eliminated in the year two thousand. Governor Kathy Hochul's order widens who can give the vaccine through the fourth of November, but coverage has already slipped below the ninety-five percent needed to keep the virus contained.
<long pause>
Two crew members died when a Turkish-owned cargo ship carrying corn sank in the Black Sea, inside Romania's exclusive economic zone. Romania's coast guard and a helicopter rescued eleven others, and Ukraine's president says Russia struck the vessel, the Royad Mammadov, with two drones. Grain from this sea feeds much of the Middle East and Africa, and every ship lost there raises the price of insuring the next one.

On the economy. <short pause>
Brent crude fell a dollar ninety-three to one hundred dollars and thirty-two cents a barrel, even after the Group of Seven agreed to release one hundred million barrels from emergency stocks. It's unclear how much of that simply repeats March's four hundred million barrel release, and America's own reserve has dropped to two hundred and eighty-three million barrels, its lowest since nineteen eighty-two. Saudi Aramco has warned that refilling those reserves could take two years... and importers across Asia and Africa have no such cushion to draw on at all.
<long pause>
Japan's central bank may be about to declare that underlying inflation has reached its two percent target, three sources have told Reuters. For a decade the Bank of Japan justified near-zero rates by saying that goal was still out of reach, and it's already lifted its rate to one point two five percent, a thirty-one-year high. Markets expect another rise in December, and because so much of the world borrows cheaply in yen, a dearer yen would be felt far beyond Tokyo.
<long pause>
London trades nearly one hundred and ninety billion dollars of gold every day, and until now it's done so without a futures market of its own. Intercontinental Exchange, the American owner of the New York Stock Exchange, has launched contracts tied to London's daily auction price, after tariffs pushed New York futures above London prices and sent traders shipping bullion across the Atlantic. The question for the central banks buying gold to hold reserves outside the dollar is who ends up setting its price.

In science. <short pause>
More than two-thirds of health professionals surveyed say they've seen complications from extreme heat, the UN's climate chief Simon Stiell told talks in Nadi, Fiji, where he linked heat during pregnancy to stillbirth and premature birth. A UN report puts the adaptation needs of developing countries at between three hundred and ten and three hundred and sixty-five billion dollars, against twenty-six billion in adaptation finance in twenty twenty-three. Pacific island states are hosting these meetings to put that gap in front of the largest emitters before the year's main summit, and whether the money follows is, God willing, something we'll learn there.

In technology. <short pause>
Russian drones have temporarily cut internet access to one hundred thousand households around Kyiv, Ukraine says, after hitting data centres and telecom providers. Officials warn that strikes on providers since the twenty-third of September threaten banks, payment systems and public services, even though President Zelenskyy says more than eighty-five percent of Shahed drones are intercepted. Years of attacks on power stations taught Ukrainians to live with blackouts... and now the network that kept daily life running through them is the target.
<long pause>
Ethereum's developers have shipped a last-minute fix before a test that raises the capacity of the Sepolia test network from about sixty million units of gas to two hundred million. Bigger blocks fit more transactions, but they demand more from the computers that run the network, and the previous release of the Prysm validator software would've left its users producing undersized blocks. How far the limit can rise on the real network depends on how many ordinary operators can still afford to keep up.
<long pause>
Australia's air force has released an American hypersonic cruise missile from an aircraft for the first time, a milestone in a joint research programme that's been slowed by delays. The weapon, built by Raytheon with Northrop Grumman, uses a scramjet, an engine that only works once the missile is already flying faster than sound. Those delays have already forced the US Air Force to scale back its testing plans, so each remaining flight now carries more of the programme's weight.
