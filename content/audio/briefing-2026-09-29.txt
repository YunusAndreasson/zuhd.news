In one cramped detention cell in Minna, in Nigeria's Niger State, thirty-seven men arrested as illegal-mining suspects died... on a single day. Survivors say guards of the Civil Defence Corps packed as many as sixty-five people into the unventilated room, then sprayed a substance officers called mosquito repellent. Nobody has yet said who ordered the mass arrests of poor artisanal miners, or whether a single officer will answer for the dead.

In politics. <short pause>
A court in Rome has sentenced three Egyptian security officers to ten years for kidnapping Giulio Regeni, the Cambridge student tortured to death in Cairo in twenty sixteen. Italy tried them in absentia after Egypt spent years refusing to serve legal papers, and all the while Rome kept selling Cairo weapons and buying its gas. It's a rare European conviction of serving foreign intelligence agents... but while Egypt shields the men, the sentence is a record rather than a punishment.
<long pause>
Israel's given Dutch diplomats posted to Ramallah seven days to leave, after Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar stripped their accreditation. Since the Oslo accords of the nineteen nineties, only Israel can accredit diplomats who serve Palestinians, and it's now using that power over occupied land to punish governments that criticise settlement expansion. The Netherlands has said only that it regrets the move, while this week settlers attacked Palestinian olive pickers during a closure of the West Bank, in the harvest season that many families live on.

French police have arrested a hundred and sixty-four people as high school protests spread from Paris across the country. Students have blockaded their lycées since last week over overcrowded classrooms and teacher shortages, and La France Insoumise accuses Prime Minister Lecornu's government of heavy-handed policing of teenagers. With a nationwide public-sector strike following straight after, a minority government pushing austerity now faces the street on several fronts at once.

Iran's proposal on the Strait of Hormuz was rejected by President Trump on Saturday, and oil jumped four dollars a barrel. Days later, Russian state news agency TASS, citing Axios, reported that he'd ease sanctions and unfreeze Iranian assets in exchange for nuclear concessions, while Iran's state-run Mehr news agency said Foreign Minister Araghchi met Qatari mediators in New York. About a fifth of the world's oil passes through Hormuz... and after officials said an Iranian missile strike injured eight US Marines aboard an unmarked ship there, the space for a deal looks narrow.

Nearly two percent of Salvadorans are now behind bars, the highest incarceration rate in the world. A state of emergency declared in twenty twenty-two suspended constitutional guarantees, and an InSight Crime investigation finds the mass arrests that followed gutted the structures of MS Thirteen and Barrio Eighteen. Governments across Latin America are now copying the model, even as its cost inside El Salvador, in detention without trial and deaths in custody, is still being counted.
<long pause>
Vladimir Putin has raised the ceiling on Russia's army to two million, four hundred and forty thousand troops, according to state news agency TASS. The decree adds fifteen thousand five hundred since July, the third increase since March, and it moves wartime troop levels onto a permanent legal footing. NATO is still debating how to defend the Baltic states, and Moscow's message is that this force isn't temporary.

On the economy. <short pause>
Argentina's given Britain two weeks to halt a two point two billion dollar oil project off the Falkland Islands, which Buenos Aires calls the Malvinas. The war of nineteen eighty-two settled nothing about sovereignty in Argentina's eyes, so it's pressing its claim through logistics instead, restricting flights, shipping and suppliers to deter investors. For the islanders, the project could triple their economy and end their dependence on fishing... which is exactly why Buenos Aires wants it stopped.
<long pause>
Gold has slipped to a seven-week low, near four thousand, one hundred and twenty-five dollars an ounce. Saxo Bank's Ole Hansen points to US real yields at an eighteen-year high, as war-driven oil feeds fears of inflation and a metal that pays no interest loses its shine, even in wartime. Households across South Asia that save in gold are watching those savings shrink, and emerging economies that borrow in dollars may be next to pay.
<long pause>
Tata Trusts, the charities that control India's largest conglomerate, have moved to keep its holding company, Tata Sons, private. They propose merging it with two units so its revenue and asset ratios fall below the Reserve Bank of India's threshold for shadow banks, the rule that would otherwise force a listing. A listing would open the finances of a group founded in the eighteen-sixties to outside scrutiny, and the central bank must now decide whether this restructuring is an answer or an evasion.
<long pause>
A Senate committee in Islamabad has told the government to halt the sale of Pakistan's state electricity distribution companies. Under the eighteenth amendment of twenty ten, which devolved power to the provinces, the panel argues these companies perform provincial functions and must first go before the Council of Common Interests. If the sale goes ahead without provincial consent, consumers could face higher power bills, and the question of who owns the grid would land back with the provinces.

In science. <short pause>
The World Health Organization has confirmed that Bhutan has eliminated dog-transmitted rabies. Rather than culling, the Himalayan kingdom vaccinated more than ninety percent of its free-roaming dogs, sterilised them, and gave free treatment to anyone who was bitten. Rabies still kills roughly seventy thousand people a year, most of them children in Asia and Africa... and a small country of modest means has just shown that those deaths can be prevented.
<long pause>
Merck has quietly discontinued Recarbrio, a last-resort antibiotic for drug-resistant hospital pneumonia. Drugs held in reserve for resistant infections sell too little to justify making them, which is why most large drugmakers have left the field since the golden age of antibiotic discovery ended in the nineteen-sixties. Global health bodies rank antimicrobial resistance among the gravest threats to human health, yet the market keeps retiring the very tools they say we'll need.

In technology. <short pause>
Telecom companies have carried out roughly a hundred internet shutdowns across Africa since twenty sixteen, according to research published by Global Voices. Their operating licences turn them into the state's enforcement arm the moment protest moves online, and researchers highlight MTN's dealings with the presidential security service in Congo-Brazzaville. The companies say they're only obeying the law, but from Niamey to Brazzaville the network is often the first thing to go dark when politics turns.
<long pause>
Florida's attorney general has asked a state court to halt ChatGPT's development until a third party approves its safeguards, and to bar minors from using it. No state has gone this far against a frontier AI model itself, and the filing comes days after OpenAI paused its own frontier training. For three decades, American law has largely shielded platforms from liability for what their users post... but a chatbot's words are its own, and that's the gap Florida is aiming at.
