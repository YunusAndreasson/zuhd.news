This is your briefing for the twenty-sixth of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Saudi air defences intercepted ten ballistic missiles on Sunday... and the Houthis say they hit the East-West pipeline, the route Riyadh uses to export crude without passing through the Strait of Hormuz. Debris from two of those missiles injured two people and damaged three homes in Khamis Mushait, but Saudi Arabia hasn't confirmed any strike on the pipeline itself. That bypass was first built during the tanker war of the nineteen-eighties, and it matters so much now that Riyadh has also announced it'll store crude in Singapore's Jurong Rock Caverns from twenty twenty-seven, far beyond the strait's reach.

In politics. <short pause>
A movement born of student protests appears to have taken power in Serbia at the ballot box. According to Russian state news agency TASS, Aleksandar Vucic said the Students' List beat his Progressive Party in the snap election, in protests that began after a railway station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad in late twenty twenty-four and killed sixteen people. Vucic calls the result a revolution backed from abroad and predicts the new majority will sanction Russia by spring... which would end the balance Belgrade has kept between Moscow and Brussels for decades.
<long pause>
Pakistan's federally appointed governor in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has admitted, for the first time, that the province's elected chief minister is under house arrest. Sohail Afridi hasn't been seen since Islamabad imposed emergency rule on the eleventh of October, and Governor Faisal Karim Kundi says the assembly speaker, Babar Saleem Swati, is held too. The province is the stronghold of Imran Khan's party, squeezed since his imprisonment in twenty twenty-three... and its people now have no elected government at all.
<long pause>
Moscow is asking for the Europeans it spent years keeping out of the room. Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov says Europe, which backs Ukraine, must join the talks, because hopes after last year's Anchorage summit that Washington could dictate terms to Kyiv haven't worked out. With trilateral talks expected in November, the real question is who now holds leverage over the terms of peace.
<long pause>
Iranian state media say Saudi defence minister Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin will meet together in Tehran. None of those governments has confirmed it, and the announcement comes as President Trump says the US may strike Iran again, with energy facilities and nuclear sites reportedly among the options. If the meeting happens, the region's main Sunni military powers would sit in Tehran while missiles fall on Saudi cities... a sign that a regional security track may be forming outside Washington's control.
<long pause>
Israel struck Gaza's Islamic University twice in a matter of hours on Sunday. A drone first wounded seven female students, then an air strike killed three staff at an institution founded in nineteen seventy-eight as the territory's first university. Gaza's health ministry now counts one thousand five hundred and fifty-one people killed since the ceasefire began... a truce in name that hasn't stopped the dying.
<long pause>
Cairo's prosecutors charged Lina Attalah, editor of the independent outlet Mada Masr, within twenty-four hours of questioning her. It's the first trial ever brought against Mada Masr, whose website Egypt has blocked since twenty seventeen, and one hundred and nineteen rights groups, including Amnesty International, call the case unprecedented. State security prosecutors spent six hours asking her about the outlet's efforts to get a licence since twenty eighteen... a licence the state has never granted.
<long pause>
María Corina Machado has re-entered Venezuela after ten months away, by a route she hasn't disclosed. The US seized Nicolás Maduro in January and backed his deputy, Delcy Rodríguez, who went on to sign an oil deal, and talks between that interim government and a rival opposition faction now exclude Machado's movement. Last year's Nobel Peace laureate has come home to find herself shut out of the transition she spent years fighting for.
<long pause>
In Geneva, mediators have proposed that Sudan's warring sides start with something modest: coordinating secondary school exams. UN envoy Pekka Haavisto named exam coordination and prisoner exchanges as first steps, and twenty-one countries want them to open a path to a humanitarian truce and then a permanent ceasefire. The war between the army and the Rapid Support Forces began in April twenty twenty-three... and an entire generation of Sudanese students has already lost years of schooling to it.
<long pause>
South Africa's sports minister, Gayton McKenzie, repeatedly visited the Sandton offices of Blu Label throughout twenty twenty-five and again in April, the Daily Maverick reports. Blu Label was selling Ticketpro, which holds exclusive ticketing for cricket and football bodies his department oversees, and the buyer is a consortium linked to his own Patriotic Alliance. Under a coalition government that promised clean hands, the investigation now tests whether anyone will ask the minister to explain those visits.

On the economy. <short pause>
US Central Command says it's escorted one and a half billion barrels of Gulf crude through the Strait of Hormuz, with three thousand ships crossing in six months and more than ninety-five percent passing safely. Iran answers that the strait stays closed until Washington meets seven conditions, including lifting the blockade of its ports, and its foreign minister says Hormuz will never be managed as it was before the war. Traffic still averaged eleven percent below normal through the eighteenth of October... and mine strikes on tankers were reported as recently as the twenty-third.
<long pause>
SpaceX has borrowed forty billion dollars to buy chips for artificial intelligence, most of it callable debt. Elon Musk says lenders have shut out AI cloud companies with no revenue beyond data centres, in an echo of the late-nineties fibre boom, when telecom firms borrowed against capacity nobody yet needed. He predicts more than two hundred of these so-called neoclouds will shrink to a few survivors... and nobody yet knows whether chips that lose value so fast can carry that much debt.

In science. <short pause>
Uganda says it's Ebola-free... yet it's ordered twenty-one-day quarantines for citizens returning from the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The Bundibugyo strain was first identified in western Uganda in two thousand seven, and there's still no approved vaccine or treatment, while symptoms can take up to twenty-one days to appear. Swiss authorities now say a woman diagnosed in Nigeria caught it in Uganda in September, and Nigeria has responded by requiring health declarations from every international traveller... because when medicine has no answer, borders become the last line.

In technology. <short pause>
Beijing's Approaching AI has raised two point four billion yuan this year, including a one point three billion yuan round led by state-linked funds. Founded by Tsinghua University computing researchers, it sells artificial intelligence output by the token and says it runs trillion-parameter models by splitting the work across Chinese and foreign chips. It's part of China's token factory boom, a way of building around American export controls at the inference layer... though the efficiency claims, for now, are the company's own.
<long pause>
Lloyds drew two thousand two hundred and ninety-seven complaints about blocked cryptocurrency transfers, more than any other British bank. The industry advocacy group Stand With Crypto logged ten thousand and ninety-two complaints from all six hundred and fifty UK constituencies, as Barclays caps transfers at two thousand five hundred pounds a payment and Chase, Starling and TSB block them outright. Britain's banks, not its Parliament, are quietly deciding where their customers' money is allowed to go.
