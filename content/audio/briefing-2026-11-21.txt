This is your briefing for the twenty-first of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Sudan's power grid is close to collapse... the UN has counted twenty-nine drone strikes on electricity and fuel sites across eleven of the country's states, all between the twentieth of October and the fourth of November. The strikes are attributed to the Rapid Support Forces, and the outages have cut dialysis, surgery and clean water far from any front line. In a war already measured in famine and displacement, the darkness has now reached the hospital ward.

In politics. <short pause>
A sandbank gave way onto a row of tents in al-Mawasi this month... and four people were killed beneath it. Rain has now affected more than two hundred and twenty thousand people in Gaza and damaged over forty thousand shelters, the UN says, as displaced families face another winter under canvas and plastic sheeting. Israel agreed to let in eighteen thousand aid trucks a month, yet figures obtained by an Israeli newspaper show it never has, and the winter rains have only just begun.
<long pause>
Sixteen-year-old Oleh, from the occupied town of Dniprorudne in Zaporizhzhia, says Russian soldiers electrocuted him over leaflets. Ukraine's human rights commissioner, Dmytro Lubinets, says one point six million children now live under Russian occupation, where soldiers confiscate Ukrainian passports and keep pressing teenage boys to enlist in the army fighting their own country. Washington wants a peace deal by January twenty twenty-seven... and what becomes of these children is a question any settlement will have to answer.
<long pause>
Turning to the Gulf, Iran's Revolutionary Guards have claimed more than thirty mine strikes on ships in the Strait of Hormuz, and not one has been verified. Five naval experts told the Abu Dhabi paper The National that the claims don't add up, and no shipping company or maritime agency has confirmed a single hit. Even so, crossings remain eighty percent below prewar levels, according to Lloyd's List Intelligence... which suggests the most effective weapon in the strait may be the announcement itself.
<long pause>
Denmark is expelling Russia's defence attaché after its intelligence service said Moscow hired a criminal gang to kill Kyivstoner, a Ukrainian rapper. Danish officials call it the first known Russian assassination attempt on their soil; two men charged with attempted murder deny it, and investigators are probing links to the banned gang Loyal to Familia. Copenhagen is now weighing whether to suspend Schengen visas for Russians, a step that would carry the dispute far beyond one diplomat.
<long pause>
In a New York federal court, the Indian national Nikhil Gupta was sentenced to more than nineteen years for a murder-for-hire plot against Gurpatwant Singh Pannun, a Sikh separatist who holds American and Canadian citizenship. Prosecutors say an Indian intelligence officer, still at large, directed the plot, echoing Canada's accusation that Indian agents were tied to the killing of the Sikh activist Hardeep Singh Nijjar in twenty twenty-three. New Delhi denies any involvement, but the verdict now puts a court's judgement behind what rights groups call transnational repression.

On the economy. <short pause>
In Pakistan's Punjab province, a ten-kilogram bag of flour will now cost no more than twelve hundred and seventy-five rupees. The province will sell state wheat to licensed mills at four thousand five hundred rupees per forty kilograms, paid in advance, and every mill must upload its daily purchase, grinding and sales records to a government system. Flour jumped ten percent in a single week before the order, and whether a retail cap can hold against the price of grain is a test families will feel first, at the kitchen table.
<long pause>
A judge in St Louis has approved Bayer's seven point two five billion dollar settlement with people who say its weedkiller Roundup gave them cancer, with each award set between ten thousand and a hundred and sixty-five thousand dollars. The deal binds even those who fall ill in the future, capping for up to twenty-one years a liability Bayer took on when it bought Monsanto in twenty eighteen. Glyphosate stays on the shelves... and people who aren't yet sick have already lost their day before a jury.
<long pause>
President Trump has threatened a tremendous tax on American companies that build AI data centres abroad, though he hasn't yet told his own staff. The threat lands hardest on the Gulf states, Malaysia and India, all courting American tech giants that UBS expects to spend up to one point eight trillion dollars on AI next year. At home, Ohio voters backed twenty-one ballot measures against data centres this month, endangering a planned seventeen billion dollar Amazon investment, so it's unclear where Washington expects the buildings to go.
<long pause>
Japan's flagship sovereign AI project, Noetra, may cost eight hundred billion yen more than planned... and the government may have to carry a bigger share of the bill. Soaring chip prices drive the rise, and because those chips are bought in dollars, a yen at about one hundred and fifty-nine to the dollar makes every server Tokyo orders dearer still. Nobody has yet said who covers the difference, a question every middle power chasing its own AI may soon face.

In science. <short pause>
Bringing the planet back from overshooting its warming targets would mean pulling three hundred and thirty billion tonnes of carbon dioxide out of the air, researchers write in Nature, as Turkey hosts the COP thirty-one climate summit in Antalya. Today's removals run at about two billion tonnes a year, mostly from planting forests, a pace that would take more than a century and a half to clear the debt. Even in the UN Environment Programme's best case the world warms by one point eight degrees this century, so the real question in Antalya is who builds the removal machines, and who pays for them.
<long pause>
A claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis, a prime-number puzzle a hundred and sixty-seven years old that carries a million-dollar prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute, runs to two hundred and fifty thousand pages. Anthropic's Levent Alpöge says he built it with the AI model Claude, and its translation into the proof-checking language Lean runs to seventeen million lines. No human can read it all... so whether mathematics accepts a proof only a machine can check may matter as much as the primes themselves.
<long pause>
Off northeastern Jamaica, fossil reefs lie just metres from living corals, a record of the sea floor long before the modern decline. Caribbean reefs have shrunk sharply over the past seventy years, and two papers from the University of Texas at Austin now identify which species dominated the ancient ones. The researchers say that baseline could guide which corals to restore first, if enough living reef survives to be rescued.

In technology. <short pause>
Nvidia has lost two point seven four trillion dollars in market value since the sixth of October, and on Friday the Philadelphia semiconductor index fell almost eleven percent in a single session. Two people familiar with the matter say the US Securities and Exchange Commission is investigating Nvidia's accounting, following Jensen Huang's resignation as chief executive, and the index now sits forty-three percent below its June peak. From Taiwan's foundries to the data centres priced on Nvidia's promise, the question is how much of the AI boom rests on numbers now under review.
<long pause>
Tencent has released its seven hundred and seventy billion parameter model, Hy four, for anyone to download and modify, including for commercial use. Its mixture-of-experts design wakes just forty-nine billion parameters for each piece of text it produces, and it can hold close to a million tokens of context at once. Since DeepSeek's release in early twenty twenty-five, Chinese labs have made open weights their calling card... and each release narrows the gap American export controls were meant to keep wide.
<long pause>
Mira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab has released Inkling two Mini, an open model small enough to run on a laptop in twenty gigabytes of memory. The middle twelve of its twenty-four layers run three times for every token, so it can reason internally without writing its thinking out, and the lab itself warns that could make such models harder to monitor. Thinking Machines has also released a research version so outsiders can test that risk, which puts one of AI's hardest safety questions in the hands of anyone with a laptop.
