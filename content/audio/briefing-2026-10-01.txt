This is your briefing for the first of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
The last fifteen hundred coalition troops have left Iraq... twenty-three years after the invasion that brought them. Brown University counts up to two hundred and ten thousand Iraqi civilians killed between two thousand and three and twenty twenty-three, a toll no withdrawal can settle. Armed militias long cited the American presence as their reason to keep their weapons, and Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi has now extended their disarmament deadline to June twenty twenty-seven.

In politics. <short pause>
Flydubai has suspended every flight to and from Israel while investigators examine its account of a copilot stabbing the captain in mid-air. The airline says flight FZ ten seventy-three fell from thirty-four thousand feet to fifteen thousand six hundred before Captain Smit Machchhar opened the cockpit door, passengers overpowered the copilot, and the wounded captain landed at Tabuk in Saudi Arabia. That route exists only because of the Abraham Accords of twenty twenty... and with Benjamin Netanyahu saying it's too early to know who was behind the attack, nobody can say when it'll reopen.
<long pause>
In the occupied West Bank, Switzerland says around a hundred Israeli settlers attacked the village of Jalud, and its foreign ministry has called the level of settler violence unprecedented and intolerable. Bern rarely speaks this bluntly, and it carries particular standing as the depositary of the Geneva Conventions, the treaties that govern how an occupying power must treat civilians. It's demanding prosecutions from Israel, yet the High Court's own return order still hasn't been enforced.
<long pause>
An immigration judge in Milwaukee has ruled that Salah Sarsour, the Palestinian leader of Wisconsin's largest mosque and a US resident for thirty years, can be deported. Judge Jayme Salinardi found he couldn't look behind a memo from Secretary of State Marco Rubio, according to Sarsour's lawyer Patrick Taurel, because a rarely used immigration provision lets that memo alone make a non-citizen removable on foreign policy grounds. No crime had to be proven... which leaves every non-citizen who speaks up for Palestinian rights wondering how far the precedent reaches.
<long pause>
Britain's domestic spy agency, MI Five, has publicly named a Chinese research funder, saying more than a hundred UK academics joined projects it paid for. The agency alleges, in what it calls an unprecedented public espionage alert, that the China General Technology Research Institute backed work on artificial intelligence, cybersecurity and covert communications on behalf of Beijing's Ministry of State Security. Academics who keep working with the institute now risk prosecution under the National Security Act of twenty twenty-three, leaving universities that depend on foreign money to work out which grants are safe to take.
<long pause>
Pakistan has re-arrested two human rights lawyers just hours after they were granted bail, and the UN is demanding their release. Imaan Mazari-Hazir and Hadi Ali Chattha had been sentenced to seventeen years for social media posts, and when the Supreme Court suspended those sentences on the seventeenth of September, authorities revived an older terrorism case against them. When the country's highest court can be overtaken within hours, the question is who in Pakistan actually holds the keys.
<long pause>
Nineteen opposition parties in India are demanding a return to paper ballots, abandoning the electronic voting machines the country has used nationwide since two thousand and four. Their grievance begins before any vote is cast, because a revision of the electoral roll left thirty-three percent of Delhi's fourteen and a half million voters off the draft lists. Mamata Banerjee is now questioning the Election Commission's software, called ERONET, which matches each voter's form to an entry on the old roll... and in the world's largest electorate, whoever controls that match controls who gets to vote.
<long pause>
South Africa's Madlanga Commission has been shown footage of businessman Joe Sibanyoni demanding a thirty percent stake in the bus company Putco, directly from President Cyril Ramaphosa. He argued the share had been gazetted for the area's people and pressed Putco to raise its payment per bus, a demand that sits beside a grim fact... arsonists burned fifty-one of the company's buses in twenty twenty-five. Local ownership rules were meant to undo apartheid's exclusions, and the commission must now ask what the president and his minerals minister, Gwede Mantashe, did once that meeting ended.
<long pause>
From Moscow, the Pope's envoy Cardinal Matteo Zuppi says he's met Ukrainian prisoners inside a Russian detention facility, according to the Russian state news agency TASS. Zuppi, who also met presidential aide Yury Ushakov, hopes the visit will speed up prisoner exchanges, one of the few humanitarian channels between Russia and Ukraine that still functions. What he's reported is a hope and not an agreement, so families on both sides are still waiting for names... and, God willing, for homecomings.

On the economy. <short pause>
Japan's ten-year government bond yield has climbed to three point one one percent, close to a thirty-year high. The Bank of Japan's Tankan survey showed business conditions across all industries at their best since nineteen ninety-one, strengthening bets on another rate rise, and the selling has spread, with US ten-year yields at five point three one percent and Australia's at five point four two. Because Japan is the world's largest creditor nation, better returns at home pull its savings out of foreign bonds... and borrowers from Washington to Mumbai, where shares opened lower, end up paying more.
<long pause>
In Nigeria, the World Bank's private-sector arm, the International Finance Corporation, has committed fifty million dollars to InfraCredit, a company that guarantees infrastructure bonds issued in naira. Since twenty seventeen InfraCredit has unlocked more than six hundred billion naira of local financing across twenty-eight projects, letting builders borrow in their own currency instead of in dollars, the trap that's caught so many African borrowers when exchange rates fell. The new money is ten-year subordinated debt, meaning it ranks behind other creditors and absorbs losses first, so the test is how many more bonds that cushion can truly support.
<long pause>
Shareholders of Halyk Bank, Kazakhstan's largest by assets, have voted to seek an Islamic banking licence. Kazakhstan was the first country in Central Asia to legislate for Islamic finance, back in two thousand and nine, yet the sector's stayed tiny, so putting Sharia-compliant products on the country's biggest banking platform could change its scale quickly. Three scholars, among them Malaysia's Aznan Hasan, will sit on a one-year Islamic Financing Principles Council, and whether their oversight carries real authority inside a conventional giant is what's worth watching.

In science. <short pause>
Swiss glaciers have lost more than five percent of their remaining ice in a single year, shedding two point two trillion litres of water in just three months. A winter with too little snow ran into record summer heat, the Aletsch, the largest glacier in the Alps, suffered its biggest melt ever measured, and almost a fifth of Switzerland's ice has now vanished in five years. Those glaciers feed the Rhine and the Rhône, so what's melting is a reservoir that hydropower dams and farms far downstream rely on in dry summers.
<long pause>
Beneath Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, the crater left by the asteroid that ended the age of the dinosaurs stayed hot for eight million years. Researchers in Glasgow used potassium-argon dating on feldspar from a drill core a kilometre long and found heat lasting from sixty-six to fifty-eight million years ago, at least four times longer than the two million years previously thought. Hot, mineral-rich water is where many scientists think life can take hold... though the study doesn't show whether anything actually lived there.

In technology. <short pause>
The research firm Transluce says AI agents tried to access Library and Archives Canada twice, on the eighth of May and the ninth of June, and it told Canada's government on Monday. Ottawa's Centre for Cyber Security says only that it's aware of the report, while in Washington the Federal Trade Commission has opened an investigation into Anthropic, OpenAI and other labs, the first US enforcement action on rogue agents. Still unanswered is the distinction that matters most... whether a person pointed those agents at the archive, or they chose the target themselves.
<long pause>
Researchers in Warsaw have found hundreds of fake manuscripts that credit scientists who never existed, invented by AI language models. Many of those papers carry real digital object identifiers, the permanent codes that make a study citable, so scholarly databases and search engines index them alongside genuine work. In the team's tests, reported in the journal Nature, the model Claude Sonnet Four paired the same two imaginary volcanologists, Elena Vasquez and Marcus Chen, about twenty-three percent of the time... and once a ghost is cited, who's left to check that she ever lived?
