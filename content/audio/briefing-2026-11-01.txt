This is your briefing for the first of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
A fourth tanker's burning in the Strait of Hormuz... with more than two million barrels aboard, Iran's state news agency Mehr reports. The Revolutionary Guards' navy says the ship ignored radio warnings and took an unauthorised southern route. Brent crude stood near a hundred and eighteen dollars on the thirtieth of October, and this account so far rests on Iranian state media alone.

In politics. <short pause>
Syria's foreign minister says Damascus is weighing entry to the Makkah pact, the three-nation agreement that binds Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey to treat an attack on one as an attack on all. Asaad al-Shaibani counts more than a thousand Israeli attacks on his country in under two years, and the pact's wording echoes NATO's Article Five, the promise that has anchored Western security since nineteen forty-nine. Whether that guarantee would ever reach the Syrian front is the question membership would force into the open.
<long pause>
Ukraine and Russia are major wheat exporters, and each now attacks the other's ships in the Black Sea. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio says interest in a ceasefire is growing as Washington reviews Turkish and Indian proposals to protect grain ships, and in some cases energy too, while Turkey has done this before, helping broker the twenty twenty-two grain deal that Russia left a year later. For the countries that import that wheat, a proposal still isn't safe passage.
<long pause>
Nigeria is isolating two suspected Ebola patients in its northeast, while laboratories test samples and officials trace their contacts. Neither case is confirmed, but the country remembers twenty fourteen, when one infected traveller reached Lagos and health workers ended the outbreak within about three months. The Nigeria Centre for Disease Control gave no date for results... so the people on that contact list are left waiting.
<long pause>
London police arrested a hundred and thirty-nine people for holding placards backing Palestine Action, the group Britain has banned under its terrorism law. Organisers at Defend Our Juries count more than four thousand such arrests since July of last year, under legislation with roots in the conflict in Northern Ireland, where proscription was aimed at armed organisations. Support for a banned group carries up to fourteen years in prison... and within days, the Supreme Court's due to hear a challenge to the ban.
<long pause>
France's interior ministry counts more than five hundred arrests after a third straight weekend of unrest over living costs, fuel prices and poor public services. Largely peaceful rallies gave way to riots, with paving stones and petrol bombs thrown at police, and the country's seen fuel anger spread like this before, in the Yellow Vest movement of twenty eighteen. What's unresolved is whether a fourth weekend follows, and whether anyone in Paris has an answer to the grievance underneath.
<long pause>
Forty-three million Americans have already voted in the midterms, ten million more than in twenty twenty-two. California's governor, Gavin Newsom, has warned judges that federal agents may seize ballots before results are certified, pointing to the January seizure in Fulton County, Georgia, as the Trump administration claims twenty thousand California voters are likely noncitizens, a figure state officials firmly reject. A Democratic House could block the president's agenda and open investigations, so who holds the physical ballots may matter as much as who cast them.
<long pause>
Hungary has signed the first known American pact against antifascist groups, the German outlet netzpolitik reports, letting an EU member share information on the left with Washington. Foreign Minister Anita Orbán and Marco Rubio signed it on Thursday, targeting a German network called Antifa Ost, whose alleged members have been at the centre of a legal fight since attacks on far-right marchers in Budapest in twenty twenty-three. What isn't clear is how much about European citizens will now cross the Atlantic, and who in Europe gets to check it.
<long pause>
Women in southern Kazakhstan spend hours each day simply securing water, in labour that's unpaid and goes unnoticed. Central Asia still lives with the Soviet decision to pour its rivers into cotton fields, the choice that drained the Aral Sea, and in remote villages like Qyzylsay, distance also limits access to government services. Activist Nazgul Aidabossynova's project now trains these women to use online state services and to put a price on their labour... though knowing the cost of water won't by itself bring a pipe.

On the economy. <short pause>
Foreign borrowers have sold a record sixteen billion dollars of yen bonds, and Japanese investors' money is now financing the global rush to pay for artificial intelligence. Alphabet raised yen in May, followed by Hon Hai and Zurich Insurance, drawing on a market shaped by the decades of rock-bottom interest rates that followed Japan's asset bubble bursting around nineteen ninety. Much now rests on the Bank of Japan, because every move it makes on rates changes how cheap that money really is.
<long pause>
Karachi's twenty-five million people live in a city their own chief minister calls ruined, and he's blaming the planners, though his party has ruled Sindh for fifteen years. Murad Ali Shah says roads were declared commercial and shops now sit on stormwater drains at cheap rent, in what was Pakistan's first capital and remains its main port and commercial hub. Funds exist to rework a hundred and forty-three roads, Dawn reports, but residents will want to know who answers for the drains.

In science. <short pause>
Iron Age pots from Keeladi, in Tamil Nadu, have given South India its first chemical proof of dairy. Mass spectrometry and X-ray imaging found the fatty traces of cow's milk beside a plant molecule never before recovered from any archaeological context, adding to a site whose excavations have already challenged older accounts of how early urban life developed in the south. What those households were making with that molecule and that milk... is a question the pottery hasn't finished answering.
<long pause>
Researchers in Darmstadt, in Germany, have found a giant virus that infects algae and carries a gene for a working potassium channel. Tandem channels of this family steady the excitability of human nerve cells, yet no bacterial precursor has been found, and biology has a precedent for such a surprise, since a protein essential to the human placenta came from ancient viruses. If the evolutionary trail holds, part of what calms our own nerves may have begun inside a virus.

In technology. <short pause>
Alibaba's Qwen model is coming to Chinese iPhones, the tech outlet TMTPost reports, placing a Chinese-built system inside Apple's assistant. It'll handle text and images in Siri, writing tools and visual intelligence, and China's required generative services to register with regulators since twenty twenty-three, which helps explain why Apple's taken a local partner. Left open is who answers when the assistant on a Chinese iPhone says something the rules don't allow.
<long pause>
Binance says it has refilled ninety percent of the six point three billion dollars stolen from it, drawing on its treasury and its insurance fund. Bitcoin, ether and eight other tokens are fully backed, the exchange says, but proof of reserves became an industry promise only after FTX collapsed in twenty twenty-two, and customers must rely for now on a snapshot Binance published itself. Cryptographic proof is due on the third of November... and until then, the word of the exchange is all they've got.
