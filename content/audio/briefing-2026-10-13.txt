This is your briefing for the thirteenth of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Riyadh's main airport cancelled eighty-two percent of its flights on Monday... and its closure has now been extended. Houthi strikes have killed fourteen people since Saturday at King Khalid International, Saudi Arabia's second-busiest airport. Lufthansa won't fly to Riyadh before the sixteenth of October, Air France-KLM not before the end of the month, and nobody can yet say when a capital of millions gets its link to the world back.

In politics. <short pause>
Turkey's intelligence service has confirmed that fighters of the Kurdistan Workers' Party, the PKK, have left four bases in the Metina area of northern Iraq. It's the latest verified step in winding down a four-decade insurgency that cost some forty thousand lives, and under August's amnesty law no fighter goes home until Turkish intelligence certifies the disarmament. Kurds in Turkey are still waiting for the political half of that bargain, and displaced villagers are waiting to learn who holds the vacated ground.
<long pause>
A hundred and thirty-three million names have left India's voter rolls since last year, according to media reports, and on Monday thousands marched in New Delhi demanding that Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar resign. Officials say the revision clears out duplicate, dead and fraudulent entries, but it's roughly one name in seven on the largest electoral roll any democracy has ever kept. Who was removed, and whether they can get back on before they next need to vote, is what the marchers want answered.
<long pause>
North of Ramallah, in the occupied West Bank, Israeli settlers have blocked the seven hundred and fifty residents of Umm Safa from their only cemetery. Since August the village has had nowhere to bury its dead, after settlers seized the surrounding land and set up an outpost there, the village council head told the outlet Mondoweiss. Residents say it's one more step in a campaign to push them out... in a territory where the International Court of Justice has ruled Israel's settlements unlawful.
<long pause>
In the United States, Archbishop Timothy Broglio, who oversees pastoral care for Catholics in the armed forces, has told Catholic soldiers to seek exemption from a firing squad. It's for Hasan, the former army officer who killed thirteen people at Fort Hood in two thousand and nine, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth wants the execution livestreamed. America's military hasn't executed anyone since nineteen sixty-one, and death penalty experts say no government execution has ever been broadcast... so the death of a Muslim American would be the first the state puts on screen.
<long pause>
Venezuela's scheduled power cuts now last eight to ten hours, and residents of five states have taken to the streets at night in protests that Interim President Delcy Rodríguez has condemned. Major generating units have gone offline, widening the shortfall by as much as three thousand eight hundred megawatts, a grid expert says, in a country that sits on the world's largest proven oil reserves. Venezuelans remember the nationwide blackout of twenty nineteen, and they've been given no date for when the lights stay on.
<long pause>
South Korea has recalled its ambassador from Kyiv, a step it hasn't taken since two thousand and eight. Seoul says Ukraine broke a secrecy agreement over two captured North Korean soldiers, whose transfer President Volodymyr Zelenskyy announced at the UN General Assembly, while Kyiv denies any such agreement existed. South Korea's constitution counts every North Korean as its own citizen, which is why the fate of two prisoners can strain a relationship both capitals have reason to keep.

On the economy. <short pause>
The International Monetary Fund, meeting in Bangkok, has raised its forecast for world growth this year to three point one percent... while projecting that Kuwait's economy will shrink by sixteen point seven percent. Only one region, the Middle East and North Africa, is forecast to contract, by two percent, because the fund now assumes the Strait of Hormuz won't reopen until March twenty twenty-seven. Gulf states built on crude can't sell what can't leave, and the migrant workers their budgets pay will feel it first.
<long pause>
In that strait, Iran's Revolutionary Guards say a third tanker has been hit in two days, naming no ship, flag or weapon. Ships there now face Iranian fire and an American blockade enforced with live fire, and oil ministers meeting in Riyadh say shipping and insurance now eat more than a third of a cargo's value. The Gulf saw this in the tanker war of the nineteen eighties, and now as then it's crews, many from South and Southeast Asia, who carry the risk.
<long pause>
Japan's imports, priced in yen, cost twenty-four point five percent more than a year ago, and wholesale prices rose six point eight percent in the year to September. A weak yen, the US-Israeli war on Iran and demand from artificial intelligence have lifted chemicals by thirteen point three percent and non-ferrous metals by forty point two, in a country that imports nearly all its energy. For a nation that spent decades fighting falling prices, the question is now whether the Bank of Japan raises rates... on a government carrying the rich world's largest debt.
<long pause>
Thirty-eight point three percent of Pakistanis live in multidimensional poverty, the United Nations Development Programme reports, against sixteen point four percent in India. Its index doesn't measure income — it counts overlapping deprivations in nutrition, schooling, water and electricity, and worldwide it finds one point two billion people poor, the first rise in fifteen years. Two countries that started from one line in nineteen forty-seven have pulled apart on what a child eats and learns, though the surveys behind each figure come from different years.
<long pause>
Bangladeshis bound for jobs in Malaysia are paying up to four hundred thousand taka to get there, reports say... roughly ten times the agreed cost. Malaysia has approved only ten Bangladeshi recruitment agencies since twenty sixteen, and a Bangladeshi minister, Mir Shahe Alam, has now announced that Malaysia will recruit two hundred thousand Bangladeshis over six months. Migrants who borrow that much begin their working lives abroad in debt, and nothing announced so far says who'll police the price.

In science. <short pause>
Lassa fever has killed two hundred and sixty-eight people in Nigeria this year, and the death rate has climbed to twenty-three point six percent from eighteen point six a year ago. Nigeria's Centre for Disease Control blames late arrival at hospital and the cost of treatment, for a rat-borne virus first identified in the Nigerian town of Lassa in nineteen sixty-nine that still has no licensed vaccine. Abuja has pledged three billion dollars more for health by twenty thirty, the year the United States ends a quarter century of funding for HIV, tuberculosis and malaria programmes... and it's unclear the one will cover the other.

In technology. <short pause>
Twenty Chinese car brands are exhibiting at the Paris Motor Show, double the number two years ago. Chinese makers held ten point seven percent of Europe's car market in the second quarter, up from five point seven, Schmidt's analysts say, after answering the EU's twenty twenty-four tariffs with hybrids and with plants in Austria and Spain. TMTPost, a mainland Chinese technology outlet, reports that European carmakers are finding home advantage less reliable, and a tariff wall that was meant to buy them time may have taught their rivals to build inside it.
<long pause>
Ukrainian drones have hit three of the six Russian data centres run by Yandex, and payments, email, ride-hailing and food delivery apps failed across Russia. Russian bloggers point to a contrast: Ukraine moved its state data to foreign clouds, while Russia's sovereign internet law, passed in twenty nineteen to wall its network off, bars Yandex from keeping backups abroad. A law written to protect a country's internet from outsiders has left it concentrated in a handful of buildings... and those buildings can be reached.
