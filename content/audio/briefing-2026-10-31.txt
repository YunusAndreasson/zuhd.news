Four hours... that's how long Oleshky's civilians had on Friday to leave a town without power, heat or water. Hundreds got out under a pause brokered by America's First Lady, while Russia's rights commissioner said twenty-five buses stood ready and two people died clearing mines from the roads. The White House had counted up to two thousand residents there, so an unknown number remain on the Dnipro's occupied bank, and the window has closed.

In politics. <short pause>
Six thousand people have vanished in Gaza, according to the territory's missing-persons committee, and more than a year after the ceasefire their families still have no answers. The committee has logged twenty-one thousand five hundred reports, with most of those people confirmed dead and eight thousand of them under rubble. As Argentina's mothers of the disappeared showed decades ago, a family with no body and no detention record can neither bury anyone nor visit them, and it's Israel that's withholding what it knows.
<long pause>
Obituaries have filled Yemeni Facebook feeds since early September, when Houthi fighters launched an offensive and seized six districts. Families in government-held areas post names and photographs, while Houthi areas announce their dead mainly through official funerals, so even grief now follows the front line. Those lines had mostly held since the UN-brokered truce of twenty twenty-two, and no one outside can yet say how many that quiet has now cost.

A Pakistani father is in critical condition after setting himself on fire outside the courts in Muzaffargarh, a day after a nine-year rape trial ended in acquittal for lack of evidence. He says his daughter's husband divorced her over the alleged assault, which means the family carried the case's cost long before any verdict arrived. Pakistan's courts hold a backlog of more than two million cases, and his has become a question about what a decade of waiting does to those who wait.

Riot police ringed Poland's Constitutional Tribunal on Friday, and the court's president was suspended as the justice minister moved to lift his immunity. President Karol Nawrocki turned his plane back mid-flight to Senegal, because whoever holds this court rules on whether the other side's laws are legal. The fight dates to twenty fifteen, when a Law and Justice government installed judges in disputed circumstances, and neither camp now accepts the other's claim to the bench.
<long pause>
Federal agents have surged into Alaska days before Tuesday's vote, in a Senate race where an independent named Daniel J. Sullivan shares the Republican incumbent's name. The Justice Department has asked a judge to bar the state from certifying the result if the incumbent loses, although the focus of its probe remains unclear. American prosecutors have long avoided overt steps close to an election, which is why this one will be judged by more than its legal merits.

North Korea displayed a stealth drone mockup in Pyongyang on Thursday, standing among reconnaissance aircraft, armed drones and loitering munitions. A fellow at the Korea Institute for National Unification estimates its tailless wing spans up to sixty-six feet, resembling the X-47B, the American demonstrator that first landed on an aircraft carrier in twenty thirteen. There's a long distance between a mockup and a working aircraft, and nobody outside Pyongyang knows how much of it has been crossed.

On the economy. <short pause>
Two limpet mines disabled a tanker near the Omani port of Khasab on Thursday, and three maritime security sources say a small boat attached them before withdrawing. Iran's Revolutionary Guard reported another tanker striking a mine on Friday, even as US Central Command declared the strait's primary routes clear, and Brent crude touched one hundred and seventeen dollars for a fourth straight monthly gain. These same waters saw mines in the tanker war of the nineteen eighties, and fuel importers from Karachi to Dhaka are paying while the two accounts of the strait remain unreconciled.

Most of the three hundred and sixty workers at the paper mill in Témiscaming, Quebec, are now idle, and property values there fell almost overnight. The town grew up around that mill a century ago, and it ships about seventy-five percent of its output south, where American duties of fifty percent have applied since August. Canadian timber and pulp have fought Washington's trade remedies for decades, but this time a single decision could remove the only reason the town exists.

OPay raised three hundred and four million dollars in a New York listing, selling sixteen million shares at nineteen dollars each and reaching a market value above four billion. It's the first major Nigeria-focused fintech on an American exchange, and its rise owes much to the cash shortage of twenty twenty-three, when a currency redesign drove millions of people onto mobile money apps. The offering closes on the second of November, and investors in New York now hold a stake in how Nigerians move their naira.

In science. <short pause>
India's space agency launched the navigation satellite NVS-03 from Sriharikota with three pyro valves already open, a workaround for the failure that stranded its predecessor. A connector fault likely stopped the oxidiser valve firing on NVS-02 last year, and this launch restores NavIC to the four-satellite minimum it needs to operate. India's push for its own positioning system is usually traced to the Kargil war of nineteen ninety-nine, when America reportedly refused it GPS data, and four satellites is still only the bare minimum.
<long pause>
Rain stopped on the tenth of June in Jocotán, Guatemala, and the maize never ripened for Maya Ch'orti' families there, or for Lenca families across the border in Honduras. El Niño's drought, worsened by climate change, took more than a harvest, because without a crop there's no native seed saved from their ancestors for next year's planting. Those varieties were bred over many generations to suit this land, and health workers now report anxiety and hopelessness in communities that did almost nothing to warm the planet.
<long pause>
Leaders of fourteen governments met in Fiji for climate talks built around islands, seeking finance reform and ocean governance on the agenda of the UN summit. It was small island states who fought to get one point five degrees into the Paris Agreement in twenty fifteen, and a recent UN report now finds warming reaching at least one point eight even under the most optimistic scenario. Few leaders from outside the Pacific came, which leaves open whether anyone with the largest emissions was listening.

In technology. <short pause>
Kuaishou's video-generation business Kling has closed a funding round of nineteen point eight billion yuan, which the Chinese tech outlet Zhidx calls the largest in AI video. Rivals Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu are among the investors, while chief executive Gai Kun holds three percent of the equity but nearly twenty-four percent of the votes. Competitors funding one another's models is an unusual arrangement in any industry, and two investors who failed to pay in full suggest not everyone's conviction lasted to the closing.

Proton has cancelled what its staff knew as AI re-interviews, after six employees told 404 Media that they feared being fired for using AI too little. In August, chief executive Andy Yen rejected one employee's call to spend a few days penetration-testing new code before release. Scientists who met at CERN founded the Swiss company in twenty fourteen, in the wake of the Snowden revelations, so its customers will want to know whether speed has begun to outrank the security they're paying for.
<long pause>
Lebanon's data protection law remains unenforced even as officials start talking about rules for artificial intelligence. At the country's Internet Governance Forum earlier this month, officials promised digital reform, yet citizens still need intermediaries for simple procedures and new government platforms keep suffering breaches. A proposed tax on WhatsApp calls helped ignite Lebanon's uprising in twenty nineteen, a reminder that digital policy there has already shaken a government, and the state hasn't yet shown who protects its citizens' data.
