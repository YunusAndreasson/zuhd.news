This is your Jumu'ah briefing for the twenty-third of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Iran has named its targets... data centres, undersea cables and artificial intelligence sites across seven Gulf states that host American forces. The warning comes from Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, after reports that President Trump is weighing several days of strikes once the US midterms pass on the third of November. With Brent crude near a hundred dollars a barrel, foreign ministers from Pakistan, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Britain, France and Germany meet in Istanbul today to try to stop the next round.

In politics. <short pause>
Leaked documents show the United Arab Emirates paid a Swiss private intelligence firm, Alp Services, to profile eleven thousand three hundred of its own residents through their Facebook likes. Liking a page of an outlet critical of the government was enough to put a person's name, home and employer on the list, compiled for fifty-five thousand euros under the code name Arnica Two. Abu Dhabi has jailed critics in mass trials before, most notoriously ninety-four defendants in twenty thirteen, and no one has yet said what became of the people on this list.
<long pause>
Thousands of Venezuelans marched in five cities for free elections, the first nationwide opposition protest in nearly two years. It's been nine months since US forces seized Nicolás Maduro, yet no vote has been scheduled, and interim president Delcy Rodríguez governs with Washington's backing while talks on a new electoral council leave out opposition leader María Corina Machado. Outside Caracas, blackouts now run six to fifteen hours a day after a turbine failed at the Guri dam... in a country sitting on the world's largest proven oil reserves.
<long pause>
Ukrainian sources say nothing has changed on the front line, despite President Trump's claim that Elon Musk has restored Ukraine's Starlink access. Earlier this week, Ukrainian drones were losing their link above a hundred and thirty kilometres an hour, and now Musk says the military controls the limits, Secretary of State Marco Rubio points to a private contract, and Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth denies any Pentagon order. A nation's drones depend on settings that nobody in Washington or at SpaceX will admit to owning.
<long pause>
A magnitude seven earthquake struck Tibet's Nagqu region late Thursday, killing at least sixteen people, injuring thirty-two and bringing down three hundred houses, according to Chinese authorities. Power and telecoms are cut in Baqen, three hundred and ninety kilometres from Lhasa, and the quake was shallow, just ten kilometres deep by the US Geological Survey's measure, which concentrates the shaking near the surface. In January last year, the Dingri quake on the same plateau killed more than a hundred and twenty people, and early tolls from a region this remote, where Beijing controls access, often rise.
<long pause>
In Brazil, police detained São Paulo state deputy Marcelo Aguiar after he shot a journalist at a party, days before the country's tied presidential runoff. Back in twenty seventeen, as a federal congressman, he filed a bill to repeal the Disarmament Statute, keeping guns only from people convicted of intentional violent or heinous crimes. Gun ownership soared under Jair Bolsonaro's decrees... and attacks on reporters and students are rising in the campaign's final days.
<long pause>
UN Secretary-General António Guterres says Israeli settlers attack Palestinians in the occupied West Bank every day, with the aim of driving whole communities off their land. He added that settlers also target journalists and diplomats, while aid into Gaza remains severely restricted as winter approaches. Since the occupation began in nineteen sixty-seven, more than seven hundred thousand settlers have moved into the West Bank and East Jerusalem, and decades of UN condemnation haven't slowed them.
<long pause>
The European Parliament awarded its Sakharov Prize for freedom of thought to the International Criminal Court, as new US sanctions threaten to cut the court off from banking and email within a hundred and eighty days. Washington's already sanctioned thirteen judges and prosecutors over the twenty twenty-four arrest warrant for Israel's prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu. Built to try the gravest crimes when national courts won't, the court now has six months to learn whether a prize from Europe comes with any protection.

On the economy. <short pause>
A judge in Pretoria has voided twelve point six billion rand in contracts for antiretroviral drugs, the three-drug pill millions of South Africans take every day. The court found that two suppliers, Innovata and Viatris, lacked valid manufacturing licences when bidding closed. South Africa runs the world's largest HIV treatment programme, and any gap in supply could be life-threatening.
<long pause>
Mexico's state oil company, Pemex, has reported nearly a hundred spills a year for four years, and a February spill in the Gulf shut local fishing for thirty days and crashed the market for the catch. Its own tallies don't agree... seven hundred and ninety-eight spills reported to financial regulators, five thousand five hundred and seventeen in sustainability reports, two hundred and seventy-four counted by the environment agency, each over a different period. Fishing families who lose a season are left to ask which of those numbers anyone will answer for.
<long pause>
Qatar will import up to four million tonnes a year of American liquefied gas from Cheniere's plants in Louisiana and Texas, with deliveries starting in twenty thirty-one. One of the world's biggest gas exporters is buying from a rival because the Gulf war has battered its economy, which the International Monetary Fund now expects to shrink fifteen point six percent this year, nearly double its earlier forecast. Priced on the American Henry Hub benchmark, the deal ties Doha's energy security to Washington for years after any ceasefire.
<long pause>
Britain's energy system operators say the country will keep warm this winter only by outbidding its neighbours, with liquefied gas now costing triple its pre-crisis price. Since its largest storage site, Rough, was largely shut in twenty seventeen, the country's held some of Europe's smallest gas stores, so it imports close to sixty percent of its gas and competes with Asian buyers cargo by cargo. Households will pay the price of being a price-taker in a market set by a war they can't influence.
---
In science. <short pause>
A NASA adviser says SpaceX may need twenty-two refuelling launches to send one Starship lander to the Moon for Artemis Four, as the agency still works toward a crewed landing in twenty twenty-eight. Each mission needs a depot ship in orbit, filled by tanker after tanker with liquid oxygen and methane, something nobody's ever done, while the National Academies this week ranked Bhabha Crater, on the Moon's far side, as the top site to explore. Bhabha lies within the South Pole-Aitken Basin, which may hold the oldest impact scar in the solar system... though reaching it depends on a launch count one rebuttal puts at just six to eight.
---
In technology. <short pause>
Attempts to exploit newly disclosed flaws in PHP, the language behind much of the web, jumped from thousands to millions within hours. Banks, airlines, railways and tax agencies from Tokyo to Helsinki suspended services as a precaution, and Aikido Security says the bugs sat unnoticed for years until AI found them, then went public before fixes were ready. PHP runs roughly three quarters of websites whose server language is known, and much of that long tail will never be patched.
<long pause>
Toronto Crime Stoppers has halted its tip service after a breach exposed one and a half million tips. About half contained personal information, and reporters were able to reach more than a dozen tipsters who'd been promised anonymity. The data sat with an American vendor, Navigate360, through its subsidiary P3 Global Intel... a reminder that a promise of secrecy is only as strong as the contractor holding the files.
<long pause>
India's eMudhra is leaving the business of issuing the certificates that put the padlock in a browser's address bar, a line it says earns about one percent of its revenue. The Bengaluru company will replace affected customers' certificates free through partners, and its root certificates keep serving customers while it works with browser makers on the transition. Fewer authorities in the Global South means the trust behind every secure website rests with an ever smaller circle of firms.
