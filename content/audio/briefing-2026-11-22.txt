This is your briefing for the twenty-second of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Two tankers were struck on consecutive days off Oman's Khasab, at the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz... and Iran's navy says one of them hit a naval mine. A mine is a different threat from a missile, because it stays in the water after any ceasefire and keeps insurers away, while traffic has already fallen to about two ships a day. Bahrain, meanwhile, says an Iranian strike set one of its industrial sites ablaze.

In politics. <short pause>
Gianni De Gennaro, Italy's former national police chief and later its intelligence coordinator, has died at seventy-eight, and it's believed he took his own life. Prosecutors had named him in a probe into obstruction of the inquiry into Paolo Borsellino, the anti-mafia magistrate killed by a car bomb in Palermo in nineteen ninety-two, and they allege that evidence was concealed, including the judge's diary, which vanished from the scene. De Gennaro denied any wrongdoing, a judge refused to place him under house arrest, and thirty-four years on, his death may close one line of inquiry into who hid the truth.
<long pause>
Denmark has asked its European Union partners to stop issuing tourist visas to Russians. Danish security officials say Russia's intelligence services planned to kill a Ukrainian rapper known as Kyivstoner in September, and Copenhagen also wants Schengen states to stop recognising Russian passports that aren't biometric. Because one Schengen visa opens twenty-nine countries, a single state's security case could become a rule for the whole bloc... and the cost would fall on ordinary Russian travellers as much as on any agent.
<long pause>
In Seattle, masked immigration agents arrested two people on a boarding jet bridge, giving Alaska Airlines no warning, and the flight left nearly three hours late. Immigration and Customs Enforcement says it was seeking Ricardo Larrondo Mendoza for overstaying a visa and will bring charges against a second traveller, Sofia Hernandez Mejia. Washington's governor and a union have condemned the arrests, but enforcement has now moved past the security line onto the aircraft door itself, and there's little a state can do to stop federal agents in its own airport.
<long pause>
Residents of Borgu in Nigeria's Niger State say gunmen killed eleven people, and that reprisals then killed about twenty more. Witnesses say Kambari militias killed Fulani residents and refugees on sight, in a town that hosts two military bases, after mobile police had withdrawn from nearby Wawa. It's a pattern seen across Nigeria's middle belt, where one attack is answered by collective punishment of an entire community, and the question of why soldiers so close didn't intervene remains unanswered.
<long pause>
The UN refugee agency estimates that two point one million people have been displaced by Cameroon's Anglophone war, and in Buea, displaced children now sell bleach and plantains to keep their families fed. Fighting between government forces and separatists began in twenty sixteen, after teachers and lawyers protested the imposition of French in English-speaking courts and schools... a grievance rooted in the nineteen sixty-one union of a British-run territory with a French-run one. Nearly a decade later, a generation is growing up outside the classroom.
<long pause>
At the COP thirty-one climate talks in Antalya, the draft deal no longer mentions fossil fuels at all. Negotiations have run twenty-four hours past their deadline, with Saudi Arabia leading opposition to a phase-out while about a hundred and thirty countries want the transition acknowledged. Just two years after Dubai produced the first pledge to move away from fossil fuels, delegates heading home could leave the summit without the quorum it needs to agree on anything.

On the economy. <short pause>
Japan's Finance Ministry is offering between fifty and sixty trillion yen for the country's next five-year defence plan. That's well short of the eighty trillion that conservatives in Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's own party and military planners want, while Washington is pressing for three and a half percent of economic output. A weak yen makes imported American weapons and dollar-priced fuel dearer, so the currency has quietly become a constraint on the biggest military build-up since Japan abandoned its long-held cap of one percent of output in twenty twenty-two.
<long pause>
Azerbaijan's president, Ilham Aliyev, has opened a three-day visit to Hanoi, where the two countries pledged closer cooperation on oil and gas. Vietnam's prime minister called bilateral trade, which passed a hundred and fifty million dollars last year, relatively low, and both governments committed to rail freight links across Central Asia through the Trans-Caspian corridor. With the Strait of Hormuz all but shut, Caspian producers and Asian buyers are hunting for routes that avoid the Gulf, though rail and ferries across the Caspian can't yet move oil at anything like tanker scale.
<long pause>
The European Commission has proposed a levy of three percent on the European revenue of the largest technology companies, which it estimates would raise about five billion euros a year. European Council president António Costa has cut the twenty twenty-eight to twenty thirty-four budget from one point seven six trillion euros to one point five eight trillion, still a quarter above current spending in real terms. France's own digital tax drew American tariff threats in twenty nineteen, and this one needs the backing of all twenty-seven member states... so a single capital can block it.

In science. <short pause>
For the first time since twenty fifteen, Mercury has a spacecraft in orbit. BepiColombo, a joint European and Japanese mission, launched in October twenty eighteen and spent seven years braking toward the Sun, because falling inward picks up speed and risks overshooting the target. Its two probes will study Mercury's magnetic field and how it meets the solar wind, and the Japanese probe, Mio, still has to settle into its own orbit, God willing, before the science begins in earnest.
<long pause>
A study of three thousand, three hundred and forty-four people in Japan, South Korea, the United States and Britain finds that the strength of social norms often varies more within cultural regions than between them. Since a landmark study of thirty-three nations in twenty eleven, psychologists have tended to rank whole societies as tight or loose, and the authors say that theory may have overstated how much cultures differ. How strongly a rule binds seems to depend on whose expectation it is and the setting, which means a single score for any culture hides more than it shows.
<long pause>
Swedish researchers have examined every patient in the country diagnosed with colorectal cancer between twenty oh eight and twenty twenty-one, of whom eighteen thousand, nine hundred and forty-three already had cancer spread to distant organs at diagnosis. Linking those records to prescriptions, the study asks whether people taking low-dose aspirin were less likely to arrive with the disease already spread. Aspirin's link to lower colorectal cancer risk has been seen for decades, but a register can show an association... it can't prove that the pill is the cause.

In technology. <short pause>
Chinese carmaker Xpeng posted record quarterly revenue of twenty-four point two eight billion yuan, and still lost money. Its net loss more than doubled, from three hundred and eighty million yuan to eight hundred and seventy million, and while the margin on its vehicles rose to thirteen point one percent, the group margin fell once payments from its partnership with Volkswagen dried up. China's electric vehicle price war has squeezed nearly every maker since twenty twenty-three, and Xpeng is now betting that overseas sales, robotaxis and humanoid robots can deliver the profit that record volume hasn't.
<long pause>
South Korea's education minister, Choi Kyo-jin, has resigned over a cheating scandal that began when the top scorer in a national tax accountant exam confessed to using a miniature camera and a smartphone. Fears then spread to the Suneung, the college entrance exam on which the country halts flights during the listening test, and officials there caught a hundred and eleven violations involving devices. It's the first time a cabinet minister has fallen over technology-assisted cheating, and exam security in a system built on one high-stakes test now has to be rebuilt.
<long pause>
Members of India's opposition Youth Congress stormed Meta's office in Hyderabad, protesting over two opposition Instagram accounts that the company had withheld. Meta's own disclosures show five point four million restrictions in India in the first half of this year, compared with seventy thousand in all of twenty twenty-five, after the government cut the deadline for takedowns from thirty-six hours to just three in February. Three hours leaves little room for anyone to weigh a request... and the question of who decides what Indians can see online is now a political fight.
