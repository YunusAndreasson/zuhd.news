In Aden, families who once fled Houthi rule are packing to flee again... as Houthi troops mass north of Kirish and in al-Bayda. The city has housed Yemen's Saudi-backed government since twenty fifteen, and more than three hundred and fifty thousand people have been displaced since August alone. If the northern and eastern roads are cut, the south's last refuge, and its port beside the Bab el-Mandeb, could be sealed off.

In politics. <short pause>
Landmines and unexploded ordnance killed or wounded six thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine people last year, a five-year high, and eighty-four percent of them were civilians. In Myanmar, where both the military government and armed groups lay mines, five people are hit every day, and almost two-thirds of townships are contaminated. Meanwhile Poland and the Baltic states are leaving the nineteen ninety-seven Ottawa Treaty, so the ban that once shamed mine users is losing members just as the toll climbs.
<long pause>
Russia's refineries have lost about forty-five percent of their output to Ukrainian drones, industry sources estimate, and several Russian regions now ration petrol and diesel at the pump. Vladimir Putin told oil executives that retaliation is inevitable, which effectively ends the energy truce Washington brokered in mid-October. With winter arriving, millions of civilians on both sides heat their homes on exactly the infrastructure each army is now targeting.
<long pause>
Across Central America's Dry Corridor, seventy thousand of seventy-three thousand farming households surveyed by Oxfam have reported crop losses. El Niño brought a dry spell of up to a hundred days, and food security is now threatened in a hundred and three Honduran municipalities and thirteen Guatemalan departments. It's the same squeeze of failed harvests and no safety net that has pushed families north for a decade... and the region's governments haven't yet said how they'll respond.
<long pause>
Five crew members died when a Turkish Air Force helicopter crashed shortly after taking off from Eskişehir on a training flight. It's the second fatal military helicopter crash this month, after an army Sikorsky went down on the twelfth of November. Turkey fields NATO's second-largest army, yet its defence ministry hasn't explained either crash, and it's unclear whether any inquiry will be made public.
<long pause>
Armed men are holding about twenty-five people inside a supermarket in Cartagena, after a failed attempt to seize an armoured cash vehicle. Camera operators alerted police during the robbery, and the attackers fled into the store with shoppers still inside. The standoff comes just hours after a car bomb in Villavicencio injured twenty-two people, a test for President Abelardo de la Espriella, who campaigned on a hardline security policy.

On the economy. <short pause>
Iran has blacklisted twenty-six tugboats, some registered in the United Arab Emirates, India and Singapore, through its new Persian Gulf Strait Authority. Tugs tow and steer the giant tankers in and out of port, so Tehran is targeting the support fleet that oil traffic can't run without. Ship crossings through the Strait of Hormuz are already down by about forty percent, and this squeeze tightens it further without firing a single shot.
<long pause>
Iraq is fighting on two oil fronts at once. Baghdad rejects an OPEC plus estimate that puts its capacity at four point nine million barrels a day, a figure that will set its twenty twenty-seven quota and therefore its revenue. On the same day, the prime minister ordered a federal takeover of oilfields and border crossings in four Kurdish-held provinces, citing smuggling, and the Kurdish regional government has rejected the order.
<long pause>
India's food inflation hit eight point nine four percent in October, and over nine percent in villages. The monsoon ran thirteen percent below normal, pushing rice, pulses and edible oil up by as much as thirteen percent in a year, and the analytics firm Crisil calculates the poorest rural households now face steeper inflation than the richest. That leaves the Reserve Bank of India weighing relief for borrowers against prices that hurt the poor most.
<long pause>
Australia's annual inflation reached four point nine percent, even after a hundred and twenty-five basis points of rate hikes from the Reserve Bank of Australia. The trimmed mean core gauge rose to three point eight percent, while unemployment already sits at a five-year high of four point nine percent. So the central bank faces a hard choice: squeeze mortgage holders on variable rates even harder, or let prices run in a slowing economy.
<long pause>
Pakistan's cabinet has ended sugar export subsidies and price controls, freeing cane prices, planting zones and new mills from state control. The country had been in breach of an International Monetary Fund benchmark since June, and sugar has long been one of its most politically connected industries. Whether open markets lower prices for households, or simply hand more power to the mill owners, is the question the next harvest will answer.
<long pause>
Off South Africa's Cape Point, scientists from the conservation group WILDTRUST simulated an oil well blowout two hundred times... and oil reached shore in three out of four runs. For blowouts starting between April and September, the figure rose to ninety-eight percent, as TotalEnergies pursues drilling sixty kilometres off the coast. Small-scale fishers on the West Coast can't simply move to clean water, and they'd bear the cost first.

In science. <short pause>
A computer proof checker called Lean has verified an AI-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis. Posed by Bernhard Riemann in eighteen fifty-nine, the conjecture governs how prime numbers are distributed, which underpins number theory and modern cryptography. Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge guided Claude from a simplified version of his ideas to the full proof, and the world's mathematicians will now test whether a machine-verified result is truly the end of a hundred and sixty-seven-year search.

In technology. <short pause>
BYD is offering steer-by-wire on its new Tang, a five-seat electric SUV that starts at about twenty-eight thousand dollars. The system removes the mechanical link between steering wheel and wheels, a technology that until now shipped mainly on luxury cars and Tesla's Cybertruck. Full features come only in a three thousand dollar four-wheel-drive package, and regulators abroad have yet to decide how they'll certify it.
<long pause>
Estonia's culture minister has called social media age limits ineffective, breaking with the European Commission's plan. The Commission's September draft would require age checks across online services and set fifteen as the minimum for accounts, with parental consent for younger teens. That objection carries weight from Estonia, a pioneer in digital identity, and it raises the question of whether verifying every user's age protects children or simply catalogues them.
<long pause>
Hamburg's transit operator, Hochbahn, will extend behaviour-scanning cameras to a third station. Its software watches footage continuously and alerts the control room to five kinds of incident, including a person on the tracks. Germany's history has made its courts and citizens wary of mass surveillance... and yet almost no reliable figures exist on whether this software actually works.
