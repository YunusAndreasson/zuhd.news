This is your briefing for the eleventh of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
On the second day of US-mediated peace talks between Russia and Ukraine in Abu Dhabi, drones struck Ust-Luga... Russia's main Baltic port for oil and fuel exports. The regional governor told the state news agency TASS that air defences downed a hundred and fifty drones, that three workers were wounded, and that a substation went dark. Washington wants these talks to produce a new security framework for Europe, and European capitals fear it'll be settled without them in the room.

In politics. <short pause>
The US House of Representatives has shelved the impeachment of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over the Iran war, by two hundred and eleven votes to two hundred and four, with eleven Democrats, Nancy Pelosi among them, absent from a procedural vote their presence could have swung. Only two cabinet secretaries have ever been impeached by the House, William Belknap in eighteen seventy-six and Alejandro Mayorkas in twenty twenty-four, and the Democrats who flipped at least forty seats last week can try again when they take control in January. Republicans have already picked their leader for that fight... Jim Jordan, a founder of the hardline Freedom Caucus, who beat Steve Scalise by a hundred and six votes to seventy-three.
<long pause>
Prosecutors in New York have charged three men they describe as Iran-backed operatives over nearly twenty attacks and attempts, from synagogues and Jewish schools to the Royal Air Force base at Fairford, used in the US war on Iran. Separately, British counterterrorism police have arrested a forty-one-year-old American soldier on suspicion of spying for Russia, using the espionage sections of the National Security Act. Fairford has hosted American bombers since the Cold War, and these cases ask what a host country pays when someone else's war runs through its airfields.
<long pause>
Iran says it's set Washington seven conditions and still hasn't had a clear answer... and the Strait of Hormuz stays closed until they're met. Mohsen Rezaei, Iran's top security official, told the Lebanese broadcaster Al-Mayadeen that no talks are under way, even though Tehran never closed the strait outright during the Tanker War of the nineteen-eighties. Each day it stays shut, the cost lands on importers from Karachi to Manila.
<long pause>
Manuel Espina, a former Guatemalan ambassador tied to a corruption case, opened Washington's doors to Nayib Bukele through prayer breakfasts, a regional investigation published by Honduras's Contracorriente has found. The model is the National Prayer Breakfast, held in Washington since nineteen fifty-three, and at El Salvador's first, in January, guests prayed for a president accused of allowing the torture of two hundred and fifty-two Venezuelan migrants. That same network reaches the promoters of Próspera, the private city on the island of Roatán that's suing the Honduran state for billions.
<long pause>
Before South Africa's Madlanga Commission, accused crime figure Vusimuzi Matlala confirmed paying five hundred and fifty thousand rand to a police general... and then moved to strike the investigator's notes on those payments from the record. Like the Zondo inquiry into state capture before it, the commission is tracing how money bends public institutions, and a two-hundred-and-twenty-eight-million-rand police health tender has already led to charges against twelve senior officers. His lawyer argues a March prison interview was confidential, and if the commission agrees, what it can say about General Shadrack Sibiya may shrink.
<long pause>
Kyrgyzstan's president, Sadyr Japarov, has filed to run in the January twenty twenty-seven election, while his five rivals have gathered fewer than forty-one thousand signatures between them. Each candidate needs thirty thousand, plus a one-million-som fee that not even the winner gets back. Japarov rose to power in the unrest after a disputed vote in twenty twenty... and this time, voters may find little left to choose from.
<long pause>
Two pile-ups in dense fog on Bulgaria's Trakia motorway near Yambol have killed eleven people and injured dozens. Visibility fell from about seventy metres to ten within minutes, the interior minister said, on the main freight corridor between the EU and Turkey. Bulgaria's roads were already the bloc's deadliest last year, at seventy-one deaths per million people, and the question now is what warning drivers had before they drove into the fog.

On the economy. <short pause>
Two-year US Treasury yields jumped eighteen basis points overnight, as markets began pricing a half-point Federal Reserve hike in December rather than a quarter. Core prices rose three tenths of a percent in October — an annual pace above four percent, double the Fed's target — the very reading Governor Christopher Waller said would make him weigh the bigger move. When the Fed tightened hard in the early nineteen-eighties, dollar debt broke much of Latin America... and borrowers across the developing world will be watching December closely.
<long pause>
Siam Cement Group's core plastics plant in Vietnam will stay shut until the middle of twenty twenty-seven, because the closure of Hormuz cut off its naphtha, the oil-derived feedstock for plastics. Southeast Asia's petrochemical industry was built on Gulf supply, and the Thai conglomerate now plans to halve its Middle Eastern sourcing and make plastic from American ethane. Whether feedstock shipped across the Pacific can compete on price is something the whole region is now finding out.
<long pause>
Oman's economy grew five percent in the second quarter, while its Gulf neighbours' trade was choked by the war. Its coastline lies outside the Strait of Hormuz, so its oil keeps flowing and its ports are taking the shipping diverted from the strait. Economists caution that growth built on a neighbour's crisis may not survive the crisis ending.
<long pause>
Nigeria flared six point six billion cubic metres of gas last year, a third straight annual rise, World Bank tracking shows. That's fuel burned off at the wellhead, a practice that's lit the skies of the Niger Delta since oil was first pumped at Oloibiri in nineteen fifty-six, in a country where millions still lack steady power. The state oil company and Oando promise to end routine flaring by twenty thirty... yet only fifteen of sixty-two companies in Nigeria's audit submitted what was asked of them.
<long pause>
Pakistan has sealed the dome on Chashma-five, a twelve-hundred-megawatt reactor and its seventh nuclear plant built with Chinese help. Nuclear already supplies sixteen point six percent of the country's electricity from just eight point six percent of its capacity, the atomic energy commission says, a programme that began with a single Canadian-built reactor in Karachi in nineteen seventy-two. With civil works finished, installation now begins, and for a country that pays dearly for imported fuel, its first power, God willing, can't come soon enough.

In science. <short pause>
Germany's six-month cut to value-added tax in twenty twenty, from nineteen percent to sixteen, lifted private spending most among lower-wealth households, a study in the Review of Economic Studies finds. Combining surveys, retail purchase records and an economic model, researchers found younger and middle-aged households responded too, whatever their financial literacy. Britain tried the same lever after the two thousand eight crash, and with war-driven inflation back, governments now have harder evidence of whom it reaches.

In technology. <short pause>
Gabon's regulator restored social media on the twenty-fourth of October, after two hundred and forty-eight days in which one point eight four million people were cut off. Access came back under stricter rules... anyone who creates or shares illegal content now shares liability, and an October law regulates AI-generated material. Most shutdowns end when the crisis passes, but this one ended with new terms for online speech, and other governments may be taking notes.
<long pause>
Anthropic has launched the roadshow for a stock market listing that could raise up to sixty-four billion dollars, more than twice Saudi Aramco's record listing in twenty nineteen. Alongside it, seven suppliers of its chips and cloud capacity, led by Google and Nvidia with ten billion dollars each, are buying twenty-six billion dollars of shares privately, the Chinese tech outlet Zhidx reports. The company's filing shows about eight hundred and ninety-five billion dollars in future computing commitments... so the firms selling it that power are also helping pay for it, with pricing set for the eighteenth of November.
