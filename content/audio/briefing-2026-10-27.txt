Saudi Arabia's defence minister sat down with Iran's president in Tehran on Monday... even as Houthi missiles kept striking Saudi airports and oil sites. In politics. <short pause>
Pakistan's government has suspended the elected government of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, a province of some forty million people, and witnesses say police fire killed a thirteen-year-old boy, Habeel Khan, in the town of Bara. It was the last provincial government held by Imran Khan's party, and since emergency rule began on the eleventh of October, party workers have been arrested and communications cut. The military's long confrontation with Khan was once fought through courts and ballots... now it's being fought by dissolving an elected government outright.

Jordan's military has struck inside southern Syria, and Damascus says a single strike near Deraa killed five members of one family. Amman says it hit groups linked to the shooting at a church in Karak on the twenty-third of October, but Syria wasn't consulted, and it's closed Nassib, Jordan's only commercial border crossing. For a Syrian state still rebuilding after more than a decade of war, the question is whether its neighbours will now treat its south as open ground.

UN investigators have found that Israel committed the crime against humanity of extermination in the occupied West Bank. It follows the commission's finding of genocide in Gaza in September twenty twenty-five, and UN figures count one thousand one hundred and forty-three Palestinians killed in the West Bank since October twenty twenty-three... at least two hundred and fifty of them children. Israelis vote today, with polls putting Gadi Eisenkot eight to ten points ahead of Benjamin Netanyahu, though Netanyahu could lose and still stay on as caretaker prime minister.

Amnesty International says fewer than seventy thousand people remain in El Fasher, and seventeen of the nineteen residents it interviewed said Rapid Support Forces fighters had detained them, often for months. The paramilitary grew out of the Janjaweed militias behind the Darfur killings of the early two thousands, and El Fasher was the army's last stronghold in the region. Britain has now frozen the assets of fifteen commanders from both the Rapid Support Forces and the army... whether sanctions ever reach the men giving orders on the ground is still the open question.

Cyclone Arnab is due to cross India's east coast near Yanam, in Andhra Pradesh, around noon on Wednesday, with forecast winds of one hundred and fifteen to one hundred and twenty-five kilometres an hour. Next door, Odisha has readied one hundred and thirty-five cyclone shelters and sixteen hundred temporary centres, a system it built after the super cyclone of nineteen ninety-nine killed around ten thousand people in the state. Extremely heavy rain is forecast for the Gajapati and Ganjam districts, and whether that hard-won model holds once again depends, God willing, on evacuations finishing before the storm arrives.

Flávio Bolsonaro is refusing to concede Brazil's presidential election, which he lost by four point four million votes, claiming a Supreme Court order that extended voting by two hours invalidated Lula's win. His father also refused to accept defeat in twenty twenty-two, and a week after Lula took office, a mob stormed the government buildings in Brasília... a path that ended in Jair Bolsonaro's conviction for plotting a coup. Five right-leaning South American governments have congratulated Lula, but the United States and Argentina have held back, and that silence gives the son's challenge an audience abroad.

Uzbekistan has summoned Iran's ambassador and delivered a formal protest over posts by Iranian embassies about the heritage of Samarkand. The posts answered a speech about ancient Persia that US Secretary of State Marco Rubio gave at the Acropolis, and Samarkand, once the capital of Timur's empire, has been claimed as a cultural inheritance by Persians, Turks and Uzbeks alike. Tashkent says it values its ties with Tehran, but a rare public rebuke shows how hard it's become for Central Asia to stay out of the information war around Iran.

On the economy. <short pause>
Indonesia cut pipeline gas to Singapore by about eight percent on Saturday, keeping more at home as the energy crisis bites. Singapore makes up to ninety-five percent of its electricity from gas, and though this cut is under two percent of its daily demand, the city-state has spent decades managing exactly this kind of exposure, from the water it once relied on Malaysia for to the fuel it now buys from its neighbours. Jakarta says it's still meeting the contract minimum, and Singapore's foreign minister is there today... at a moment when every exporter in a Hormuz-driven shortage is weighing home demand against its contracts.
<long pause>
HSBC's pretax profit jumped thirty-two percent to nine point six billion dollars in the July to September quarter, and the bank's launching a buyback of up to a billion dollars. Higher interest rates did most of the work: central banks raised them to fight the oil shock, and the wider margin the bank now earns on loans is the extra cost carried by mortgage holders and businesses from Hong Kong to Britain. Charges for bad loans fell seventeen percent too, so the question is how long borrowers can keep paying before those losses start to climb again.

In science. <short pause>
Researchers tracking South Australia's Coorong lagoon across sixteen years found that freshwater inflows, more than anything else, shaped the microscopic algae living there. The lagoon sits at the end of the Murray-Darling, a river system heavily drawn on for irrigation upstream, so how much fresh water reaches it is a political choice as much as a natural one. More than eighty of its phytoplankton species can produce toxins, and since pipis, mussels and small fish all feed on them, every water allocation ripples through the whole food web.

In technology. <short pause>
Chinese carmaker Leapmotor's B ten now rolls off the same assembly line in Zaragoza, Spain, as the Opel Corsa built by its partner Stellantis. Building inside Europe gets around the EU's countervailing tariffs on Chinese-made electric cars... the same move Japanese carmakers made in Britain and the United States in the nineteen eighties to escape import limits. A battery plant thirty kilometres away assembles packs from cells that are still imported, so the open question is how much of the car Brussels will eventually count as European.

OpenAI has priced its new model, GPT six point one Terra, at one dollar per million input tokens, and says it matches its flagship on computer-use tasks at a tenth of the per-token price. That puts work like reading invoices and keying data into accounting software within cheap reach of automation, though the benchmarks are OpenAI's own. In the same week, Microsoft, which forecasts one hundred and seventy-five billion dollars in capital spending this year, offered buyouts to sixty thousand employees... more than a quarter of its workforce.

Proton, the Swiss company built on encrypted email, has told staff that interviews about artificial intelligence could change their roles or end their employment, according to a leaked internal email. Chief executive Andy Yen first interviewed more than forty senior leaders, and now interviewers outside each employee's reporting line will question everyone else. The company made its name as the privacy-first alternative to big tech, and it's now adopting the same AI-driven restructuring sweeping the industry it set out to challenge.
