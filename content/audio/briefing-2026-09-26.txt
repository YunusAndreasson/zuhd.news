This is your briefing for the twenty-sixth of September, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
In Ceuta, eight thousand three hundred migrants are packed into shelters... and four thousand more are sleeping outside. Morocco says it'll take them back, but Spain must first identify each person and hear every asylum claim — a slow process that leaves people stranded in a tiny Spanish enclave on the African coast. Thousands marched in Madrid on Saturday demanding an early election, and the people stuck at the border have become the ground Spain's political fight is waged over.

In politics. <short pause>
A military plane crashed onto a residential plot in Kenge, the capital of Congo's Kwango province, killing thirteen or fourteen people. Two lieutenant generals died with it, including the army's auditor general, and the deputy governor says the aircraft developed a technical fault over a town with no landing strip. Congolese carriers have sat on the European Union's air safety blacklist for years... and this crash will test whether the military holds itself to any higher standard.
<long pause>
Two of India's election commissioners reportedly objected on the record fourteen times to the revision of voter rolls now under way. In Karnataka alone, the commission's own data placed ten point eight million voters in categories marked absent, shifted, dead or duplicate — and the Congress party says real voters are being deleted, demanding the chief commissioner's arrest. With the world's largest electorate at stake, the fight is over who carries the burden of proof: the state, or the citizen who has to show they still exist.
<long pause>
About one thousand six hundred and forty Nigerians have now been flown home from South Africa since xenophobic attacks on Black foreigners began in April. One hundred and five more landed in Lagos this week, and a church, the Redeemed Christian Church of God, paid for a hundred of their one-way tickets, the foreign ministry says. South Africa has seen waves of this violence since two thousand eight... and two of Africa's largest economies are still answering it with airlifts rather than accountability.
<long pause>
Saudi air defences shot down two Houthi drones aimed at Riyadh, the capital. The blockade the Houthis declared in July was meant to target tankers — it now reaches the heart of the kingdom, and the coalition says two ballistic missiles fired at Khamis Mushait, home to an air base, were also brought down. Yemen's civilians, who've absorbed more than a decade of coalition bombing since twenty fifteen, are left bracing for whatever retaliation comes next.
<long pause>
Russian attacks killed five people in Kyiv, one of them a child, and injured forty-three across the capital. A daytime drone struck an office building in the Solomianskyi district, and three bodies were found in its parking lot. Vladimir Putin insists his country isn't preparing for war with Europe... but for Ukrainians, the war he's already waging still arrives in broad daylight.
<long pause>
All four of Iraq's international airports have halted flights to Iran. Ground-service firms reportedly wouldn't risk secondary American sanctions and being cut off from dollar clearing, so Baghdad ended up enforcing Washington's ban on Iranian airlines. Pilgrims, students and patients now face a land journey of at least twelve hours — proof that control of a currency can close a border no army ever did.

On the economy. <short pause>
Italian truckers are threatening to strike over diesel that now costs more than two euros forty a litre in nine of the country's twenty regions. An excise rise added six point one cents a litre, and Claudio Donati, who leads the hauliers' association Assotir, warns that a single day's stoppage would halt the country. Next door, Greece is subsidising fuel for just the first fifteen days of October... as governments answer the oil shock with patches written a fortnight at a time.
<long pause>
Pakistan's finance secretary says the International Monetary Fund wants one hundred and seventy-four legal amendments, covering taxation, energy, privatisation and the sovereign wealth fund. They're part of the fourth review of a seven billion dollar programme that also tests fifteen structural benchmarks and the finances of the provinces. Islamabad has turned to the Fund more than twenty times since the nineteen fifties, and each programme rewrites a little more of the country's law from outside.
<long pause>
Azerbaijan has signed a memorandum with Microsoft and a term sheet with Oman as it pitches itself as a manufacturing hub. The bet rests on the Middle Corridor, the trade route across the Caspian Sea between Asia and Europe, whose trade the World Bank estimates could triple by twenty thirty with enough investment. Silk Road caravans once passed through Baku on this same axis... whether modern cargo follows depends on ports and railways that are still unbuilt.

In science. <short pause>
Nearly three hundred millimetres of rain has fallen on Bangkok since Thursday, and Governor Chadchart Sittipunt has declared a disaster across all fifty of the city's districts. The Thai capital sits just one and a half metres above sea level on former marshland, every canal is full, and about nine hundred people are sheltering in more than two hundred centres. Decades of groundwater pumping have left the city sinking, and planners have warned since the great floods of twenty eleven that the water will keep coming back.
<long pause>
At the University of Colorado Anschutz, researchers have built a library of bacteriophages from fifteen years of sampling untreated wastewater. These viruses punch through bacterial walls but can't infect human cells, and the team is aiming them at drug-resistant gut bacteria that cause severe infections in people with weakened immunity. Phage therapy was pioneered in Tbilisi in the nineteen twenties and then sidelined by antibiotics... now rising resistance may bring it back.

In technology. <short pause>
A hacker has moved eighty-three million dollars of stolen XRP from the exchange Bitget, and Ripple can't freeze those funds under the network's current rules. Bitget, registered in the Seychelles, lost three hundred and fifty-one point six million dollars and froze withdrawals after attackers compromised a backend system and spoofed transaction data to trigger authorisation. It claims a hundred and twenty million users, all now learning the gap between an exchange saying their funds are safe and code that decides who actually holds them.
<long pause>
Apple has pulled Advanced Data Protection, its strongest iCloud encryption, from new users in Britain. The government ordered access to protected data under the Investigatory Powers Act of twenty sixteen, so identical iPhones in the UK now offer different security depending on when their owners switched the feature on. Critics have long called that law a snooper's charter, and the precedent it sets for other governments is still being written.
<long pause>
Latvia is moving to buy Barracuda cruise missiles from the American firm Anduril, which would make it the first Baltic state to field a long-range strike weapon of this kind. Its likely choice, a surface-launched version, reaches more than five hundred nautical miles, compared with about one hundred and eighty-six miles for the Army Tactical Missile System. For a country of fewer than two million people, the purchase shows how far the Baltic states now doubt that deterrence can simply be borrowed.
