This is your briefing for Wednesday, the seventh of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
A projectile's struck the oil tanker On Peace in the Strait of Hormuz, off the coast of Oman... injuring twelve of its crew, eleven of them Indian. Iran effectively closed the strait after the US-Israel war began, and it attacks ships that avoid its designated route, though no one's claimed this strike. India imports nearly eighty-five percent of its fuel, and today its central bank raised interest rates for the first time since twenty twenty-three.

In politics. <short pause>
Delhi police detained two hundred and sixty people on Tuesday, among them the opposition leaders Rahul and Priyanka Gandhi, and then filed a criminal case over members of parliament entering the state broadcaster's building. They were marching to demand the removal of India's chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar, over a revision of the voter rolls that deleted names from the lists. A protest about who gets to vote has become a case against the people who raised the question... and the voters who were removed are still waiting for an answer.
<long pause>
In the United States, Christa Pike is awake and speaking after surviving two doses of pentobarbital in a Tennessee execution. She's fifty, she's thought to be the first prisoner in the country to survive such an injection, and her lawyers say the intravenous lines failed before the team gave a backup set of syringes anyway. Back in nineteen forty-seven, the Supreme Court let Louisiana send Willie Francis to the electric chair a second time... so Tennessee may try again, while the family of Colleen Slemmer, the young woman Pike killed, waits once more.
<long pause>
Yemeni government forces say they've retaken ground near the Bab al-Mandab strait, while the Houthis say they repelled the offensive... and each claim is only its own side's. The advance leans on the Giants Brigades, southern fighters backed by the United Arab Emirates, and on Saudi-led air strikes, following a Saudi-Emirati meeting in Riyadh on the twenty-ninth of September. Those brigades want an independent south, as existed until unification in nineteen ninety, so the ground Riyadh gains may not belong to the government it says it's restoring.
<long pause>
A South African court has barred further demolitions in Airport Valley, in Gqeberha, after municipal bulldozers flattened fourteen homes on the twenty-eighth of September. Families are refusing to move into relocation units that have no roofs, water, power or sewerage, and a ruling from last year had already barred evictions until that site had water and sanitation. South Africa's constitution, written after apartheid's forced removals, promises that no one loses a home without a court's say... and the municipality's now been reminded of that twice.
<long pause>
Four Nigerian states, Edo, Oyo, Osun and Rivers, have approved a constitutional amendment that would allow state police forces. Nigeria has three hundred and eighty thousand officers for two hundred million people, about one for every six hundred and fifty, all under a single federal command that dates from the years of military rule. The amendment needs twenty-four of the thirty-six state assemblies and only creates the legal basis... so who'd pay for those forces, and who'd stop governors from misusing them, is still unanswered.
<long pause>
In Cairo, six journalists remain in fifteen-day remand, after several of them said they'd been tortured. The Journalists Syndicate has filed a complaint, Egypt's Interior Ministry denies any torture or mistreatment, and the Supreme State Security Prosecution has largely repeated its first questions. That prosecution's detention orders are renewable, and they've kept reporters behind bars for months or years without trial... so fifteen days is rarely just fifteen days.
<long pause>
Colombia's Cauca River, once choked with corpses and mining waste, was recognised in twenty twenty-three as a victim of the country's war. That status came from the Special Jurisdiction for Peace, the tribunal created by the twenty sixteen peace agreement, and the president, Abelardo de la Espriella, opposes that tribunal. If the court's weakened, a river that carried the dead loses its standing... and the people living along it lose a rare legal lever.
<long pause>
Britain's Conservative Party has taken thirty-nine donations from the digger maker JCB under Kemi Badenoch's leadership, worth more than one point three million pounds, according to the advocacy outlet Declassified UK. The United Nations accuses JCB of supplying equipment used to demolish Palestinian homes and farms, and Badenoch has opposed new sanctions over the settlements. Settlements on occupied land are illegal under the Fourth Geneva Convention... which leaves open what a party hoping to govern again owes to one of its donors, and what it owes to the families who lost their homes.
<long pause>
Across France, unions count four hundred and fifty thousand marchers, and the interior ministry two hundred and fifty-six thousand, after students blockading hundreds of high schools since late September drew teachers into the streets. The students cite missing teachers, overloaded timetables and dilapidated classrooms. French students have forced governments to back down before, in nineteen eighty-six and again in two thousand and six... whether this government listens is the open question.

On the economy. <short pause>
Twenty-three Indian sailors were rescued from a burning crude tanker off the Russian city of Sochi, on the Black Sea. Russia's transport ministry says naval drones hit the Liberian-flagged ship and that oil's burning openly on the water, while Kyiv hasn't commented. Much of Russia's crude leaves through its Black Sea ports, so both sides now target the shipping... and it's crews from third countries, sailing under borrowed flags, who carry the risk.
<long pause>
In Peru's Madre de Dios region, a Facebook post has been offering permitted mining land to would-be partners. Experts say the national registry meant to bring informal miners inside the law now gives illegal operations legal cover, because a permit holder can let others work his land for a percentage. The registry was meant to be temporary and has been extended again and again... while gold dug out with hydraulic pumps strips the Amazon forest and leaves mercury in its rivers.

In science. <short pause>
Biologists in Düsseldorf report that two ingredients found in deep-sea hydrothermal vents, phosphite and the metal palladium, could've powered life's first metabolism. Every living cell uses phosphate as its energy currency, but how that began before enzymes existed was unexplained, and the team writes in The FEBS Journal that both ingredients occur in vents that produce hydrogen. The idea that life began at such vents goes back to their discovery in the late nineteen seventies... this gives it a chemical step, though a reaction in a lab isn't proof of what happened four billion years ago.
<long pause>
CT scans of fifty-six ankle bones suggest that shoes reshape us: people who go barefoot carry denser bone at the front of the ankle, while in shoe wearers the bone's internal struts run in narrower directions. Spongy bone remodels itself under load, a principle the German surgeon Julius Wolff described in the nineteenth century. Fossil ankles have long been compared with those of modern shoe-wearing humans, so the baseline for reading how our ancestors walked may be skewed... though the study finds an association, not a cause.

In technology. <short pause>
South Korea's president, Lee Jae Myung, has asked ministries to inspect the security of core national infrastructure and to develop artificial intelligence tools that block cyberattacks before they land. He says a series of leaks at financial and public institutions appears to involve AI. One of the world's most connected countries keeps losing its citizens' data from the very institutions that hold the most of it... and no defence has ever stopped every attack, so the promise is larger than the plan.
<long pause>
The European Union's deadline for its reusable rocket demonstrator, Themis, has slipped from November twenty twenty-five to the thirty-first of December this year. That extension lets Themis attempt its first hop test at Esrange, in northern Sweden, under a project led by ArianeGroup worth forty-two point eight million euros, which funds the first two low-altitude hops. SpaceX flew its own hop tests in twenty twelve and landed an orbital booster in twenty fifteen... Europe's a decade behind, and its demonstrator hasn't yet left the ground.
