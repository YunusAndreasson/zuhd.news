This is your briefing for the nineteenth of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Iran's offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within six days... if Washington lifts its blockade of Iranian ports, its oil sanctions, and hostilities on every front. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi set out the terms in Baghdad, and hours later Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy said another tanker had struck a mine in the southern strait and caught fire, Iranian state media reported. Before the war, that narrow channel carried about a fifth of the world's oil and liquefied gas, and Brent crude now sits near a hundred and ten dollars a barrel.

In politics. <short pause>
Vladimir Putin has proposed a summit with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping to settle the war in Ukraine... with no Ukrainian at the table, the Wall Street Journal reports, citing its sources. He floated the idea on a call with Trump on the ninth of October, aiming for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit on the eighteenth of November — a format that recalls Yalta in nineteen forty-five, when great powers settled Europe's borders without the people living inside them. That same weekend, Trump accused Volodymyr Zelenskyy of never wanting the war to end, after Ukrainian drones struck Russian refineries.
<long pause>
Brazil's run-off is six days away, and two polls this week put Senator Flávio Bolsonaro ahead of President Lula by fifty-one percent to forty-nine. In their first debate, Bolsonaro, standing in for his convicted father's movement, promised five hundred thousand new prison places and a narco-terrorist label for gangs, while Lula defended minimum wage rises above inflation. Both men are now chasing thirty-three million voters, one in five, who skipped the first round... and whoever reaches them decides the largest democracy in Latin America.
<long pause>
In Cairo, Egypt's parliament debated for three hours behind closed doors and adjourned without a vote on sending troops to defend Saudi Arabia. President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi wants that authority after Houthi missiles and drones struck Riyadh's airport, and the constitution requires two-thirds of the House to approve any combat mission abroad. Egyptians still remember the nineteen sixties, when Nasser's intervention in Yemen cost thousands of soldiers' lives, and lawmakers will try again on Tuesday.
<long pause>
France's interior ministry says police arrested nearly two thousand four hundred people in a single night of rioting — more than double the earlier count — and nearly a thousand officers were injured, seven seriously. The unrest grew out of marches by five hundred and fifty thousand people over fuel prices and living costs, and rioters looted shops and burned the town hall in Limoges. Emmanuel Macron, addressing the nation from the Élysée, said those who destroyed must pay... answering a cost-of-living revolt with order rather than concessions.
<long pause>
One woman in Gaza City buried ten of her relatives this week, three years after Israeli bombing killed them inside their homes. Civil Defence crews recovered seventy-five bodies, but the Euro-Mediterranean Human Rights Monitor estimates sixty-eight million tonnes of rubble cover the Strip, and Israel still blocks the heavy machinery needed to clear it. A year after the October twenty twenty-five ceasefire, families across Gaza are still waiting to bury their dead.
<long pause>
Near Gwalior's Sheetla Mata temple in central India, a runaway truck loaded with paddy broke through a police checkpoint and killed at least fifteen devotees during Navratri. The road was meant to be closed to heavy vehicles, and investigators suspect brake failure on a steep slope where trucks share the way with tens of thousands of pilgrims on foot. Madhya Pradesh's chief minister, Mohan Yadav, removed the city's police chief and suspended eight officers... but suspensions don't fix the roads that bring the crowds.
<long pause>
Britain's BBC found that four hundred and ten million pounds of housing benefit flowed to so-called supported housing in a single year, while vulnerable residents, some recruited through social media, got little or no support. The law asks registered providers for little more than minimal care, yet one resident's place drew four hundred and forty-three pounds a week from the public purse. Charities and politicians say managing agents are treating people in crisis as cash cows, and the Department for Work and Pensions is the one paying.

On the economy. <short pause>
Punjab's government bought just a hundred and sixty thousand tonnes of wheat this season, against a target of two and a half million. Pakistan scrapped its wheat support price in April twenty twenty-five under International Monetary Fund pressure, leaving farmers to carry price risk alone while traders and millers set what they're paid. Banks have questioned the private purchasing scheme, crackdowns on hoarders haven't lifted prices, and growers in the country's breadbasket face another season with no floor beneath them.
<long pause>
Durban has called back three dozen displaced workers to unjam Africa's busiest container port. A computer system switch in mid-August froze cargo at the Durban Gateway Terminal, which the state firm Transnet runs with a private partner, and ships now wait about two hundred and fifty hours at anchor, with delays of eighteen to twenty-one days. For South African exporters, and for landlocked neighbours who route their trade through Durban, every week at sea is a cost passed down the line.
<long pause>
Yemen's Houthis have told Saudi Aramco shareholders to sell, and warned Saudi civilians and oil workers to stay away from the kingdom's oil facilities, Iran's state agency Mehr reported. The movement struck Aramco's Abqaiq plant in twenty nineteen, briefly knocking out about half of Saudi output, and its missiles have hit Aramco sites again since a truce collapsed this autumn. Mehr cites no new strike... yet a threat aimed at investors rather than soldiers is a bet that markets will flinch before armies do.

In science. <short pause>
Japan's Martian Moons eXploration probe is set to launch from Tanegashima this week, bound for Phobos and a return with samples by twenty thirty-one. The Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency has done this twice before, bringing back grains of the asteroids Itokawa and Ryugu on its Hayabusa missions. Those samples could settle whether Mars's moons are captured asteroids or debris blasted off Mars by an ancient impact... and if it's the second, Japan may bring home the first pieces of early Mars.
<long pause>
Physicists in Vienna have proven that no amount of energy makes a quantum system settle into equilibrium faster than a fixed speed limit. Paolo Abiuso's team at the Austrian Academy of Sciences showed in Nature Physics that the bound depends only on temperature and two of nature's constants, those of Planck and Boltzmann. Experiments on strange superconducting metals had hinted at this Planckian limit for decades, and now the question is what other materials have been quietly obeying it.

In technology. <short pause>
Shares in a Chinese robotics star valued at twenty-two billion yuan can't find buyers even at twelve billion, the mainland tech outlet TMTPost reports. Regulators now want recurring revenue before robot makers list, bankers say, so early investors who bet on humanoid robots have no way out through a stock market debut. It's the familiar arc of a Chinese funding boom, much like the bike-sharing bubble of twenty seventeen, and the losses now sit with the funds that bought at the top.
<long pause>
In the Philippines, US-backed data centres have become a target of anti-corruption protests that brought tens of thousands into Manila's streets, met by seventeen thousand police. The planned Pax Silica hub at New Clark City, worth forty to seventy billion dollars, would draw on scarce water and a grid that already charges some of Asia's highest power rates, and the local mayor says he was never consulted. An indigenous chieftain warns more than a thousand people could be displaced, the Quincy Institute's magazine Responsible Statecraft reports... and Manila hasn't said who'll bear the cost.
<long pause>
Broadcom, Verizon and seven other companies have refused to let their shares be tokenized, shrinking the list of sixty-three stocks a planned crypto venue backed by OKX had meant to launch with. Under a five-year exemption from the Securities and Exchange Commission, tokens backed one-for-one by real shares can trade around the clock in automated pools on the Uniswap protocol. The companies' objection is about consent... who keeps the shareholder register, and who votes, once a company's stock lives on a ledger it doesn't control.
