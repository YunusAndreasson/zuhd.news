In Abyei, activists say a strike on a crowded market has killed more than a hundred and fifty people... one of the deadliest single attacks of the war. Drones also hit Khartoum, Nyala, a gold mine and a power station across Sudan this week, killing hundreds more. Khartoum rejects any truce that leaves the Rapid Support Forces in control, and offers UN-monitored aid deliveries instead, so the bombing goes on.

In politics. <short pause>
Secretary of State Marco Rubio has invited Cubans into what he called a discreet partnership with American intelligence, and a Spanish-language video from the Central Intelligence Agency tells would-be informants how to make contact. The appeal lands on an island already living with fuel shortages and blackouts under a US oil blockade, a squeeze that echoes the Special Period of the nineteen-nineties, when the Soviet collapse cut off Cuba's oil. For eleven million Cubans, a regime-change aim once kept in classified files is now being said out loud, and Havana has dismissed it as a sign of Washington's desperation.
<long pause>
In Nigeria's Zamfara State, residents of Dansadau say bandits killed fourteen soldiers, overran the town's only military base, and then extorted about three hundred and sixty million naira from villagers. The bandit leader Dankarami Gwaska reportedly takes a cut of local gold mining, the trade that's funded armed groups across Nigeria's northwest for more than a decade. Nearly everyone has fled, and each military operation against the gangs seems to be followed by a reprisal that civilians, not soldiers, end up paying for.
<long pause>
Chinese authorities say a magnitude seven earthquake has killed sixteen people in Baqen County, in the Nagqu region of Xizang, with sixty-five injured and rescuers still searching for the missing. Those figures come through Iran's state-affiliated Mehr News Agency and are early... the quake struck late Thursday night at high altitude, where freezing temperatures shorten the window for anyone trapped. Xi Jinping has ordered an all-out rescue, and the toll on the plateau could still rise.
<long pause>
Seven hundred and twenty-seven doctors in Honduras remain unpaid after forty-seven days of protest, and the health ministry has only now promised to pay them. President Nasry Asfura holds the health portfolio himself — and on the twentieth of October, he left for a tour of Italy. At Tegucigalpa's Hospital Escuela, kidney patient Javier Ordóñez says supplies almost never arrive... and a promise isn't a paycheck.
<long pause>
Argentina's courts have dropped the criminal investigation into President Javier Milei and his sister Karina over the LIBRA meme coin he promoted before its price collapsed. Judge Marcelo Martínez de Giorgi ruled that a president's standing may amplify a token's launch, but doesn't make it a fraudulent scheme. That leaves investors who lost most of their money with no criminal case at all, and no court will now test how far a head of state's endorsement can move a market.
<long pause>
In Russian-occupied Oleshky, on the left bank of the Dnipro, up to two thousand people, most of them elderly, have been promised a pause in the fighting so aid can enter and civilians can leave. Strikingly, it was Melania Trump's office that announced it, not the White House or the State Department, and it's not yet clear both armies will honour it. Meanwhile, the Kremlin has confirmed talks in Abu Dhabi with Ukraine and the United States, but Vladimir Putin says he wants a final settlement, not a short truce, and Moscow still demands Ukrainian territory.
<long pause>
Haiti's elections have been cancelled again, and UN experts say gangs now control seventy percent of Port-au-Prince. The country hasn't voted since twenty sixteen, and a quarter of its people — one and a half million — have fled their homes. UN investigators traced close to a million rounds of ammunition to Dominican police stocks and say former senators armed the Viv Ansanm coalition, so the real question is who keeps the guns flowing while the ballot keeps slipping away.
<long pause>
Pakistan and Saudi Arabia have both ratified the Makkah defence pact, leaving just one of its three signatories still to sign off. Under Article Two, an attack on one member is an attack on all, language that echoes NATO's founding treaty of nineteen forty-nine, and it comes as Houthi attacks on the kingdom continue. A nuclear-armed Pakistan pledged to the defence of the Gulf's largest economy could change the calculus for anyone weighing a wider war.
<long pause>
Brazil's Federal Police have found a note worth a hundred and eighty thousand dollars, naming an indicted intelligence official, belonging to Willer Tomaz, a lawyer and friend of presidential candidate Flávio Bolsonaro. Police say Paulo Fortunato's unit inside the Brazilian Intelligence Agency used Israeli spyware to track politicians and journalists. What the money was for is now the question investigators have to answer.

On the economy. <short pause>
Iran's Revolutionary Guards say two more supertankers, carrying four and a half million barrels between them, struck mines on an unauthorized southern route through the Strait of Hormuz, and their crews abandoned ship. No one has independently confirmed the claim, but Russia's TASS reports that the foreign ministers of Egypt, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkey have jointly demanded the strait reopen in full. Tehran is using the mines to decide which ships may pass... and four Muslim-majority powers, two of them US allies, are now speaking as a bloc for the lane their economies depend on.
<long pause>
A tanker carrying forty thousand tonnes of Russian diesel has left Primorsk for New York, the first Russian diesel bound for the United States since the twenty twenty-two import ban. The country that built the sanctions on Russian fuel is now buying it, because the Gulf war has squeezed diesel supply, and Russia's deputy prime minister Alexander Novak says the sanctioned firm Rosneft would supply most of it. Yet the cargo covers under eight percent of one day's American demand, and shipping data show it arrives on the seventh of November — four days after the midterms.
<long pause>
Franklin Templeton has put tokenized money-market fund shares onto the XRP Ledger, a public blockchain, yet it can still freeze about two hundred million dollars of them. Holders need the issuer's approval, and two seventy-five-million-dollar subscriptions went to a single wallet that shares eight signing addresses with Franklin's own issuing account. Crypto was sold as money no institution could freeze... but as tokenized government debt spreads into crypto collateral, that promise is quietly being rewritten.

In science. <short pause>
Researchers in Tsukuba, Japan, have shown that every photon a superconductor absorbs produces a pair of magnetic flux quanta, the tiny, indivisible units of magnetism. Until now, detectors faced a trade-off: transition-edge sensors can count photons but slowly, while nanowire detectors are fast but can't count at all. Because each flux quantum makes a voltage pulse of fixed size, counting the pulses counts the light, a possible route to the fast, exact detectors that quantum computing and quantum communication still lack.

In technology. <short pause>
ASML's customers are already asking for lithography machines to be delivered in twenty twenty-nine and twenty thirty. The Dutch company, the world's only maker of extreme-ultraviolet tools, will raise capacity thirty percent for both its extreme-ultraviolet and deep-ultraviolet lines, yet demand for twenty twenty-seven and twenty twenty-eight still outstrips supply. Prices for high-end machines are going up, and with Chinese buyers shut out of the most advanced tools by export controls, a single company near Eindhoven is effectively setting the pace of the AI chip boom.
<long pause>
South Africa's Presidency took its website offline on Friday as a precaution against what it called a global cybersecurity event. The US Federal Bureau of Investigation has warned of two critical flaws in PHP, the programming language that, by one estimate, runs nearly seventy percent of websites whose technology can be identified. A thirty-year-old language quietly holds up much of the web... and few governments or companies can be sure they aren't exposed.
