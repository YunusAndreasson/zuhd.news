The International Court of Justice has ruled, by nine votes to six, that Myanmar's military committed genocide against the Rohingya... a judgment seven years in the making. The Gambia, a small West African nation backed by the Organization of Islamic Cooperation, brought the case after clearance operations in twenty seventeen killed at least ten thousand people and drove seven hundred thousand into Bangladesh. Around a million Rohingya still live in the camps of Cox's Bazar, waiting to learn what justice will mean for them.

In politics. <short pause>
Taliban authorities in Afghanistan's Balkh province have reportedly banned women's names on medical records, and health workers have been told to call female patients Bibi, meaning grandmother. Some women are reportedly being recorded as goat or cow. Since returning to power in twenty twenty-one, the Taliban have barred girls from secondary school and women from most work. Now a prescription without a name can reach the wrong patient... or no patient at all.
<long pause>
Ukraine has proposed a ceasefire limited to the Black Sea, after its grain exports fell seventy-six percent year on year by August. Kyiv and Moscow are both among the world's largest grain exporters, so strikes on their ports reach bread prices from Cairo to Istanbul. Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha says Moscow wants oil shipping included, which Kyiv wants handled separately. Moscow hasn't answered yet, and Vladimir Putin has just commissioned a new nuclear submarine with five more promised by twenty thirty.
<long pause>
Taiwanese voters are choosing thousands of local officials, and Puma Shen, a candidate wanted by Chinese police, is contesting the mayoralty against Taipei's incumbent, Chiang Wan-an. Four years ago, the Kuomintang won fourteen of twenty-two cities and counties, and Chiang took Taipei by double digits. Local races won't change policy toward Beijing, but they'll show where each party stands before the twenty twenty-eight presidential race.
<long pause>
Canada's Supreme Court has largely upheld Quebec's Bill twenty-one, so teachers and police there remain barred from wearing the hijab at work. The court ruled that legislatures may invoke the Charter's notwithstanding clause pre-emptively, against several rights at once. That clause was a compromise of nineteen eighty-two, written to win the provinces over to a new constitution. Some Muslim women have already removed their hijabs at work or left Quebec, and other provinces may now reach for the same tool.
<long pause>
In South Africa, Herman Mashaba has returned as mayor of Johannesburg, beating Helen Zille by one hundred and seventy votes to ninety-seven. His party, ActionSA, and the African National Congress now run all three metros in Gauteng, the country's economic heartland. That shuts out the Democratic Alliance, whose ninety-one seats fell far short of a majority. It's an awkward arrangement for the national unity government, where the ANC and the Alliance still sit together.

On the economy. <short pause>
American aircraft have disabled the Pacific Archer, a commercial ship trying to run the blockade of Iranian ports after leaving Bandar Abbas. Central Command says precision munitions struck the stern and the crew was unharmed. It's the most direct assault on merchant shipping in the Gulf since the tanker war of the nineteen eighties. Mohammad Mokhber, adviser to Iran's supreme leader, has vowed to keep Hormuz shut and break the embargo, leaving tankers squeezed from both sides.
<long pause>
Germany's parliament has passed a record budget that borrows more than two hundred and ten billion euros in twenty twenty-seven. Defence rises to nearly one hundred and forty billion, while development aid falls almost five percent. Much of the borrowing sits in special funds outside the constitutional debt brake, a rule written into law in twenty oh nine after the financial crisis. A country once famous for thrift is now funding rearmament on credit, and future interest bills will fall on taxpayers.
<long pause>
Spot bitcoin funds lost a record five point four billion dollars in November, as the price fell from above seventy-seven thousand dollars to about fifty-five thousand. The supply of stablecoins, the digital dollars traders use to buy, shrank by nearly fourteen billion in three weeks. Companies that hold bitcoin as a treasury asset now trade below the value of their coins, which makes new purchases harder to fund. The price has bounced to around sixty-one thousand, but the buyers who carried the last rally haven't returned.

In science. <short pause>
Measles killed an estimated seventy-two thousand people in twenty twenty-five, a record low, according to the World Health Organization. Yet Bolivia, Mexico and the United States all lost their measles elimination status this month. The Americas were declared free of the disease in twenty sixteen, but vaccine coverage there has fallen below the ninety percent it held before COVID. A virus that had been beaten is quietly finding its way back.
<long pause>
A new study finds that Lagos and Alexandria hold five million of the seven million Africans exposed to coastal flooding by twenty fifty. Under a moderate warming scenario, a million buildings and one hundred and eighty billion dollars of assets are at risk in an extreme sea-level event. Rising water is only half of it, because pumping groundwater and drilling for fossil fuels are sinking the land itself. Alexandria has faced the Mediterranean for more than two thousand years, and its future may now depend on what lies beneath it.
<long pause>
In the Philippines, two marine reserves cover just one thousand six hundred and seventy-seven hectares of a whale corridor spanning one point seven million. The humpbacks that breed in the Babuyan corridor numbered only about a thousand in twenty sixteen. Researchers have no data from inside the reserves, so they can't yet say whether protection is drawn where the whales actually gather.

In technology. <short pause>
OpenAI has countersued Apple in San Jose, alleging that Apple pressured hardware suppliers to stop working with it. The filing says one supplier moved OpenAI's metal finishing overnight after Apple objected, and another halted planned work under threat. Apple sued first in June over trade secrets, and it denies the claims. Whoever controls the component makers may decide whether anyone can build a rival to the iPhone.
<long pause>
India's former chief justice, BR Gavai, says a court ruling once relied on a judgment that never existed, apparently invented by artificial intelligence. He said courts have struck down outcomes where the technology was misused. Six years ago, India's Supreme Court held that an indefinite internet shutdown in Kashmir wasn't permissible, testing technology rules for proportionality. The harder question now is who checks the machine before its errors become law.
<long pause>
ByteDance's assistant, Doubao, can now edit a video in its own Jianying app from a single spoken sentence. Tests by the Chinese tech outlet Zhidx produced a usable talking-head cut, but the agent failed on a fifty-two-minute video. It leaves a draft that a human can still adjust, so editing isn't fully automated... at least not yet.
