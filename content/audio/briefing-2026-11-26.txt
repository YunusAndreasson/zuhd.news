This is your briefing for the twenty-sixth of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Black smoke rose over Saudi Arabia's largest refinery at Ras Tanura on Wednesday... and Yemen's Houthi movement says it carried out the strike. Its spokesman, Yahya Saree, called it retaliation for a Saudi attack on buses and trucks in Lahij that killed at least twenty-six people. With shipping through the Strait of Hormuz already running a quarter below normal, Gulf oil is now under pressure from both ends of the Arabian Peninsula.

In politics. <short pause>
Gaza's health ministry counted two hundred and seventy-two meningitis cases in October, and on the twenty-fourth of November it declared an epidemic alert across the whole Strip. In a normal month Gaza sees thirty to fifty cases, and the ministry blames Israel's destruction of infrastructure, crowded shelters and winter floods for a disease that's already killed fifteen people this year. Meningitis spreads fastest where people sleep close together... and Gaza's winter is only beginning.
<long pause>
In Abuja, the text of Nigeria's new health agreement with the United States shows what two point oh eight billion dollars of American money buys: Nigeria must ship pathogen samples to America within five days of any request. Washington may pass those samples to American companies and keep using them even if Nigeria ends the deal. It's the same question of virus sovereignty that led Indonesia to withhold bird flu samples in two thousand seven, and Nigeria, which is adding about three billion dollars of its own, still hasn't said who'll own any medicine made from its people's samples.
<long pause>
At least thirty people were killed in Giza when a fuel tanker burst into flames on Cairo's Ring Road, engulfing the cars around it. State media say a fallen high-voltage cable made the driver lose control, sending the tanker across the median in the working-class district of Al-Warraq. It's one of Egypt's deadliest road accidents in years, and it raises a hard question about why hazardous fuel moves through such densely packed neighbourhoods at all.
<long pause>
Honduran police investigators have received two hundred and nineteen reports of missing women and girls so far this year. The national university's violence observatory counted two hundred and eleven violent deaths of women from January to September, in a country where few of these killings ever end in a conviction. On the twenty-fifth of November, the day the world marks against violence toward women, feminist groups in Tegucigalpa demanded immediate, funded searches... and an end to impunity.
<long pause>
Further north, Ukraine's General Staff says it damaged five aircraft at Savasleyka airbase, east of Moscow, including three MiG thirty-ones used to fire hypersonic missiles. Kyiv's long-range drones have already cut Russian refinery processing by forty-five percent since late October, according to industry sources. Russia hasn't confirmed the losses, and President Vladimir Putin has already vowed retaliation for the strikes on his refineries.
<long pause>
Chen Jining, Shanghai's party secretary and a sitting member of the Politburo, is under investigation by China's discipline watchdog. He's the fourth Politburo member pushed out in two years, and the first Shanghai party chief to fall since Chen Liangyu was purged in two thousand six. The watchdog cited serious violations of discipline and law, usually code for corruption, and hasn't said who'll now run the country's financial capital.
<long pause>
A fourteen-month-old girl in Karak has become Pakistan's tenth polio case of twenty twenty-six, and the district's first in six years. Pakistan and Afghanistan are the last two countries where wild polio still circulates, and eight of this year's cases come from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. The national emergency operations centre says her virus is genetically linked to intense circulation in the province's south... which means it's travelling.

On the economy. <short pause>
Gold has fallen below three thousand five hundred dollars an ounce for the first time since September twenty twenty-five, and it's now down about thirty-seven percent from this year's record. Hot American inflation data point to Federal Reserve rate hikes, and when interest rates rise, holding a metal that pays nothing gets more expensive. That's the reverse of the old inflation-hedge story, and it lands hardest on families and central banks that keep their savings in bullion.
<long pause>
India's rupee settled at ninety-two point two three to the dollar, an eight-month high, as foreign investors poured nearly fifteen billion dollars into Indian stocks in November. Selling in AI-linked shares elsewhere sent that money toward a market many fund managers had shunned for two years. The risk is oil: India imports most of its crude, and Brent prices are climbing again.
<long pause>
Brazil's government has called new American tariff proposals unacceptable, after Washington floated a ten percent duty on aircraft... one that lands squarely on Embraer. Aircraft are Brazil's fourth-largest export to the United States, and the proposal relies on Section three-oh-one, an unfair-trade law, because the Supreme Court struck down emergency-law tariffs in February. American airlines have no domestic alternative for regional jets, so Brasília is betting the duty would hurt American travellers first, and it isn't ruling out reciprocity.
<long pause>
Across the border, Argentina appears to have slipped into recession after two straight quarters of contraction under President Javier Milei. Economist Elena Donoso of Emerald Capital says private wages sit three and a half percent below late twenty twenty-three, and manufacturing's down eight percent. The sectors that employ the most people are the ones shrinking, which means the cost of Milei's austerity is falling on workers first.

In science. <short pause>
The European Space Agency's Hera probe has sent back the first close images of Dimorphos, the small asteroid NASA's DART spacecraft rammed in twenty twenty-two. A crater now covers nearly its whole front face, and because Dimorphos is a loose rubble pile, it convulsed like a fluid and shed hundreds of boulders. It's humanity's first real look at what a deflection strike does... and planners will need that answer before they ever have to move a rock heading for Earth.
<long pause>
Drained peatlands cause about two point seven percent of human-caused warming, according to a study in Nature from researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Peat holds thirty percent of the world's soil carbon on just three to four percent of its land, and once it's drained, microbes keep releasing carbon dioxide for hundreds of years. Indonesia learned the cost in twenty fifteen, when drained peat burned for months, and the open question is whether countries will count these emissions at all.

In technology. <short pause>
America's Federal Communications Commission has licensed SpaceX to launch up to one million satellites... fifty times everything now in orbit. The commission skipped environmental review, ruling that launch emissions fall outside its purview and that concerns about reentry pollution weren't supported by the record. One national regulator has effectively claimed orbit and radio spectrum that every country shares, while astronomers and other nations' satellites carry the debris risk.
<long pause>
The password manager One Password says an attacker downloaded the names and email addresses of a subset of its users. A hijacked employee account on GitHub let the intruder push malicious code to backend services and copy private source code, though vault contents and the keys protecting them weren't touched, according to the company. For millions who trust a single company with every login... the next danger is convincing phishing emails sent to exactly those addresses.
