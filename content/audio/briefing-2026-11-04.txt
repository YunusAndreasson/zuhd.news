This is your briefing for the fourth of November, twenty twenty-six.

Iran has set five conditions for any regional security deal... and they reach far beyond what mediators are offering. Armed forces chief Ali Abdollahi demands recognition of Iranian sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz, sanctions relief and war reparations, while mediators seek a simpler swap: an end to attacks in the strait for a lifted American blockade. Qatari envoys now fly to Tehran, with Brent crude back at a hundred and six dollars after American strikes on Qeshm Island.

In politics. <short pause>
Israel has shut the Rafah crossing with Egypt completely, and forty-two percent of Gaza's essential medicines are already depleted. The closure blocks commercial goods as well as aid, and it comes as the UN Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees, known as UNRWA, sheds staff over a seventy million dollar deficit, ending forty-three microfinance contracts in Gaza in September alone. Rafah was the one gate out of Gaza that didn't open onto Israel... and the agency created in nineteen forty-nine to sustain refugees is shrinking just as the need grows.
<long pause>
Democrats have won the US Senate, flipping Texas for the first time in thirty-eight years. Voters disillusioned by an unpopular war in Iran and rising prices carried Roy Cooper in North Carolina, while in Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed could become the chamber's first Muslim after Republican ads tied him to terrorism and Sharia. And on Election Day itself, the Justice Department sent a criminal target letter to Democratic lawyer Marc Elias over a namesake candidate in Alaska's race, a step the department's own norms usually avoid so close to a vote.
<long pause>
Greenland's parliament passed a security deal with Washington and Copenhagen sixteen to nothing... but seven members abstained. The agreement lets the US expand its operations at Pituffik Space Base, the Cold War installation once known as Thule, and it still applies if Greenland ever chooses independence from Denmark. On an island Trump has threatened to seize, that clause means part of the price of future sovereignty may already have been paid.
<long pause>
A day before South Africa's local elections, a group in party clothing held electoral officials hostage at a ballot warehouse in Marianhill, in KwaZulu-Natal. They demanded that special-vote ballot boxes be moved, disrupting two of the site's twenty-four voting stations, and coverage names the uMkhonto weSizwe party in disruptions across the province. KwaZulu-Natal's elections have carried the shadow of political violence since the nineteen-nineties, and this vote decides who runs municipalities that are, in many cases, broke.
<long pause>
Honduras has recognised Próspera, a private charter city on Roatán island that its own Supreme Court declared unconstitutional. Development secretary Eddy Ordóñez signed the recognition digitally on the seventeenth of July, while the Winklevoss twins, who gave more than forty-six million dollars to Trump's agenda, invest in a Próspera hub. In the country that gave the world the phrase banana republic, a private enclave backed by American money is suing Hondurans for billions over the repeal of the law that created it.
<long pause>
Two helicopters clipped rotors while landing near Pheriche, about twelve kilometres south of Everest Base Camp, killing Indian tourist Vasanth Kumar Balaramudu. Nepal's Civil Aviation Authority says both aircraft tried to set down on the same spot under clear skies, in a Khumbu valley crowded with small operators during the autumn trekking season. For more than a decade, the European Union has barred Nepali airlines from its skies over safety... and the question now is who's directing traffic at the roof of the world.
<long pause>
At the University of Jos in Nigeria, a wheelchair user crawls into her hostel bathroom. The country's twenty eighteen disability law gave public buildings until January twenty twenty-four to add ramps and lifts, yet the Village Hostel, built in the mid-nineteen-eighties, has neither, and its residents still pay a five thousand naira facility improvement fee. A federal university is breaking a federal law... and it isn't clear that anyone is enforcing it.

On the economy. <short pause>
The Swiss franc rose almost one percent and the yen seven-tenths of a percent as early American results gave the dollar its worst day in a month. Markets are pricing a Congress that can check Trump's war spending and tariffs, and a day earlier Brent crude fell more than seven percent when Tehran said its contacts with Washington were continuing. Both currencies have served as refuges since the crash of two thousand eight... and their rise says traders now see risk in Washington itself.
<long pause>
Pakistan's oil refiners earned thirty-two point two billion rupees in just three months, according to Topline Securities, what the petroleum minister calls a normal year's profit. Higher refining margins during the war between the US and Iran drove the jump, and fuel dealers now demand a forensic audit they say could cut pump prices by forty to fifty rupees a litre. Pakistanis are paying war prices under a formula the state itself sets... so the question is who that formula was written to protect.
<long pause>
Bundesbank President Joachim Nagel says a third European Central Bank rate hike this year, in December, looks almost inevitable. The bank expects inflation above four percent in the coming months, driven by energy prices over which it has no control. That's the trap Europe faced in the oil shocks of the nineteen-seventies... higher rates can't pump more oil, but they can slow an economy already paying for someone else's war.

In science. <short pause>
Deep under the Mediterranean off Toulon, the K M three Net collaboration has used five thousand eight hundred and twenty-eight neutrinos to test one of Einstein's foundations. Its ORCA telescope, a lattice of light sensors two thousand four hundred and fifty metres down, records neutrinos born when cosmic rays strike the atmosphere, probing the principle that physics looks the same to every observer. Some theories uniting gravity with quantum physics predict that principle breaks down... and a crack would be the first experimental clue to how the universe truly fits together.
<long pause>
The French drugmaker Servier says its one point five five billion dollar drug, sevasemten, failed to slow Becker muscular dystrophy in its pivotal trial. Sevasemten had shown promise in a smaller randomised study, but the larger Phase Three results didn't align, and Servier is still analysing the full data. Becker is a genetic cousin of Duchenne muscular dystrophy, first described in the nineteen-fifties... and seventy years on, its patients still have no approved treatment.

In technology. <short pause>
Kling, the artificial intelligence video unit of China's Kuaishou, has raised about nineteen point eight billion yuan at a private value of eighteen billion dollars, more than its parent is worth on the Hong Kong market. Its quarterly revenue rose from over six hundred and fifty million yuan to over eight hundred and fifty million, while the wider group grew just one point four percent. Rivals Tencent, Alibaba Cloud and Baidu have all taken stakes... a sign that China's platforms may back one video model together rather than each building their own.
<long pause>
Ukrainian drone strikes have forced Yandex, Russia's largest technology company, to relocate its three main supercomputers. The attacks disrupted payments, banking, streaming and ride-hailing for millions, and a decree Putin signed on the second of November makes data-centre operators reserve ten percent of their capacity for the state. Russia once buried its command bunkers against nuclear war... now it's burying its servers, and handing the state a share of the machinery as it goes.
<long pause>
In Brazil, accounts on X posting fabricated gambling wins drew nearly half a million views as the Supreme Court held a public hearing on challenges to President Lula's betting ban. Agência Pública found the accounts follow and amplify one another, and one promoted Greenasbet, a site registered in California. Brazilians lose billions a month to online betting, much of it from welfare recipients... and the court must now weigh a ban while the industry's voice is already in the feed.
