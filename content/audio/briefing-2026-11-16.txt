This is your briefing for the sixteenth of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Iran's Revolutionary Guard says two tankers have hit mines in the Strait of Hormuz... after straying outside the lane Tehran now authorises. It claims one supertanker, carrying over two million barrels, sank, though no one's independently confirmed either strike. Mines haven't stalked these waters like this since the tanker war of the nineteen-eighties, and now crews, insurers and importers across South Asia must decide whether Iran's lane is the only safe one left.

In politics. <short pause>
Drones struck three merchant ships inside Romanian waters overnight, and Romania, a NATO member, scrambled fighter jets twice. Two of the vessels were hit just four and five nautical miles off the Sulina canal, the mouth of the Danube that's carried Ukraine's grain since Russia's navy throttled its Black Sea ports in twenty twenty-two. Bucharest hasn't said where the drones came from... and that silence leaves open what an attack in alliance waters actually obliges NATO to do.
<long pause>
On Yemen's coast, the Southern Armed Forces have pulled out of Dhubab airport and the area around the Bab el-Mandeb, leaving the Houthis holding the Red Sea's southern chokepoint. The southern forces say they're needed to defend Aden and to break the siege of Taiz, a city cut off for most of a decade of war. With Iran already disrupting Hormuz, Gulf oil exporters now face two chokepoints at once, and few routes left that don't pass within range of someone's guns.
<long pause>
In Israel, Gadi Eisenkot is negotiating a unity government with Benjamin Netanyahu, the caretaker prime minister he says must leave office. Neither bloc reached the sixty-one seats needed for a Knesset majority, while Arab-led parties won a record nineteen, and Eisenkot's camp together with both Arab factions would hold sixty-seven. Avigdor Lieberman and Naftali Bennett refuse to rely on those Arab votes, though Bennett did exactly that in twenty twenty-one, when Ra'am became the first independent Arab party in a governing coalition.
<long pause>
At the Francophonie summit in Phnom Penh, the Democratic Republic of the Congo is threatening to quit the organisation over Rwanda's candidate for its top post. With more than a hundred and ten million people, Congo is the most populous country where French is an official language, and UN experts say Rwanda backs the M twenty-three rebels who hold much of its east. Kinshasa is backing a Romanian challenger against Louise Mushikiwabo... and in a body that decides by consensus, the war in the Kivus may finally carry a diplomatic price.
<long pause>
Kandahar's Taliban-run information department has phoned radio stations and banned cricket commentary, news and even results, two broadcasters said. Cricket is the one major sport the government still lets men and boys play, and it's the game that carried Afghanistan from refugee camps in Pakistan to a World Cup semi-final in twenty twenty-four. Whether the ban spreads beyond the movement's spiritual heartland will show how much of that national pride the leadership is willing to tolerate.
<long pause>
Cape Verdeans are choosing a president, and more of them live abroad than on the islands themselves. Six candidates are standing before some three hundred thousand voters, among them Joana Rosa, the first woman ever to run, in a country that's changed power peacefully at the ballot box since its first multiparty election in nineteen ninety-one. President José Maria Neves's party won thirty-seven of seventy-two seats in May, so this vote decides whether the head of state, who holds a veto, becomes the government's ally or its check.

On the economy. <short pause>
In Yongin, South Korea's SK Hynix is building a four-fab, four hundred and fifty billion dollar chip complex, and its first plant alone will stand a hundred and fifty metres tall. The company makes high-bandwidth memory, the stacked chips that AI processors can't run without, and it'll also spend one point four trillion won, about a billion dollars, over five years backing smaller domestic suppliers. Memory has become the bottleneck of the AI build-out, and nobody yet knows how long consumers will pay for that squeeze in pricier phones and laptops.
<long pause>
Brazil's president, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, says the United States now accounts for just nine point two percent of his country's trade. He's in Manila alongside Canada's Mark Carney and Australia's Anthony Albanese, each seeking Southeast Asian deals to hedge against Trump's tariffs, while Brazil's trade with China has grown nearly twenty percent. Washington was Brazil's biggest partner for most of the last century, until China overtook it in two thousand nine... and the middle powers are now testing whether Southeast Asia can be the market they route their trade through.
<long pause>
Nigeria flared twelve percent more gas in twenty twenty-four, the World Bank says, burning off fuel it could have sold or used at home. Its flaring intensity reached twelve cubic metres for every barrel of oil, more than twice the global average, in a delta where flares have burned beside villages for decades and deadline after deadline to end them has passed. An official at the upstream regulator says the energy transition is now starving gas-capture projects of funding, a twist that leaves the communities beside the flares still breathing them.
<long pause>
Egypt has set aside a hundred and twelve million square metres of Gulf of Suez coastline to cut its public debt. Finance Minister Ahmed Kouchouk gave no value or timetable, but one model is Ras El Hekma, the twenty twenty-four deal in which Abu Dhabi committed thirty-five billion dollars for Mediterranean coast and helped pull Egypt back from a currency crisis. Selling land to creditors buys time... yet it doesn't change why the debt keeps growing.

In science. <short pause>
Lancaster University researchers have used ultrasound to watch speakers' tongues in Blackburn, one of the last places in England still pronouncing the R after vowels. Most of England once spoke that way, before the R-less speech of the southern elite spread outward from the late eighteenth century, which is partly why Americans still say it. Younger speakers made weaker tongue movements for the sound, sometimes movements listeners can't hear at all... so an accent may keep dying inside the mouth long after the ear has stopped noticing.
<long pause>
In Saudi Arabia's Empty Quarter, scientists have traced an ancient lake at Mundafan that once covered three hundred and forty square kilometres. Humans lived on its shore a hundred and twenty-five thousand years ago among hippos, ostriches and extinct buffaloes, during one of the wet phases that turned Arabia green and opened a corridor for people moving out of Africa. Residue on five thousand five hundred and eleven stone tools shows toxic plants, possibly used for hunting, raising the question of how early our ancestors learned to turn poison into a tool.

In technology. <short pause>
United Launch Alliance's perfect record ended on Sunday, when its Vulcan rocket failed ten minutes after liftoff from Cape Canaveral and lost all forty Amazon Leo satellites aboard. Amazon has only three hundred and ninety-eight satellites in orbit, twelve percent of the three thousand two hundred and thirty-six it needs, and it depends on scarce launches from companies other than its rival SpaceX. The company says it has no data on the cause yet, and if investigators ground Vulcan, both Amazon and the American military's launch schedule will wait with it.
<long pause>
Ukraine's interceptor drones lose contact above a hundred and sixty-one kilometres an hour, and President Volodymyr Zelensky blames limits SpaceX has placed on its Starshield satellite service. That leaves the Shahed drones Russia launches free to outrun interceptors to the power grid before winter, and it isn't the first time: in twenty twenty-two Elon Musk refused to extend Starlink coverage for a Ukrainian strike near Crimea. Zelensky says the limit came despite assurances from Secretary of State Marco Rubio that Washington held more control... leaving open who really decides whether Ukrainian homes have heat.
<long pause>
Coles, one of Australia's two dominant supermarket chains, has told the stock exchange it's investigating a cyber attack, without saying what happened. A small number of customers can't use Liquorland click-and-collect, and the company says it's found no evidence that customer, staff or supplier data was compromised, a careful line in a country still scarred by the twenty twenty-two breaches at Optus and Medibank, which exposed millions. Until Coles says more, its shoppers are left to trust a disclosure that tells them very little.
