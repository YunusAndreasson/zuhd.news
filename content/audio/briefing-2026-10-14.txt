A missile has set fire to a Saudi Aramco administrative building in Dhahran... and the war has now reached the Eastern Province, the heart of the kingdom's oil industry. The Saudi-led coalition struck back at a fuel station in Sanaa and the loading piers of Hodeidah, the port most Yemenis depend on for imported fuel and food. In the Strait of Hormuz, six ships were hit in a single day, the most of the war so far, and at least two Indian sailors are missing.

In politics. <short pause>
Russia's Federal Security Service has charged Yuki Imai, a reporter for Japan's Hokkaido Shimbun, with espionage, a charge that carries up to twenty years. He was detained on Sakhalin, the island whose southern half Japan governed until nineteen forty-five, and Moscow has used spy charges against foreign reporters before, including Evan Gershkovich, who was sentenced to sixteen years. Tokyo has asked only for impartial treatment, and it's unclear what a government that sanctions Russia has left to trade for him.
<long pause>
In Cairo, six fact-checkers from the platform Matsada'ash will stay in jail for another fifteen days. Egypt's Supreme State Security Prosecution accuses them of joining a terrorist group, spreading false news and taking foreign money, and it's the same body that investigates the case and renews the detention, block after block, often for months without trial. Egypt's constitution already forbids jailing journalists over what they publish, and a draft law now promises the same... so the question is whether this one would be obeyed.
<long pause>
A widow in Gaza City with six children can't reach the five thousand dollars sitting in her late husband's bank account. Banks demand a court-issued inheritance certificate, but Israel's military blew up Gaza's Palace of Justice on the fourth of December, twenty twenty-three, and the courts that once settled inheritance, divorce and maintenance have stopped working. When a judiciary is destroyed, the cost falls hardest on women who have no other way to claim what's theirs.
<long pause>
Pakistan's police fired live rounds into the air and tear gas at protesters in Bara, in Khyber district, injuring at least five supporters of Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf. Governor's rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has sidelined the party's chief minister, Sohail Afridi, and Bara sits in the former tribal areas that were merged into the province only in twenty eighteen. Islamabad is betting that it can govern the province without the party that won it.
<long pause>
Kyrgyzstan has detained the blogger Syrgak Kenzhebaev abroad and brought him home. For two months he'd alleged that Sabyr Japarov, the president's brother, settled political questions and influenced the customs service. The interior ministry now accuses him of calling for mass unrest and of fraud, charges that'll test how far Bishkek's reach extends beyond its borders.
<long pause>
Donald Trump has asked Kimberly Guilfoyle, his ambassador to Greece, to resign after reports that two Greek tycoons paid for her luxury trips. She's also accused of promoting a Greek construction company and letting its lobbyist into private diplomatic meetings, while a Trump donor says she asked him to wire a hundred thousand dollars. Guilfoyle denies wrongdoing and says she'll fight, leaving open what Greek business interests expected from an embassy that matters for East Mediterranean energy and arms deals.

On the economy. <short pause>
The International Monetary Fund projects that emerging economies will spend almost eleven percent of their tax revenue on debt payments. That's up from about seven percent between twenty eleven and twenty nineteen, driven by borrowing costs that rise with the United States ten-year Treasury yield, now at a twenty-four-year high. Every extra point of interest paid to creditors in Islamabad, Cairo or Abuja is money not spent on schools and clinics.
<long pause>
Shell is selling its Nigerian gas distribution business, which serves more than a hundred and thirty customers, to the private Nigerian firm Vaaris Energy. The deal hands over a hundred and seventy kilometres of pipeline across Ogun, Abia and Rivers states, after more than twenty-five years of Shell piping gas to the country's factories. It continues the majors' long retreat from Nigeria's onshore assets, and factory owners will now learn whether local ownership means cheaper gas or less reliable supply.
<long pause>
South Africa's trade minister, Parks Tau, is pressing carmakers after the local share of the cars they build fell to thirty-eight point one percent. His review found the industry imports a hundred and fifty-one billion rand of components a year, far from the target of sixty percent local content by twenty thirty-five. Tau wants incentives to reward manufacturing at home, and the foreign carmakers that dominate the industry haven't yet said whether they'll agree.
<long pause>
Hackers heated supermarket fridges to fifty degrees across France, starting fires. Mousquetaires, which owns Intermarché, says three hundred of its stores were hit overnight through refrigeration controllers left exposed to the internet, with losses in the millions of euros. It's a reminder that the machines keeping food safe were connected to the internet long before anyone secured them.

In science. <short pause>
Ebola has now killed four thousand three hundred and eighty-one people in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, out of nine thousand and sixty-six confirmed cases. Twelve days ago the toll stood near four thousand, and this Bundibugyo strain has no approved vaccine or treatment, while M23's advance and community mistrust slow the response across seven provinces. Kenya found its first imported case last week... and the region is waiting to see whether it stays the only one.
<long pause>
A fossil braincase that sat unnoticed in the drawers of London's Natural History Museum since nineteen twenty-four has been named as Europe's first megaraptoran. Invictibellatrix, found on the Isle of Wight, was a five-metre hunter about the weight of a tiger, from a lineage known mostly from South America, Australia and Asia. It supports the idea that these predators spread from Asia through Europe into Africa and South America, though the rest of the route is still missing.
<long pause>
Researchers in Neuchâtel used real-time magnetic resonance imaging to watch thirty-two people speak, and found they used different tongue movements to make identical vowels. Writing in the journal PLOS Biology, they suggest such variation in anatomy could help explain how the world's roughly seven thousand languages drifted apart. If our bodies quietly shape how we speak, part of linguistic history may be written in the throat.

In technology. <short pause>
Anthropic says its AI agents now merge thirty thousand code changes a day, twelve times the rate in May, and the tool every programmer uses couldn't keep up. Git was built for human-paced coding, so seven engineers spent five months building Scion, which commits and merges on servers and needs a two-hundredth of the computing power of the old Git mirrors. The company won't release it, but every software team using agents will hit the same wall.
<long pause>
China's Envision says wind farms now supply eighty percent of the power for its data centre in Ulanqab, in Inner Mongolia. Grid substations take a year to eighteen months to build, while data-centre customers want capacity in six, so Envision runs dedicated lines with batteries, forecasting models and high-voltage direct current. Whether the model scales could decide where the next generation of AI computing gets built.
