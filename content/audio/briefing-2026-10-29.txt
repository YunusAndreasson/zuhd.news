This is your briefing for the twenty-ninth of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
At least sixty-eight people have died near Toledo after one train ran into another... it's Spain's third fatal rail crash this year, after forty-six were killed in January. A signalling failure had halted traffic, and a controller then cleared a Renfe train past a red light at about two hundred kilometres an hour. For a country that's built Europe's largest high-speed network, the question now is whether the fault lies with one person or with the system.

In politics. <short pause>
Qatar has put a seven-day plan to end the war on the table, and Tehran says it's in contact with mediators, even as one Iranian official calls the ideas unacceptable. Washington has reportedly warned Gulf states to brace for a short, intense round of strikes after the third of November midterms, with a carrier group due to join about twenty warships already in the region. Iran's security officials, for their part, have drawn up plans to hit the undersea cables beneath the Gulf... so the war's next phase could reach the lines that carry the region's internet and its money.
<long pause>
Cyclone Arnab came ashore near Kakinada in India's Andhra Pradesh with winds of up to one hundred and thirty-five kilometres an hour, and Chief Minister Naidu says five people have died. Officials told about ninety-eight thousand people to leave the coast before landfall, on a shoreline where a single cyclone in nineteen seventy-seven killed around ten thousand. What remains is the damage in the fields: floods have hit crops on more than two hundred and twenty thousand hectares, and three hundred and forty-five thousand farmers now face the cost.
<long pause>
In New York, Judge Alvin Hellerstein, who was presiding over the drug trafficking case against Venezuela's deposed president Nicolás Maduro, has died at ninety-two, and the case now needs a new judge. Not since Panama's Manuel Noriega, taken after a US invasion in nineteen eighty-nine, has an American court tried a foreign leader removed by American force. Meanwhile in Caracas, talks overseen by Washington have agreed a committee to nominate new Supreme Tribunal justices... and whoever controls those nominations may shape Venezuela long after any verdict.
<long pause>
Cuauhtémoc Cárdenas, the man many Mexicans believe truly won the nineteen eighty-eight presidential election, has died at ninety-two. On election night the vote-counting system went dark, and the ruling party's candidate emerged the winner — a defeat President Claudia Sheinbaum now calls the beginning of Mexico's democratic transformation. Son of Lázaro Cárdenas, who nationalised Mexico's oil in nineteen thirty-eight, he became Mexico City's first elected mayor, and the left he helped build would eventually take the presidency.
<long pause>
One year ago yesterday, police stormed the Penha and Alemão favelas of Rio de Janeiro, and one hundred and twenty-two people died in Brazil's deadliest police raid. That toll exceeds even the Carandiru prison massacre of nineteen ninety-two, when police in São Paulo killed one hundred and eleven inmates. Residents still remember carrying bodies out of the forest and laying them in a square in Vila Cruzeiro... and a year on, it's unclear whether anyone who ordered the raid will answer for it.
<long pause>
South Africa's opposition Economic Freedom Fighters posed no threat to national security, a former spy has told a court in Pretoria, testifying under a court order that hides his identity. Prosecutors allege that twelve million rand in public money ran through Project Mayibuye, a secret twenty fifteen operation by the State Security Agency against the party, which the witness says lacked proper authorisation. Under apartheid, the security police spied on liberation movements... now a court must decide whether the democratic state's spies turned on its own opposition, with local elections due on the fourth of November.
<long pause>
In Lahore, Noreen Niazi, a sister of jailed former prime minister Imran Khan, has been moved from detention to a cardiology hospital with high blood pressure, her lawyer says. All three of Khan's sisters have been held since the twenty-first of September, under thirty-day orders issued with a public-order law written in nineteen sixty, during Ayub Khan's military rule. Preventive detention needs no charge and no trial, and how long it can keep a prisoner's family behind bars is a question Pakistan's courts haven't yet answered.

On the economy. <short pause>
After eight months of war, with the Strait of Hormuz effectively shut, the head of Abu Dhabi's Al Gharbia Pipe Company says every country in the region is now considering a pipeline around it. The United Arab Emirates is doubling its three-hundred-and-sixty-kilometre Habshan to Fujairah line beyond its current one and a half million barrels a day, Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline already reaches the Red Sea, and Iraq is planning a route of its own. If the Gulf's oil learns to avoid Hormuz, the strait's power as leverage over the world economy may shrink with it.
<long pause>
Kazakhstan's President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev wants to spend six and a half trillion tenge, about fourteen billion dollars, from the National Fund on roads, rail, energy and logistics through twenty twenty-nine. That's more than a fifth of a fund worth around sixty-seven billion dollars, created in the year two thousand to save oil wealth for future generations, and the plan scraps a goal of growing it to one hundred billion by twenty thirty. It's a bet that infrastructure in a landlocked country between China and Europe will pay back more than savings would... though if oil prices fall, there'll be less cushion left.
<long pause>
Nigeria still burns off about five hundred million cubic feet of gas a day, its regulator says, and it's renewed a pledge to end routine flaring by twenty thirty. Flaring has fallen from ninety percent of gas output to seven, but the country's first legal deadline to stop it was set for nineteen eighty-four, and every deadline since has slipped. With a penalty of just two dollars per thousand cubic feet, communities in the Niger Delta who live beside the flames may wonder whether burning is still cheaper than stopping.
<long pause>
Alphabet's free cash flow has turned negative... minus nine point one billion dollars, as its quarterly capital spending more than doubled to fifty-one point four billion. Google Cloud revenue roughly doubled and the company reports a cloud backlog of five hundred and sixty-one billion dollars, but search missed expectations, and its own executives blamed AI Mode, a chatbot interface, for slower ad growth. The world's most profitable advertising business is now spending more than it earns to build AI, and investors, who sent shares down ten percent after hours, aren't sure the trade's worth it.

In science. <short pause>
Surgeons in Russia have cut a damaged section out of a patient's completely injured spinal cord and glued in a segment from a donor, in what New Scientist calls a world first. The closest precedent came in twenty fourteen, when Polish doctors helped a paralysed firefighter, Darek Fidyka, walk again with a frame using cells transplanted from his own nose. Details of the Russian operation are scant, though, and whether this patient will ever move again, nobody yet knows.
<long pause>
Spider silk is tougher than Kevlar and, weight for weight, stronger than steel, yet decades of attempts to make it in the lab have fallen short. Researchers led by Gregory Holland at San Diego State University have now found a missing step: inside the spider, liquid silk proteins first gather into semi-liquid droplets before they harden into thread. If engineers can copy that step, a material spiders have refined over hundreds of millions of years could finally be spun in a factory.

In technology. <short pause>
China has created its hundredth central state enterprise, the National Data Group, and ranked it twenty-eighth on the list, ahead of the state electronics giant China Electronics. Since twenty twenty, Beijing has formally treated data as a factor of production, alongside land, labour, capital and technology, and now the National Data Administration writes the rules while this new group, owned by the State Council with Xiong'an and the telecom carriers, runs the business. Mainland tech press reads the ranking as data outranking hardware... and how the centre and the provinces will share control of it is still being worked out.
<long pause>
In Melbourne, the producer Fremantle plans to use AI to scan nine thousand three hundred episodes of the soap opera Neighbours and cut them into short dramas for phones, starting with footage of a young Margot Robbie. The software, from the American firm RoseBerry Media, maps storylines across four decades of tape, and Fremantle says it creates no new scenes or voices. Yet the actors signed their contracts long before anyone imagined this, and whether old consent covers new products is a question every performer's archive now faces.
