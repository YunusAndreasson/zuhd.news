<long pause>
Two Italian technicians are dead in Kuwait... killed, Kuwaiti officials say, by debris from an Iranian drone that air defences had just shot down. They were servicing a Skyguard air-defence station, a system built by the German arms maker Rheinmetall, and theirs are the first reported deaths since the United States and Iran resumed exchanging fire. A war between two capitals is now killing people in a third country, and the Gulf states that host American bases are carrying that risk.

In politics. <short pause>
Nine days before polling, Guinea-Bissau's military rulers have pushed the presidential and legislative vote back two years, to November twenty twenty-eight. The army seized power last year promising a one-year transition, and the decree from transitional president Major-General Horta Inta-a now cites a lack of money and materials... the same reasoning juntas in Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Guinea used to extend their own rule. With the three Sahel states already gone from the Economic Community of West African States, it's unclear what leverage the bloc still holds over a fifth.
<long pause>
In Gaza, recorded birth defects rose seventy-five percent between twenty twenty-two and twenty twenty-five, according to the territory's health ministry. It logged one hundred eighty-four cases in twenty twenty-two and three hundred twenty-two last year, a rise that echoes what doctors reported in Fallujah after the American assault on that Iraqi city in two thousand four. With two hundred sixty cases already recorded by this September, obstetrician Ashraf Qeshta of Nasser Medical Complex blames toxic gases, missile remnants, hunger and displacement... and the children born now will carry the consequences long after the bombing stops.
<long pause>
Debris from a destroyed SpaceX Starship has washed ashore at Playa Bagdad in Tamaulipas, Mexican authorities say, and President Claudia Sheinbaum wants talks with American aviation regulators. The Starbase launch site sits just across the Rio Grande, licensed by the US Federal Aviation Administration, which answers to no Mexican authority... even as SpaceX asks to go from twenty-five launches a year to four hundred fifty-six. Mexico's coastline takes the risk, while the licence, the jobs and the revenue stay north of the border.
<long pause>
On Constitution Day, police in Lucknow detained Uttar Pradesh's Congress chief Aradhana Mishra, along with party leaders Rajendra Pal Gautam and Imran Masood, as they marched toward Ambedkar Crossing. The date marks the adoption of a constitution drafted under Bhimrao Ambedkar, the Dalit jurist who wrote equal citizenship into India's founding law. Congress says a revision of the voter rolls has deleted thirteen crore votes, some one hundred thirty million, falling hardest on Dalits and other marginalised groups... and in India's most populous state, its leaders couldn't even march to say so.

On the economy. <short pause>
Food prices in Sudan rose more than fifteen percent in October alone. The statistics bureau puts annual food inflation at fifty-seven percent, in a country where war between the army and the Rapid Support Forces has wrecked farms, markets and supply routes since April twenty twenty-three. Poor families spend most of what they earn on food, economist Wael Fahmi says, so the headline inflation rate of nearly thirty-five percent badly understates what they're living through.
<long pause>
Iraq earned eight point eight billion dollars from crude exports in October, its oil ministry says, and it now wants the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries to raise its quota. Exports climbed to three point six million barrels a day from just two hundred thousand in May, even as traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, where most Iraqi crude must pass, ran a quarter below normal. A member profiting from a regional war is asking to pump more... and the rest of the group must decide whether that's a windfall to share or a precedent to resist.
<long pause>
Core prices in Tokyo rose two point nine percent in November, the fastest pace since July of last year. The capital's figures arrive ahead of the national data, and they've now held above the Bank of Japan's two percent target for three months, pushed by the end of utility subsidies and rising costs for food and hotels. This is a country that spent most of three decades fighting deflation... and a December rate hike, which could lift the yen, now looks harder for the central bank to postpone.
<long pause>
In an Abuja courtroom, a police investigator testified that former central bank governor Godwin Emefiele merely recoloured the naira rather than redesigning it. President Muhammadu Buhari had approved sample notes with scannable security codes, the witness said, but the notes that were printed lacked them and never received fresh approval. That twenty twenty-two policy drained cash from the economy before the twenty twenty-three election, leaving Nigerians queueing at banks for their own money... and this trial will test whether a central banker answers for it.
<long pause>
President Donald Trump has demanded an investigation into his own government's growth figures, after the Bureau of Economic Analysis revised an estimate of American growth up from three point four to four point three percent. He calls the lower first estimate a deliberate error and has offered no evidence, though revisions like this are routine as fuller data on trade, inventories and services arrive. Last year he fired the head of the Bureau of Labor Statistics over a weak jobs revision... and every market and foreign central bank that prices off American data is watching whether those numbers stay independent.

In science. <short pause>
Researchers have reported a flexible solar cell with a certified efficiency of twenty-seven point five eight percent, converting more sunlight than most rigid rooftop silicon panels. The trick is a stiff molecule called Py six, which arranges itself across the rough surface of a copper indium gallium selenide cell so a dense, flawless perovskite layer can grow on top... a material that first appeared in solar research in Japan in two thousand nine, at under four percent efficiency. Light enough for vehicles and roofs that can't carry glass, these cells still face perovskite's old weakness, breaking down in heat and moisture far faster than silicon.

In technology. <short pause>
Nvidia has stopped shipping its advanced AI servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia, after a new US Commerce Department rule required licences for buyers there. Washington sees the region's data-centre boom as a back door, since Chinese firms rent much of that computing power, and the move follows this week's blacklisting of the firm Megaspeed. Billions of dollars of data centres now sit half-built across Southeast Asia, and it's Malaysian and Thai builders, not Beijing or Washington, who carry the cost of a fight that isn't theirs.
<long pause>
China's industry ministry and eight provinces and cities, from Beijing and Shanghai to Guangdong and Hubei, have launched six G trials inside working factories rather than laboratories. The pilots feed a standards process expected to produce the first international six G specifications around twenty twenty-nine, and with five G, it was Huawei's early deployment that gave Chinese engineers a heavy hand in writing the rules. Whoever proves the technology on real production lines first is likely to shape the standard everyone else must build to... ahead of the national rollout Beijing wants by twenty thirty.
<long pause>
Germany's draft artificial intelligence law would let agencies use the software across asylum, visa and residence-permit decisions. The bill allows training on applicants' personal data, automatic monitoring of procedures and matching applicants against existing records, building on the Federal Office for Migration and Refugees' use of dialect-recognition software since twenty seventeen. Experts warn that the people with the least power to object would feed the system its most sensitive data, with too few limits on the authorities using it.
<long pause>
Binance has fully reopened withdrawals four weeks after a hack of six point three four billion dollars froze customer funds, and says it's recovered ninety-eight percent of what was taken. The exchange says attackers used fake job interviews and malware to reach its wallet systems, and the blockchain analysts at TRM Labs blame North Korea, whose hackers took one and a half billion dollars from the exchange Bybit last year. An exchange that called itself fully backed still kept people from their own coins for a month... a reminder that in crypto, whoever holds the keys holds the money.
