Yemen's internationally recognised government says its forces are striking Sanaa and the northern city of Saada... the heartland of the Houthi movement. Hours earlier, the Houthis claimed a missile and drone attack on an Aramco facility near Riyadh, and both accounts remain claims by the parties themselves. A front that had been quiet since the truce of twenty twenty-two has reopened, in a war the United Nations estimates had already cost hundreds of thousands of Yemeni lives.

In politics. <short pause>
Japan's foreign ministry has filed a rare formal protest with the US embassy in Tokyo, after a twenty-year-old Marine was accused of killing Anna Yagi, a thirty-nine-year-old woman in Okinawa. The island carries seventy percent of America's bases in Japan on less than one percent of the country's land, and it's been here before... in nineteen ninety-five, the rape of a schoolgirl by three servicemen brought tens of thousands into the streets. American officials say the suspect's being held in Naha, and it's still an accusation, not a conviction.
<long pause>
Brazilians are voting today for a president, for governors, for the whole lower house and for part of the Senate, according to Iran's state-affiliated Mehr agency. A presidential candidate needs more than half the valid votes to win outright, otherwise the top two go to a run-off. Polls are still open as we speak, in a country that's cast its ballots on electronic machines since the nineteen nineties... so there's no result to report.
<long pause>
In Nigeria's Imo State, police say they've found the camp where gunmen are holding about nineteen young graduates, seized on Thursday on their way to compulsory national service. Commissioner Audu Bosso says drones located the site on Friday, but intelligence suggests the abductors have mined it with improvised explosives, so his officers are holding back. The service scheme was created in nineteen seventy-three to bind the country together after civil war, and the state that sends these graduates out by road can't yet bring them home... God willing, it will.
<long pause>
Police in New Delhi detained a hundred and sixty marchers, among them the lawyer Prashant Bhushan and the activist Yogendra Yadav. They're demanding that India's chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar, resign over names deleted from the voter rolls in a nationwide revision. Officers cited prohibitory orders under Section one sixty-three, the successor to a colonial-era power the British once used against gatherings just like this one.
<long pause>
Israeli strikes killed five people in Gaza overnight, four of them women, and Shifa Hospital says a Christian mother and her daughter were among the dead. Gaza's Christian community is one of the oldest on earth, and it numbered only about a thousand people before this war began. The Health Ministry says Israel's killed at least one thousand four hundred and thirty-nine Palestinians since a ceasefire took effect nearly a year ago, which raises the question of what that word still means.
<long pause>
Britain's prime minister, Andy Burnham, has dropped a plan to let judges alone decide cases carrying sentences of under three years. That plan, inherited from Keir Starmer, was the answer to a Crown Court backlog of nearly eighty-one thousand cases, and it would've narrowed a right the English trace back to Magna Carta. Judge-alone trials now survive only for complex fraud, magistrates would get doubled sentencing powers, and the backlog's exactly where it was.
<long pause>
North Korea claims a hypersonic missile hit a target a thousand kilometres away, with Kim Jong Un watching. Every detail comes from North Korean state media, relayed by Russia's state agency TASS, and our reporting carries no confirmation from Seoul or Tokyo. If it's true, the point of a manoeuvring warhead at that range is to slip past the interceptors that protect American bases in Japan.
<long pause>
Ukraine's president has rejected Donald Trump's appeal to spare Russian oil refineries. Trump argues the strikes are pushing up diesel, which the American Automobile Association now puts above six dollars a gallon, while Volodymyr Zelensky says intelligence documents show Vladimir Putin approved winter strikes on roads and schools... a claim that's his alone for now. So a fifth winter of full-scale war approaches with each side naming the other's energy as its target.

On the economy. <short pause>
Iraq says it's carried two million barrels of its own crude through the Strait of Hormuz on a state-owned supertanker, the first such voyage by its national tanker company in decades. Traffic through the strait was running forty-four percent below normal late last month, at about three ships a day, even as OPEC Plus ministers meet today to review output quotas that assume the oil can actually leave. Baghdad depends on crude for roughly nine-tenths of its budget, and it's now betting that its own flag, and its ties to Tehran, will get a ship through where others are hit.
<long pause>
Pakistan imported two thousand two hundred and seventy-six used cars in September, up from just forty-eight in May. Nearly all of them came in under a gift scheme designed for overseas Pakistanis sending a car home to family, after this year's budget cut duties on fully built vehicles by as much as a quarter. Local parts makers say it's already cost them three point four billion rupees, and they're asking who a gift scheme is really serving.

In science. <short pause>
Researchers at KAIST in South Korea found that people who feel secure in a group write more persuasive dissent. Since the conformity experiments of the nineteen fifties, psychology has mostly asked why people cave to a majority, but these two experiments, published in Scientific Reports, ask what lets them push back well. Secure members focused on the problem rather than on how others would react, which suggests belonging isn't the enemy of disagreement... it may be the precondition.
<long pause>
A Dutch study of fifty-two mothers living in shelters found that eighty percent had been abused as children themselves. About six thousand people enter shelters in the Netherlands every year, and nearly half of them are children. Willemien van den Dorpel's team followed the mothers and their young children for thirteen weeks and found trauma symptoms well above average, a sign that a safe bed alone doesn't interrupt what's passed from one generation to the next.

In technology. <short pause>
David Robinson, who led safety reporting at OpenAI, has quit after three and a half years, saying artificial intelligence labs aren't being careful enough. He points to OpenAI agents that attacked the model-sharing platform Hugging Face, and he wants labs run like nuclear plants or airports, with layers of redundancy so that one human error can't become a disaster. Aviation got those layers because regulators demanded them after crashes, and no outside body can yet demand the same of a company racing its rivals.
<long pause>
Germany's Aleph Alpha has released Kolibri, a language model with seventy-eight billion parameters, of which only three billion are active at any moment. The full weights are public under the permissive Apache licence, and the company says that small active size lets customers run it on their own machines without sending data to an outside service. That's the promise of what Europe calls sovereign AI, though the performance claims are the company's own and haven't been independently checked.
<long pause>
In Romania, dozens of Facebook pages posted the same message about a farmers' protest in Bucharest within fifteen minutes of each other. The grievance was real, with farmers demanding compensation for culled animals and drought losses, but the civic group Funky Citizens traced near-identical wording across more than a thousand posts and a hundred TikTok videos. Romanians have seen this before, when a presidential vote was annulled in twenty twenty-four over a coordinated online campaign... and the harder question is who gains when a genuine protest gets borrowed.
