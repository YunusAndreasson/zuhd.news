This is your Jumu'ah briefing for the twentieth of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
In Yorito, in northern Honduras, gunmen killed twelve people before dawn on Wednesday... among them Delma Yolanda Olvera, the first woman to lead the Tolupán people of Mina Honda. She died with her husband and her daughter, ten years after the murder of Berta Cáceres showed how Honduras treats those who defend its land. Olvera helped make Yorito free of mining in twenty nineteen, and so far the state has declared a high-risk zone rather than charging anyone.

In politics. <short pause>
Deaths in Colombia's armed conflict rose eighty percent in President Abelardo de la Espriella's first hundred days, according to the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data monitor. Cauca and Norte de Santander bear the worst of it, and clashes involving security forces are up a third. The twenty sixteen peace deal disarmed the largest guerrilla army, but bombing its successors breaks them into smaller groups while the coca and mining money that funds them keeps flowing.
<long pause>
Britain has frozen the assets of eight firms building Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank, and Australia followed with sanctions on three people and four organisations. Fifteen states, including Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Egypt and Jordan, welcomed the move and urged others to do the same. The target is the E1 plan, which would cut the West Bank off from East Jerusalem... and the question now is whether any European capital follows.
<long pause>
The air quality index in Gujranwala hit six hundred and sixty-one on Friday, far beyond the hazardous band, while Lahore and Multan choked too. Since January, Punjab's inspectors have demolished more than fourteen hundred brick kilns and sealed nearly a thousand more. The air still got worse, and every winter the same cold inversion traps the smoke over tens of millions of people, children and outdoor workers first.
<long pause>
Indonesia's President Prabowo Subianto met Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, his fourth visit to China in two years. Beijing invited Jakarta to hold several posts in its new artificial intelligence cooperation body, and the two leaders discussed joint development in the South China Sea. That's delicate ground for a country that's long insisted it isn't a party to Beijing's claims, and whose foreign policy has prized independence from every bloc since nineteen forty-eight.
<long pause>
Satellite images show at least two hits on the rail span of the Kerch Bridge, Ukraine's third strike in four days on Russia's main supply link to Crimea. It's harder to bring down than the road span because it's built for heavier loads, and the damage appears limited to one of its two tracks. The bridge has survived attacks in twenty twenty-two and twenty twenty-three, so the open question is whether Kyiv is now trying for something more lasting.
---
On the economy. <short pause>
Gold is heading for its worst week since March, down almost nine percent, as the dollar sits near a nineteen-month high. Chicago Fed president Austan Goolsbee has left a larger-than-usual rate rise open for December, and he blames data-centre building and consumer spending for overheating the American economy. Markets now price a near-certain hike, and the cost lands abroad too: Mexico's peso has slid past nineteen to the dollar, its weakest in seventeen months.
<long pause>
Japan's core inflation reached two point two percent in October, just under forecasts but strong enough to cement a Bank of Japan hike next month. Tokyo only left negative interest rates behind in twenty twenty-four, after eight years below zero. Board members agree on December but split over how far rates should go, and that doubt is pushing the yen toward a hundred and fifty-nine to the dollar... close to the level where Tokyo has stepped in before.
<long pause>
South Africa's Reserve Bank held its rate at seven and a quarter percent, but three of its seven members voted to hike. Inflation hit five point two percent in October, the highest since May twenty twenty-four, with petrol near thirty rand a litre, up from twenty-one a year ago. Borrowers already carry heavy real rates, while the public health system is short of about fifty critical medicines and some epilepsy patients are suffering seizures without them.
<long pause>
Just two liquefied natural gas tankers crossed the Strait of Hormuz last week, according to Russia's state news agency TASS. That's the lowest count since early September, when Qatar began raising exports to ease shortages. Britain's maritime trade monitor has reported attacks on two tankers in the region, and with almost all of Qatar's gas leaving through that one narrow channel, buyers from Tokyo to Karachi are watching every crossing.

In science. <short pause>
Ethiopia's Fentale volcano released thirty-eight thousand tonnes of methane without erupting, the first confirmed outburst of that size from a volcano. Researchers at Bristol found that a sheet of magma forced its way through the crust and cracked a seal over methane held in hydrocarbon-rich sediments. Methane traps over eighty times more heat than carbon dioxide over twenty years, so scientists now have to ask how many quiet volcanoes along the Afar rift hold the same.
<long pause>
Fossils studied in Rotterdam suggest an orca nearly eight metres long hunted the North Sea more than two million years ago. Its teeth sat in deep sockets reinforced with bone, and its head moved freely, traits of today's orcas that hunt seals and other marine mammals. That would push the origin of mammal-hunting orcas far earlier than biologists believed, though a handful of bones leaves much of the animal's life unknown.
<long pause>
University of Chicago researchers project that heat could kill thirteen thousand three hundred Brazilians this summer. Of Brazil's roughly fifty-five hundred municipalities, only three hundred and eighty-five reported no extreme weather event, and floods hit nearly half of all cities between twenty twenty-one and twenty twenty-four. After the floods that drowned Rio Grande do Sul in twenty twenty-four, a strong El Niño now threatens the opposite extreme, and many of those cities, God willing, will get through it.

In technology. <short pause>
Hackers took customer names, emails and phone numbers from JPMorgan Chase, Visa and Mastercard through a single breached vendor. In the same week the White House fired its national cyber director, Sean Cairncross, after outages hit Microsoft, air traffic control and even the National Security Agency, cancelling more than twelve thousand flights in a single day. The post that coordinates America's cyber defence now has only an acting occupant, while the payment system's three pillars turned out to share one weak door.
<long pause>
Ethereum's developers have pushed their next upgrade, called Glamsterdam, from December to late January at the earliest. It raises the cost of creating a contract almost sixfold, and an estimated fifteen thousand programs could fail mid-transaction. Code on a blockchain can't simply be rewritten, which is why the network has split before over hard choices, and it's a lesson for any platform whose users can't be forced to upgrade.
<long pause>
Germany's federal police reportedly track phones using advertising data, through a tool called Webloc from the American firm Penlink. It follows chosen devices in near real time from location data that apps collect for marketing. This is a country whose highest court made privacy a basic right in nineteen eighty-three, yet the Interior Ministry won't say what legal basis allows any of it.
<long pause>
That's your briefing.
