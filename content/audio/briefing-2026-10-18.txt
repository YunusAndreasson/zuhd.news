This is your briefing for the eighteenth of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Kenya's Ebola outbreak has grown to seven cases... and three of the newest are health workers who treated the country's first patient. All four new cases developed fevers while already in quarantine, and the strain is Bundibugyo, which has no licensed vaccine or treatment. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the same outbreak has passed nine thousand cases, so whether Kenya's clinics can protect their own staff may decide how far it spreads.

In politics. <short pause>
Seventeen people died in a crush at a Durga Puja pandal in Kolkata, and another thirty-five to forty were injured. Police say rain drove a dense crowd to the exit gate, which eyewitnesses call narrow and poorly lit... and India's religious gatherings have seen this before, from Hathras in twenty twenty-four to the Kumbh Mela in twenty twenty-five. Prime Minister Modi has pledged two lakh rupees to each bereaved family, but witnesses are already asking where the police were when it happened.
<long pause>
In the occupied West Bank, settlers kicked American photojournalist Heidi Levine, sixty-four, for about ten minutes and nearly severed her finger. Near Taybeh, masked men also beat a seventy-eight-year-old French mayor, Bernard Baudoux, with iron bars, and even Mike Huckabee, Washington's ambassador and a longtime champion of the settlements, has demanded that Levine's attackers be jailed. Palestinians face this violence every olive harvest and rarely see anyone charged, so the test is whether foreign victims change that, and what it says if it takes them.
<long pause>
A deadly fire has crossed the perimeter fence of Canadian Forces Base Wainwright in Alberta, one of the country's busiest army training bases. High winds carried the blaze out of a training area, the local municipality says, and Defence Minister David McGuinty has confirmed deaths and injuries. He hasn't given a toll or a cause... and until he does, it isn't clear what happened inside the fence.
<long pause>
Yemen's internationally recognised government claims it inflicted eight hundred Houthi casualties in a single day, a figure relayed by Russian state news agency TASS that can't be independently verified. The offensive, called Operation Dawn of Yemen, began on the fifth of October with the aim of retaking Sana'a, the capital the Houthis have held since twenty fourteen. Combatants' tallies in this war have rarely held up, and no independent count yet says what the push is costing civilians on the front.
<long pause>
Three weeks before the US midterms, the Justice Department has warned California's election officials they'll be charged if they count ballots it's flagged as possibly cast by noncitizens, and President Trump has promised them jail. The department sued the state after giving it just forty-eight hours to check the names, but a database match isn't proof, and eligible citizens can be caught in such lists. Threatening prosecution of the very officials who certify a count is new ground, and it's not yet clear whether the courts will let it stand.
<long pause>
Nigeria's Senate has advanced a bill that would end prison terms for attempted suicide. The offence sits in two of the country's criminal laws, inherited from colonial-era codes, and advocates say the threat of prosecution keeps people from seeking care. Under the bill, survivors would get medical assessment, referral and follow-up instead, though it still has to clear the House of Representatives and win the president's signature.
<long pause>
The Organisation of Islamic Cooperation's foreign ministers have opened their fifty-second session in Baghdad, with Gaza and the widening regional war at the top of the agenda. It's a pointed venue: a capital rebuilt after two decades of invasion and civil war is now hosting the Muslim world's main bloc while war runs on across the Gulf. Turkey's Hakan Fidan met Egypt's Badr Abdelatty on the sidelines, but nothing binding is on the table, and the bloc has never had a way to enforce what it declares.

On the economy. <short pause>
Iran has offered a seven-day plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, according to state-run Press TV, while Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi blames the insecurity on the US-Israeli war that began on the twenty-eighth of February. Before the closure, the strait carried eighteen to twenty million barrels of oil a day, said Russia's deputy prime minister Alexander Novak, who warns that emergency stockpiles are nearly exhausted. Brent crude stood at one hundred and nine dollars ninety on the sixteenth of October, up almost five percent in a week... and the plan's full terms haven't been made public.
<long pause>
Cost-of-living protests have spread from France into Italy, where police tear-gassed stone-throwing crowds outside Milan's cathedral and arrested seven people in Bologna. Across France, interior ministry figures put the marchers at five hundred and fifty thousand, with four hundred arrests, and in Limoges fire took two-thirds of the exterior of the town hall, built in eighteen eighty-three. It's the same squeeze on both sides of the Alps, with fuel, food and wages that haven't kept pace, and Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni's condemnation of the violence doesn't answer it.
<long pause>
Pakistan has shut its official trade crossings with Iran at Gabd-Rimdan and Taftan, withdrawing its earlier order with immediate effect and giving no reason. Gabd-Rimdan alone reportedly carried four thousand tonnes of liquefied petroleum gas a day into Balochistan, a province that's long been Pakistan's poorest despite supplying much of the country's gas. Traders warn of shortages as far away as Karachi, and when the official route closes, the informal fuel trade across that border tends to grow.
<long pause>
Petrol stations in Sudan have become campsites as fuel prices skyrocket. On top of that, an October ban on imported fava beans, the base of the country's everyday breakfast, is drawing fierce criticism even from government sources and traders. The army-led government of Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, at war with the Rapid Support Forces since April twenty twenty-three, says it'll tighten fuel allocations and seek new supplies, but hasn't said where they'll come from.

In science. <short pause>
Off Kekova, on Turkey's southern coast, archaeologists have found a wreck they tentatively date to between twenty-seven hundred and twenty-four hundred BC, which would make it the oldest shipwreck known. No timbers survive, so the team dated it from large two-handled storage jars embedded in the rocky slope, putting it some three centuries ahead of the Dokos wreck in Greece. If the date holds, it's direct evidence of cargo moving along this coast nearly fifteen centuries before the famous Uluburun ship sank nearby.
<long pause>
Researchers writing in Nature Communications found that two ordinary gut bacteria, Bacteroides and Escherichia, together suppress Clostridioides difficile across its strains. That infection is a leading cause of hospital illness, usually striking after antibiotics wipe out the gut's normal flora, and patient data showed the ratio between the two bacteria predicted resistance better than standard measures of diversity. It points toward defined bacterial treatments that could one day replace crude faecal transplants... though for now it's a finding, not a therapy.

In technology. <short pause>
Drones targeted a data centre belonging to VK, Russia's largest social network, in a raid on St Petersburg and the Leningrad region where officials say a hundred and twenty-six drones were shot down, according to Russian state news agency TASS. VK says its services stayed up, but the target matters: days earlier, Iran's Mehr agency reported a warning from senior official Mohsen Rezaei that Gulf data centres and undersea cables could be attacked. Data centres were once the quiet background of the internet; now they're on target lists, and nobody has settled how they'll be defended.
<long pause>
A Chinese model called StartLux ranks first of a hundred and seventy-three on a Hugging Face leaderboard for decision models, its maker says, as reported by the tech outlet TMTPost. A decision model is the controller inside an AI agent that routes requests, approves tool calls and scores its own confidence, and StartLux says reasoning only when confidence falls below ninety percent cut one model's reasoning by sixty-five percent. China's tech press frames the agent era as a race for this cheap, controlling layer rather than for the biggest model, and the ranking hasn't yet been checked independently.
