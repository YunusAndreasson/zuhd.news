This is your briefing for Saturday, the seventh of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
UK Maritime Trade Operations counted eleven attacks on merchant ships around the Strait of Hormuz in a single week... and three vessels sunk. On Thursday a projectile struck the empty tanker Velos Sapphire as it sailed for Bahrain, killing a seafarer aboard, and Iran's Mehr news agency says two more tankers are burning after hitting mines. Traffic has crept up to three hundred and twenty-five transits a week, yet that's still sixty-five percent below where it stood before the war.

In politics. <short pause>
A court in Dar es Salaam has dropped the treason charge against Tanzanian opposition leader Tundu Lissu... and jailed him for four years for incitement instead. Lissu has already spent nineteen months behind bars, and across East Africa, from Uganda's Kizza Besigye to Lissu himself, treason trials have become a way to sideline challengers without facing them at the ballot box. The death penalty is off the table now, but President Samia Suluhu Hassan's main rival is still kept off the field.
<long pause>
In Washington, President Trump has named Stephen Miller, the architect of his mass deportation drive, as White House chief of staff from Monday, three days after a midterm defeat. A chief of staff decides who reaches the president and which orders land on his desk, and Miller's the official who set immigration agents a target of three thousand arrests a day. Rather than moderating after the losses, the White House is doubling down, and migrants and Muslim travellers, the people his raids and travel bans have targeted, are likely to feel it first.
<long pause>
Syrian military trainers are now working inside Saudi Arabia, two Syrian sources say, under a defence pact the two countries signed on Wednesday. For more than a decade Riyadh kept Damascus at arm's length and backed the uprising against Bashar al-Assad, yet today Syrian personnel are helping the kingdom as Houthi missiles strike its airports and oil facilities, as they've done since July. What a Syrian state still rebuilding from war gets in return, in Gulf money and political cover, hasn't been made public.
<long pause>
Ukraine, Russia and the United States are set to meet in Abu Dhabi on the tenth of November, with Rustem Umerov leading Kyiv's delegation. The Emirates have already brokered prisoner swaps between the two sides, and now a Gulf state is hosting talks on Europe's largest war since nineteen forty-five... while Europe, which supplies most of Kyiv's support, isn't expected at the table at all. Ukraine's central bank now expects the economy to shrink this year, according to Russian state agency TASS, so how much Kyiv can afford to hold out may be shaped by its ledgers as much as its front lines.
<long pause>
At the Hospital Escuela in Tegucigalpa, Honduras's largest public hospital, more than six thousand patients are waiting for surgery. Forty-nine days of doctors' assemblies left over a thousand elective operations undone, and the government's answer was a five-hundred-million-lempira trust paying private hospitals up to a hundred and ten thousand lempiras an operation. By July it had delivered just four hundred and five procedures... and how the rest of that public money is being spent hasn't been disclosed.
<long pause>
Provisional results from South Africa's local elections show ActionSA, which won no wards in Gauteng in twenty twenty-one, now leading in fifty-one. Its gains run through Soweto, Tembisa and Atteridgeville, townships that carried the struggle against apartheid and gave the African National Congress its most loyal votes for three decades. In Johannesburg alone the congress is falling from eighty-seven wards to forty-four, and the party that ended apartheid is losing the very streets that carried it to power.

On the economy. <short pause>
Rahul Gandhi, India's opposition leader, says eleven of fourteen petrol samples drawn from seven districts in Karnataka broke the twenty percent ethanol cap, with one Indian Oil sample reaching thirty-one point two percent. India's ethanol mandate trims oil imports and pays sugarcane growers, but motorists carry the cost in lost mileage and repair bills, and Gandhi says the higher blends go unlabelled at the pump. These are allegations backed by lab tests... and nobody independently checks what's actually in the tank.
<long pause>
Pakistan has cut fuel prices for a fifth straight time, bringing petrol to three hundred and forty-six rupees a litre, down from four hundred and fifty-eight in early April. Taxes and duties still take a hundred and fourteen rupees of every litre, a levy that's become a pillar of Islamabad's revenue under its International Monetary Fund programme. Refineries are absorbing fifteen billion rupees of that levy for thirty days, and what drivers pay once the month runs out is anyone's guess.
<long pause>
Copper slid to thirteen thousand, seven hundred and seventy-eight dollars a tonne, its lowest since July, as Goldman Sachs warned that forty-five percent of planned American data-centre capacity sits in states likely to restrict it. For Chile, Peru, Zambia and the Democratic Republic of Congo, the metal is the export their budgets lean on, and many of those budgets were built on forecasts of an artificial intelligence boom. Traders have already shipped so much copper ahead of a promised American tariff that ninety percent of warehouse stocks now sit in the United States... metal that may have nowhere to go if that demand falters.
<long pause>
Brazil's long-promised sin tax on tobacco, alcohol and sugary drinks won't cost manufacturers anything yet. Created in the sweeping tax reform of twenty twenty-three, the levy was meant to make these industries pay toward the health costs their products cause, but a provisional measure published Wednesday sets the rates at what the government calls fiscal neutrality. The public health system that treats the diseases is still waiting for its share.
<long pause>
Two veterans remain in the race to succeed Christine Lagarde at the European Central Bank: the Dutch economist Klaas Knot and Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos. Top posts at the bank are traded between capitals like seats in a coalition, and economists say the result hinges on another job entirely... if France takes chief economist, Knot's the likelier president, and if Germany does, de Cos. Whoever wins inherits rising interest rates, stress in French bonds and two departing board members.

In science. <short pause>
Up to a billion years of rock are simply missing from the walls of the Grand Canyon, a gap geologists call the Great Unconformity. Ice ages have long been the favoured explanation, but glaciers alone can't explain why the gap is so stark in North America and faint in other parts of the world. Researchers working from Wollongong in Australia found that in five of six simulations the mantle lifted the continent for hundreds of millions of years, until ice scoured it and Cambrian seas buried the wound... and now the rocks themselves will have to confirm it.
<long pause>
In Toronto, artificial intelligence agents wrote more than two hundred commands for robot arms after testing every one of them in a virtual laboratory first, a study in Nature reports. Setting up an automated lab usually means slow trial and error followed by endless troubleshooting, but here a scientist simply describes the experiment, and the agents build a simulation, test the protocols, then direct the real machines. Whether that opens automated science to poorly funded labs, or widens the gap with those who can afford the robots, remains open.

In technology. <short pause>
BYD's luxury brand Yangwang says its U9X reached five hundred and three kilometres an hour on a closed highway in the Gulf, certified by the International Automobile Federation and Guinness as the fastest production car ever. Thirty years ago BYD was a small battery maker in Shenzhen, and now four electric motors spinning at thirty thousand revolutions a minute on a twelve-hundred-volt platform push more than three thousand horsepower. That speed crown long belonged to European and American petrol engines, so the real question is how quickly this technology reaches ordinary cars.
<long pause>
Clips4Sale has hidden three hundred and ninety-two of one creator's five hundred and six videos, even though she films herself talking alone, and the platform didn't tell her what to change. Britain's new Crime and Policing Act makes possessing or publishing pornography that depicts choking punishable by up to five years in prison, and platforms are purging wholesale to stay clear, much as American sites did after a twenty eighteen law on online sex trafficking. Who decides where the line falls, a court or a nervous compliance team, is still unsettled.
