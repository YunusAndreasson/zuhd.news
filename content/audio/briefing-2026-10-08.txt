The White House has asked the Pentagon to draft options for striking Iran before America's midterm elections on the third of November... that's according to US media reports, and it isn't confirmed. Mediation between Tehran and Washington has reportedly deadlocked this week, and oil jumped on the news while bitcoin slid below eighty-three thousand dollars. For Iranians already living under bombardment, blockade and sanctions, the question is whether a war is now being timed to someone else's vote.

In politics. <short pause>
Maricarmen Abascal, eighty-seven years old, has died in Madrid just weeks after being evicted from the flat she'd lived in for seventy-one years. Her tenants' union says the firm that bought the building in twenty eighteen raised her rent from about five hundred euros to two thousand six hundred and fifty, and her eviction had already set off protests across Spain and a snap election. After the two thousand and eight crash, Spanish courts ordered hundreds of thousands of evictions... and the country's still arguing over whether a home is a right or an asset.
<long pause>
In Cairo, Egypt's public prosecution says one of six detained journalists has confessed that they all belonged to a Muslim Brotherhood media cell. The six ran a fact-checking platform, and they were held for five days without lawyers or family before a prosecutor even saw them. Rights lawyers call the statement an unproven position, and it leaves a hard question hanging: what's a confession worth when it's announced to the public before any court has heard it?
<long pause>
A South African police general has told parliament that some of the guns used by criminals come from the police themselves. It isn't a new wound... a decade ago, a police colonel was jailed for selling some two thousand firearms, meant for destruction, to gangs in the Cape. With nearly thirty-seven murders for every hundred thousand people, the institution charged with disarming the country still can't say how many of its own weapons are missing.
<long pause>
Yemen's Houthis say they've fired eleven missiles at Saudi-led coalition forces just south of Bab el-Mandeb, the narrow gateway to the Red Sea. That claim comes from the Houthi military spokesman, relayed by the Russian state news agency TASS, and no independent outlet has confirmed the casualties he describes. Bab el-Mandeb means the Gate of Grief in Arabic, and if fighting settles on its shore, ships bound for Suez will have two contested straits to think about instead of one.
<long pause>
France's finance minister has told Britain to come back to the European Union, anytime. He was answering Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who's said a second referendum is possible in a future Labour manifesto... which is a signal, not a decision. Returning would need the consent of all twenty-seven members, and the old British opt-outs on the euro and the budget rebate aren't on offer anymore.

On the economy. <short pause>
Only seven ships crossed the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday, the fewest since late July. Crude flowing through the strait fell twenty-seven percent to about ten million barrels a day, the data firm Kpler says, after the war's worst week of tanker attacks. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio insists oil exports are near prewar levels, but that figure counts every pipeline and bypass route... and it's Iraq and the Gulf exporters who can't afford for him to be wrong.
<long pause>
Gold has fallen to its lowest price since early August, even as the war deepens. The metal pays no interest, so when markets put eighty percent odds on another Federal Reserve rate rise in December, holding it simply costs more than fear is worth. Fed officials themselves are split on whether they're fighting an energy shock or stubborn demand, and families from India to Turkey who keep their savings in gold are left watching that argument from afar.
<long pause>
Pakistan's farmers start sowing wheat within weeks, and the government still hasn't told them what price they can expect. Islamabad scrapped its guaranteed support price under its International Monetary Fund programme, and Punjab and Sindh are also bracing for a twenty-two percent shortage of irrigation water this season. Without a price, smallholders may plant less... and a country of two hundred and forty million imports wheat whenever the harvest falls short.
<long pause>
Nigeria's Auditor-General has flagged more than two hundred and forty-five billion naira, roughly a hundred and eighty-five million dollars, in contracts at the National Population Commission. Auditors found no proof that census devices worth over half that sum ever arrived, only handwritten entries on unofficial papers. Africa's most populous nation last counted its people in two thousand and six, so revenue, parliamentary seats and services are still being shared out on numbers nearly twenty years old.
<long pause>
Two unions at Chile's Centinela copper mine have decided to strike after wage talks with Antofagasta Minerals ended without a deal. Chile is the world's largest copper producer, and a strike at its Escondida mine in twenty seventeen lasted forty-four days and shook global prices. Every electric car and power grid needs this metal, which gives the miners in the Atacama Desert more leverage than their numbers suggest.
<long pause>
Twenty-three Indian sailors escaped a burning oil tanker off the Russian port of Sochi, after what Moscow says was a naval drone attack. New Delhi demanded an end to strikes on commercial ships and seafarers, though it pointedly didn't name Ukraine. India is one of the biggest buyers of Russian crude and one of the world's biggest suppliers of ship crews... so its citizens are exposed on both ends of that trade.

In science. <short pause>
A global fund meant to make companies pay for using nature's genetic data has received contributions from just two firms, reportedly totalling six thousand dollars. The Cali Fund, agreed at UN biodiversity talks in Colombia two years ago, invites businesses to give one percent of profits, but doesn't require it. Old rules covered physical samples taken from forests and reefs, while sequenced DNA now sits in open databases, and the communities who guarded those species are still waiting, in sha'Allah, for a share.
<long pause>
Neanderthals shaped wooden spears, trays and a club-like tool, according to researchers working at a rock shelter in Catalonia. Wood rots, which is why stone dominates what we know of them, but minerals at the site preserved imprints of the vanished timber, and thirteen of nearly six hundred objects were confirmed as worked or used. It joins the famous hunting spears found in Germany in suggesting that most of what these people made has simply disappeared.

In technology. <short pause>
A version of ChatGPT built for teenagers failed to point users to crisis help in a quarter of tests, the youth-safety group Common Sense Media reports. Its researchers ran more than four thousand prompts on suicide, self-harm and disordered eating, and they rate the product an unacceptable risk to children. OpenAI launched it only two months ago with its own safety promises, and it's an advocacy group, not a regulator, that's doing the checking.
<long pause>
Washington is offering up to ten million dollars for information on Zhang Yu, whom it accuses of hacking for China's Ministry of State Security. US officials tie him to the Hafnium campaign, which exploited flaws in Microsoft's email servers in twenty twenty-one and, they say, hit more than twelve thousand seven hundred American organisations. Both powers break into each other's networks, and a bounty on a man beyond extradition is mostly a way of saying so in public.
