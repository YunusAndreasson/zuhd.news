This is your briefing for the twenty-second of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Brent crude has climbed past a hundred and ten dollars a barrel... after a Houthi drone struck near Saudi Arabia's Shaybah oilfield, deep in the Empty Quarter. The kingdom's East-West pipeline, its way around a closed Strait of Hormuz, reportedly stopped flowing after an earlier strike on the fifth of October, so even the bypass now carries a war premium. Washington's ordered non-emergency diplomats and their families out of Saudi Arabia, but the kingdom's millions of migrant workers have no evacuation order at all.

In politics. <short pause>
Zelensky's called for freezing the front line as the first step toward a ceasefire, and the Kremlin says it's ready to return to talks by mid-November, possibly in Abu Dhabi. A frozen line concedes occupied land for now without signing it away... the formula that halted the Korean War in nineteen fifty-three, along a border that still hasn't become a peace. Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov says Moscow wants the talks to focus on post-war security, and Russian missiles still struck Kyiv overnight.
<long pause>
Iran has told Pakistan's army chief, Asim Munir, who's shuttling mediators' proposals, that no enriched uranium will leave the country. Under the twenty fifteen nuclear deal, Tehran shipped most of its enriched stockpile to Russia, but this time it offers only to dilute the material at home under international monitors. Senior official Mohsen Rezaei has told Drop Site News that Iran expects a US-Israeli strike before the American midterms, so the general's shuttle is racing a clock.
<long pause>
Qatar, Turkey, Pakistan and Indonesia have signed an accord in Doha to produce weapons together. Between them they're the Muslim world's most populous nation, its only nuclear power, a NATO member and the Gulf's richest state per head, with Turkey bringing the technology and Pakistan and Indonesia the factory floors. Analysts read it as a hedge against dependence on Washington after the US-Iran war, not a break with it... and India's watching closely what the pact means for its neighbour's arms industry.
<long pause>
El Salvador may quietly free about ten thousand prisoners early, after President Nayib Bukele said he's weighing conditional release for detainees forced to work with gangs. For more than four years, the state of exception has jailed tens of thousands without trial, and the government insisted every one of them was a gang member or a helper. A commission now decides behind closed doors who walks out... with no public criteria, no court, and no word of compensation for anyone wrongly held.
<long pause>
Macron's rejected claims by Donald Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu that migrants and Islam are behind France's riots, calling talk of an invasion false. The unrest began with students angry over school funding and rents, yet in the country with Western Europe's largest Muslim minority, outside leaders have recast it as a story about faith. Macron blames AI-generated videos for spreading the claims, and EU regulators are reportedly examining whether a foreign disinformation campaign is at work.
<long pause>
In Brazil, the betting site Blaze operated from the same address as companies tied to Willer Tomaz — a lawyer close to Senator Flávio Bolsonaro who's suing Blaze for a hundred and sixty-three million reais he says he wagered. Federal Police suspect Tomaz used the platform to send money out and recover it, a risk that's grown since Brazil legalised sports betting in twenty eighteen. With election season under way, investigators still have to establish whose money moved through the site, and where it ended up.

On the economy. <short pause>
Forty thousand tonnes of Russian diesel are heading for the United States today. Washington banned Russian oil imports in twenty twenty-two, but American diesel now averages six dollars and thirty-two cents a gallon, nearly seventy-five percent above last year. Ukrainian drones have cut Russia's own diesel exports by three-quarters... so the barrels America's buying are ones Moscow can barely spare.
<long pause>
Tesla's net profit fell forty-three percent to seven hundred and eighty-seven million dollars, and Meta's free cash flow turned negative, at minus two point one billion. Meta's operations brought in nearly thirty-three billion dollars in the quarter while capital spending and leases took almost thirty-five, and Tesla more than doubled its own spending to four point seven billion. Investors ran to US Treasuries, pushing long-term yields to a one-month low even with oil above a hundred and ten... a bet that AI's payoff is further off than promised.
<long pause>
South Africa's cutting its fuel levy until January twenty twenty-seven, lowering petrol and diesel prices as Brent keeps climbing. Opposition parties call Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana's move electioneering before the fourth of November vote, a test for the African National Congress, which lost its majority in twenty twenty-four for the first time since apartheid ended. Relief at the pump costs the treasury revenue it'll need later, and voters will decide within two weeks whether it bought anything.
<long pause>
Japanese visitors to Korea may one day pay with yen stablecoins, as Shinhan Card and units of Japan's SBI study wallet-to-wallet transfers. Japan was among the first major economies to give stablecoins a legal framework, in twenty twenty-three, and Shinhan's card arm already serves about twenty million customers after a stablecoin deal with Visa in August. If the study turns into a service, a card network could soon move money that never touches a bank account.

In science. <short pause>
The World Meteorological Organization forecasts Pacific waters could run three point eight degrees above average by January... an El Niño off the charts. The current record, two point six degrees in twenty fifteen and twenty sixteen, brought drought to Ethiopia and southern Africa, because El Niño shifts winds and rainfall right across the tropics. UN aid chief Tom Fletcher warns fifty million people could face deeper hunger, and whether help reaches them before the rains fail is still an open question.
<long pause>
Smoke advisories in Montreal cut bikeshare trips by just five point eight percent, researchers at McGill University found. They compared twenty-seven advisory days against a model of travel without warnings, a pressing question since twenty twenty-three, when Canada's record wildfire season sent smoke as far as New York. Recreational running, the very activity the warnings target, didn't change measurably... so the warnings themselves may need to change before behaviour does.

In technology. <short pause>
Attackers could forge trusted website certificates after private keys for eight of eMudhra's intermediate certificates were compromised, and the Bengaluru firm issues many of India's digital signatures. In twenty eleven, a hack of the Dutch authority DigiNotar ended with browsers distrusting it and the company going bankrupt. Google, Mozilla and Apple must now weigh a similar call, even as India's IT Ministry insists the impact is limited.
<long pause>
ElevenLabs' new flagship voice model ranks third on Artificial Analysis's voice-reasoning test, behind StepFun at ninety-nine point seven percent and Alibaba at ninety-nine point two. The London firm's model scored ninety-eight point eight, yet answers in just over half a second, nearly three times faster than Alibaba's rival. Chinese labs now lead on reasoning while ElevenLabs leads on speed... and a hands-on test by the Chinese outlet Zhidx found its Chinese still carries a strong foreign accent.
<long pause>
Ghost Shark, the extra-large uncrewed submarine Anduril builds for Australia, has launched an aerial drone from a twelve-tube launcher. The sub surfaces, its hull doors slide open, and the launcher rises, and Anduril says Australia's confirmed payloads including sea mines, supply caches and strike drones. Australia's crewed nuclear submarines under AUKUS are still years away, so robots like this may be what patrols its waters first.
