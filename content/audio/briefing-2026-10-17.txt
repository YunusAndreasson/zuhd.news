This is your briefing for the seventeenth of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Donald Trump has waived five-hundred-percent tariffs on Russian energy... before they ever took effect. Congress passed the law by wide margins to cut the money funding Moscow's war, but a national-interest clause let the president set it aside, and Republicans are among those calling it craven. Meanwhile in Brussels, Volodymyr Zelenskyy asked European leaders for nearly eighty billion dollars more for twenty twenty-seven, as Kyiv's main backer eases the pressure.

In politics. <short pause>
Honduran prosecutors charged nineteen mayors across thirteen departments in a decade, and audits of a hundred and seventy town halls found nearly four hundred and sixty-nine million lempiras lost since twenty fifteen. Of eighteen mayors tracked by the outlet Contracorriente, only six were convicted, and Alexander Ardón, who confessed to fifty-six murders in a New York court, was acquitted at home in August. His victims still have no case in Honduras... and the town hall remains the place where organised crime meets the state.
<long pause>
Brazil cleared three thousand three hundred and thirty-five square kilometres of Amazon forest in a year, the lowest since records began in nineteen eighty-eight. Clearance has fallen every year since twenty twenty-two, down seventy-one percent over four years, after Lula's government tightened monitoring and penalties for illegal logging. Under Jair Bolsonaro, losses ran near eleven thousand six hundred square kilometres a year, and the record lands just as Lula faces Bolsonaro's son Flávio in a runoff.
<long pause>
In Cairo, President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi says Egypt's constitution will stay untouched, while warning in the same breath against a repeat of the twenty eleven uprising. That's the constitution amended in twenty nineteen to stretch his rule to twenty thirty, and three parliamentary sources close to a security agency told Mada Masr he wants to avoid Hosni Mubarak's three decades in power. Other sources doubt the pledge is sincere... and Egyptians won't know which reading is right until the clock runs down.
<long pause>
Nigerian security operatives have reportedly arrested Faisal Maina in Karsana, on the edge of Abuja, six years after he vanished. A court had already convicted him in his absence of laundering fifty-eight point one million naira through a fictitious bank account, and his father Abdulrasheed Maina, who once led a pension reform task force, was convicted of laundering in twenty twenty-one. The arrest rests on a single source for now, with no official confirmation yet.
<long pause>
Pope Leo the Fourteenth, still recovering from lung surgery, phoned Vladimir Putin to ask for a truce around Oleshky, where residents can't safely leave and sometimes can't bury their dead. The town sits on the Russian-held left bank of the Dnipro, which flooded when the Kakhovka dam was destroyed in twenty twenty-three, and it's been on the front line ever since. The Kremlin's readout says Putin blamed Kyiv and welcomed Vatican help... but it doesn't mention a truce.

On the economy. <short pause>
Iran's Revolutionary Guards say a supertanker carrying two million barrels of crude struck a mine after crossing a prohibited area of the Sea of Oman with its tracking switched off. Tehran is enforcing its closure of Hormuz with mines and a lane only it permits, echoing nineteen eighty-eight, when an Iranian mine nearly sank an American frigate and set off a day of naval battle. Nobody has independently confirmed the strike, yet Brent crude already sits near a hundred and ten dollars a barrel, up almost five percent in a week.
<long pause>
Across the peninsula, the Saudi-led coalition says it shot down a missile over Yanbu, the Red Sea terminal where a pipeline lets the kingdom export crude without touching Hormuz. That pipeline was built in the early nineteen eighties for exactly this scenario, during the Iran-Iraq war. Now Yemen's Ansar Allah movement, known as the Houthis, has struck Saudi tankers, oil facilities and Riyadh's airport since July... and with one exit closed and the other under fire, Saudi oil is running out of safe roads.
<long pause>
In Chile, two unions at Antofagasta's Centinela mine in the Atacama reached a preliminary deal after a strike of more than a week, and members now vote on whether to ratify it. Chile mines about a quarter of the world's copper, the metal wired into every grid and electric car, and its price hit fourteen thousand four hundred and seventy dollars a tonne in September, up almost seven percent in three months. In a market that tight, one mine's stoppage moves prices... and the workers know it.
<long pause>
The European Union is building a tool to close its market to rivals in a trade conflict, Turkish state broadcaster TRT World reports, with France and Germany pushing for stronger powers aimed at China. Brussels ran a goods deficit with China of more than three hundred billion euros in twenty twenty-four, and Ursula von der Leyen has made that gap the centre of her case. What's unresolved is whether a bloc built on open markets will actually shut one.

In science. <short pause>
A hundred and thirteen of a hundred and ninety-six countries have now filed new national plans to protect nature, up from fifty-five a year ago, according to an analysis reported by Carbon Brief. Those plans are owed under the Kunming-Montreal framework of twenty twenty-two, which pledged to protect thirty percent of land and sea by twenty thirty, yet only fifteen percent of countries met the October twenty twenty-four deadline. When governments gather in Yerevan for the seventeenth biodiversity summit, the first global review of those targets will rest largely on late homework.
<long pause>
Researchers in Göttingen and Amsterdam found that when political violence is framed as a justified response to an out-group's threat, voters approve more of both the violence and the party behind it. Among supporters of India's governing Bharatiya Janata Party, the effect went further: they approved less when their own party chose a peaceful response. It builds on decades of work, like the political scientist Steven Wilkinson's on how riots track elections, and suggests a justification story doesn't just excuse violence... it punishes restraint.
<long pause>
Google says its Willow quantum chip cut errors about forty-fold in producing magic states, a resource consumed on use that a fault-tolerant machine needs to run Shor's algorithm, the nineteen ninety-four method that could break bitcoin's cryptography. That doesn't make an attack forty times cheaper, since one modelled attack needs about half a million reliable magic states every second. Still, an industry alliance counts six point four million bitcoin, nearly a third of all supply, whose public keys are already exposed, many from the earliest days of the network.

In technology. <short pause>
Beijing has drafted export controls covering twenty-three AI technologies, including model weights, and models over two trillion parameters trained on important data, like Moonshot's Kimi K three, would need a licence to leave China. It's a reversal of roles: the country Washington has spent years cutting off from advanced chips, and which curbed gallium and germanium exports in twenty twenty-three, now believes its labs have something worth guarding. Open-source code that's already public is exempt, as are people using models through apps... so where Beijing draws the line will decide who abroad keeps access to its frontier.
<long pause>
Researchers at Citizen Lab found that online ads were used to target forty-five Al Jazeera devices with spyware from the Israeli firm Candiru, along with government networks in at least twenty-four countries. An ad network used auction data on each target's device and location to win the ad slot, then redirected them to the spyware... no click needed. Washington blacklisted Candiru back in twenty twenty-one, yet the ad system every website and app relies on has now become a delivery channel, and Israel's export licensing still decides who can buy.
