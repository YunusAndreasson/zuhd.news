This is your briefing for the twelfth of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Riyadh's main airport has been hit twice in two days. A Houthi missile killed thirteen people and wounded more than three hundred at King Khalid International... and on Sunday a projectile struck Terminal Four and started a fire, the Saudi-led coalition says. The Houthis have declared Saudi airspace a target, four states are demanding an unconditional halt, and Iran's foreign minister has phoned his Saudi counterpart offering any assistance to start talks between Riyadh and Sanaa.

In politics. <short pause>
An airstrike in Sudan killed seventy-two gold miners on Saturday, according to the Emergency Lawyers, a Sudanese rights group that accuses Egypt's air force of killing civilians at work. Gold has helped finance both sides of Sudan's war since it began in April twenty twenty-three, which makes the mines both a livelihood and a target. The group calls its toll preliminary, and says it's still working to document and verify the dead.
<long pause>
Lebanon's health ministry now counts four thousand three hundred and eighty-seven dead and twelve thousand four hundred and twenty-four wounded since the second of March, the Russian state agency TASS reports. Israeli troops are still operating against Hezbollah in the south, where airstrikes hit the villages of Kfar Tibnit and Tallousah, and at least one civilian died on Sunday. That toll has already passed the roughly four thousand people Lebanon counted killed in the last war, which ended with a ceasefire in November twenty twenty-four.
<long pause>
Pakistan's largest city, Karachi, has banned every gathering of more than five people, an order that covers protests, sit-ins and rallies. The ban rests on Section One Forty-Four of the Code of Criminal Procedure, a colonial-era law from eighteen ninety-eight that lets an administrator, not a court, prohibit assembly by notification. It comes as eleven unelected advisers, two former tax chiefs among them, help run the province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa under emergency and governor's rule.
<long pause>
Residents in Gurgaon, India, say fifty plainclothes police sealed the housing society of the YouTuber Dhruv Rathee, who was detained ahead of a Delhi protest over a hundred and thirty million voter deletions, Drop Site News reports. Police say prohibitory orders were in force, the protest lacked permission, and the detention was preventive. India's chief justice, Surya Kant, has meanwhile told opposition leader Rahul Gandhi to file a petition if he wants the voter-roll revision halted and the chief election commissioner removed.
<long pause>
North Korea's fired ballistic missiles from near Pyongyang, and Seoul says one of them flew more than nine hundred kilometres. The North has now tested missiles more than ten times this year, and this launch came days after South Korea's first hypersonic test on Thursday. UN Security Council resolutions have barred Pyongyang's ballistic launches since two thousand and six... yet each side's test now serves as the other's justification.
<long pause>
South Africa's justice minister, Mmamoloko Kubayi, has counted five prosecutors shot dead in five years. Prosecutors in Nelson Mandela Bay say they still feel unsafe fifteen months after the killing of their colleague Tracy Brown, a hit that court evidence indicates was ordered from inside St Albans Prison. A justice system can't function if the people who bring its cases can be reached from a prison cell, and it isn't yet clear what protection they'll get.
<long pause>
A federal judge in New York, Jennifer Rochon, has ordered a two-week halt to ads promoting Donald Trump, after the Department of Homeland Security spent twenty million dollars of public money on them. Legal experts say the ads break a federal statute barring appropriated money for publicity or propaganda, in a case brought by the watchdog group Common Cause. It took a private lawsuit, not Congress, to stop them weeks before the midterm elections... and the halt is only temporary.
<long pause>
Álvaro Pulido Vargas, a Colombian businessman indicted in twenty nineteen, is in US custody in Miami, accused of profiting from Venezuela's state food programme. Prosecutors allege that one point six billion dollars in contracts were won through bribes and carried inflated prices, with about a hundred and eighty million dollars moving through American banks. Those food boxes were meant for Venezuelans who couldn't afford to eat, and the allegations have yet to be tested in court.

On the economy. <short pause>
Finance ministers and central bankers gather in Bangkok this week for the annual meetings of the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, with developing countries facing four hundred billion dollars in external debt payments this year. The Fund says global public debt is at its highest since the Second World War, and interest already consumes more than ten percent of developing countries' revenue on average. Many of them are party to none of the wars driving up their fuel bills, and what relief Bangkok can offer is still unknown.
<long pause>
Iran says Washington's met only some of its seven conditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz, and its security chief, Mohsen Rezaei, says the strait stays closed to the Americans until all seven are met. Tehran made its proposal at the UN General Assembly, promising to restore passage within seven days, and the American reply came back through Qatar. Meanwhile the Revolutionary Guard says a crude-laden tanker hit a mine and caught fire in its engine room, according to Iranian state media... and it's the crews in the strait who carry that risk.
<long pause>
European officials say the United States is threatening to halt weapons for Ukraine that Europeans have already paid for, and to cut intelligence sharing, unless Kyiv stops striking Russian oil refineries. Donald Trump has announced an energy ceasefire that President Volodymyr Zelensky says was news to him, and Zelensky says Russia hit Kharkiv's power plant at around eight in the evening, after that announcement. In Washington, four Republican senators have joined three Democrats in saying Trump's licence for three hundred thousand tonnes of Russian diesel breaks the ban on Russian energy imports that Congress passed in twenty twenty-two.

In science. <short pause>
The World Wide Fund for Nature's Living Planet Index puts the decline in wildlife at seventy-three percent since nineteen seventy... but that figure's an average decline across monitored populations, not a count of animals lost. A preliminary analysis finds the underlying records skew toward undisturbed habitat such as national parks, while far more wild populations go uncounted altogether. Because the index tracks progress on the UN's biodiversity goals, what it doesn't measure may matter as much as what it does.
<long pause>
A flu vaccine built from four whole, inactivated avian influenza viruses has come through its first human trial, with eighty-five point seven percent of injected volunteers making antibodies against the H seven strain. Seasonal vaccines must be reformulated every year because they target the part of the virus that mutates fastest, and this candidate, given by injection or nasal spray to forty-five volunteers, aims for broader immunity. Side effects were mostly mild, but a phase one trial measures safety and antibody response, not protection... larger trials, in sha'Allah, will show whether it actually prevents illness.
<long pause>
Physicists at Harvard report that isolated quantum systems keep traces of how they began, an effect they call a quantum birthmark. Classical chaos erases a system's starting conditions, so a system that wanders through every available state should forget its origin entirely. Writing in Physical Review X, the team finds that even scrambled states revisit their own history more often than expected, which raises the question of how much any quantum system truly forgets.

In technology. <short pause>
A Chinese startup that owns no data centres, Infinigence AI, has raised two point three billion yuan, which the tech outlet Zhidx calls the country's largest AI infrastructure round. The company sells software that schedules work across mixed chips, and says it ran a training job in twenty twenty-four on four Chinese and two foreign processors... a patchwork that export controls have made a necessity in China. Separately, the Fudan University spin-out MOSI raised more than a billion yuan for real-time AI in glasses, cars and robots, though Zhidx notes its commercial use is still unproven.
