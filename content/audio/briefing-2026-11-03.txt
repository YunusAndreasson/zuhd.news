This is your briefing for the third of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Five blasts, in rapid succession, hit Iran's Qeshm Island... which guards the Strait of Hormuz. American officials call the strikes defensive, part of weeks of unannounced raids on Iran's ability to attack shipping, and they came a day after President Trump said no when asked about attacking Iran after the midterms. Tehran had just shortened its plan to reopen the strait from seven days to two, and a Qatari delegation is still heading there to see what's left to mediate.

In politics. <short pause>
Twenty-four people died at a school in Komyshuvakha, in Ukraine's Zaporizhzhia region, where residents came each week to collect aid, and President Zelenskyy says Russia dropped nine more bombs on the shelter after the first. Among the dead are a police officer and two rescuers, killed by the kind of follow-up strike on first responders that rights groups documented through years of war in Syria. Zelenskyy now says Ukrainian intelligence shows Moscow preparing a massive attack on the energy grid, timed to the American vote, while three-way talks in Abu Dhabi are still planned for later this month.
<long pause>
In Yemen, the Houthi-run health ministry says Saudi airstrikes killed seventeen civilians and wounded fifty-nine more in a single day, most of them children, according to Iran's state-run Mehr News Agency. The Houthis' military spokesman, Yahya Saree, counted one hundred and nine airstrikes in twenty-four hours, part of an escalation since October that's already displaced around two hundred and eighty thousand people. Coverage of this war tends to follow Houthi attacks on Saudi airports... and the cost on the other side of the border rarely gets counted.
<long pause>
Israeli strikes killed at least five Palestinians in Gaza on Monday, and the killing of five police officers has brought the police death toll since the ceasefire to seventy-five. October brought more injuries in Gaza than any month since the truce began, while Israeli attacks across the occupied West Bank have risen fifty-three percent since September. Targeting police hollows out the civil order that any ceasefire depends on, and it leaves Gazans asking what the word ceasefire has actually meant for them.
<long pause>
Former army chief Gadi Eisenkot could gather sixty-seven of the Knesset's one hundred and twenty recommendations to form Israel's next government... but nineteen of them would come from Arab parties. Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc won fifty-three seats and the opposition forty-eight, so neither reaches sixty-one, and the Joint List says it won't join an Eisenkot government. Only once, in twenty twenty-one, has an Arab party sat in an Israeli coalition, and it's unclear whether Eisenkot can govern on the votes of Palestinian citizens without giving them a seat at the table.
<long pause>
Americans vote today for control of Congress, with President Trump's approval at thirty-two percent and diesel having hit a record six dollars and fifty-two cents a gallon in September, pushed up by the war on Iran. The president's party has lost House seats in most midterms since the Second World War, and Republicans hold the chamber by just two hundred and eighteen to two hundred and fourteen, while Democrats need a net gain of four Senate seats. Whoever wins tonight decides who holds war powers over the Iran campaign for the last two years of this presidency.

On the economy. <short pause>
Brent crude fell more than eight and a half percent to a hundred and three dollars and seventy-three cents, its first dip below a hundred and four since the fifteenth of October, as talk of a deal over Hormuz spread. The strait carries about a fifth of the world's oil, which is why a single sentence from Washington or Tehran can move the price this far in a day. Reports of the strikes on Qeshm then sent it climbing back toward a hundred and five... a sign the market's pricing hope, not peace.
<long pause>
In Karachi, wheat's fallen ten rupees a kilo since the first imported cargo, fifty-one thousand tonnes, berthed on the twenty-eighth of October, yet a chapati still costs twenty rupees. A tandoor owner says cooking gas at up to five hundred and fifty rupees a kilo wipes out the saving from cheaper flour, and wholesaler Rauf Ibrahim says the lowest-paid workers already spend almost everything they earn on food. Somewhere between the port and the bakery, someone's keeping the margin... and it isn't the family buying bread.
<long pause>
Chinese brands now supply eighty-eight percent of Argentina's electric cars, and Chinese registrations have risen fivefold in nine months to almost forty thousand. President Javier Milei's January twenty twenty-five decree cut import duties on hybrids and electric cars from thirty-five percent to zero, a sharp break with the protected car industry Argentina built over the second half of the twentieth century. Buyers are getting cheaper, cleaner cars, while workers at the country's own plants wait to learn whether they're the price.
<long pause>
Bank lending in Nigeria equals just under thirteen percent of national output, against about twenty percent across the region. Fewer than one in ten properties has a proper title, says Armstrong Takang of Ministry of Finance Incorporated, so most homes can't be pledged as collateral, which the economist Hernando de Soto famously called dead capital. Millions of Nigerians own the very asset that could fund a business... and still can't borrow a naira against it.

In science. <short pause>
Two UN reports find that today's warming, about one point three seven degrees, is already unsafe for the Earth's ice, and that the safe level sits near one degree or below. If warming overshoots to two point eight degrees, seas could rise five centimetres a year by twenty-one fifty, more than ten times today's pace, and that ice includes the Himalayan glaciers feeding the Indus, which Pakistan's farms and cities drink from. A lead author says getting back to one and a half degrees by twenty-one hundred remains possible, a claim negotiators will test at the COP thirty-one summit in Antalya.
<long pause>
A generic version of lenacapavir, the twice-yearly HIV prevention injection, is expected to cost forty dollars a year instead of more than twenty-eight thousand. India's Doctor Reddy's Laboratories will make it under a deal brokered by Unitaid, opening supply to one hundred and twenty low- and middle-income countries, much as Indian generics broke the price of AIDS drugs in the early two thousands. The price barrier has fallen, and the open question now is whether clinics in the countries that carry the epidemic can deliver it at scale.
<long pause>
Researchers in Liverpool and Oxford have found that lysosomes, long seen as the cell's recycling centres, also tell the heart's natural pacemaker to speed up. Christian de Duve discovered these structures in the nineteen fifties, work that later won him a Nobel Prize, and for most of the decades since they've been treated as little more than waste disposal. If they really act as signalling hubs in heart cells, they could become a new target for rhythm disorders, though that's a long road from the lab to the clinic.

In technology. <short pause>
Britain's National Cyber Security Centre warns of an unprecedented rise in cyberattacks in the coming months, and its chief executive, Richard Horne, says AI is letting attackers exploit long-tolerated weaknesses at a scale once unimaginable. For decades, defenders relied on flaws being slow and costly to find, and last month the Linux kernel project alone patched more than a thousand of them. Whether the same tools can fix weaknesses faster than they expose them is the race that'll define the year ahead.
<long pause>
ByteDance's ad platform, Ocean Engine, will end the rebates it pays thousands of Douyin advertising agencies from the first of January, twenty twenty-seven. Agencies used that rebate as their sales commission, their source of credit and their pricing tool, and it'll be replaced with lower list prices and ranking-based incentives the platform can rewrite every year. It follows a similar move by Google, and it shows where AI-driven ad buying leaves power... with whoever owns the algorithm.
<long pause>
Apple expects its gross margin to slip to between forty-five and forty-six percent, down from nearly forty-eight, because the AI build-out is bidding up the memory chips inside every phone. It's already raised iPhone seventeen prices by a hundred dollars in the US and seventeen thousand rupees in India, the market and manufacturing base behind chief executive John Ternus's record first quarter. Finance chief Kevan Parekh says costs will keep rising into next year, so the bill for the world's data centres is landing in ordinary pockets.
