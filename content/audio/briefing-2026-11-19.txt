Debris from a Houthi missile has fallen on Riyadh... and on the ground, Houthi forces are advancing on Aden, the temporary capital of Yemen's recognised government. Saudi Arabia's coalition reports material damage in its own capital and says it's struck sixty Houthi targets, while Yemeni government forces have abandoned Dhubab airport near the Bab el-Mandeb strait. If Aden falls, the government loses its last major city, and the Houthis would stand over one of the world's busiest shipping lanes.

In politics. <short pause>
Israeli tanks pushed deeper into central Gaza as the body overseeing the truce warned it's close to collapse. The Board of Peace, chaired by Donald Trump, blamed both Israel and Hamas in a twelve-page report, and medics say three Palestinians were killed, one in an airstrike near the raid and two more in Deir al-Balah and Gaza City. Hamas says the board is protecting Israel... and Gazans are left asking who, if anyone, will enforce the ceasefire they were promised.
<long pause>
Bahrain has now faced four Iranian attacks in six days, and Kuwait's seen strikes on an airbase used by American forces. It began when Iran hit a US radar in Bahrain, and Washington answered on Tuesday by bombing a missile site on Qeshm Island, at the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz. Gulf states that host US bases are learning that each American strike brings fire onto their own soil, while traffic through Hormuz runs forty percent below normal.
<long pause>
Nigeria's army has admitted that talks with gunmen in Kwara State freed ten hostages. For years the military insisted it doesn't negotiate with armed groups, and it changed its account only after videos of soldiers mingling with the men went viral. Kidnapping for ransom has spread south from the northwest into the country's centre, and villagers there have no say in the local bargains struck with the men who raid them.
<long pause>
France's governing centre is collapsing in the polls. The Ifop polling firm puts Marine Le Pen's far right at up to thirty-six percent and Jean-Luc Mélenchon's hard left at up to nineteen and a half, while the camp that's ruled since twenty seventeen can't break eleven. Édouard Philippe refuses a joint primary and wants the right and centre united behind him... but a Le Pen–Mélenchon runoff would put the euro zone's second-largest economy, its debt and its Ukraine policy in the hands of the two extremes.
<long pause>
Ten months after American forces seized Nicolás Maduro, US Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth has signed a military pact in Caracas. Venezuela's government under acting president Delcy Rodríguez gets joint training and intelligence sharing with a country that was its declared enemy until this year. The elections promised after Maduro's removal still have no date, and Venezuelans are watching sovereignty traded before they've been asked a single question at the ballot box.
<long pause>
El Salvador's helicopters have fired missiles at another gang stronghold in Port-au-Prince, President Nayib Bukele says. The United Nations recorded four thousand five hundred and ninety-five killings in Haiti in the first ten months of the year, and Bukele has now sent more than two thousand Salvadoran troops. It's a Latin American government exporting its own hardline security model... and Haitians, who've seen foreign forces come and go since nineteen fifteen, have little control over how it ends.
<long pause>
Austria, Denmark, Germany, Greece and the Netherlands plan to send rejected asylum seekers to Rwanda. European money would build and run deportation centres in Kigali from twenty twenty-seven. Britain's own Rwanda scheme was struck down by its Supreme Court in twenty twenty-three and scrapped the next year, so this new plan will have to survive the same legal questions about whether Rwanda is safe.

On the economy. <short pause>
Minutes from the Federal Reserve's October meeting show almost all officials now expect to raise interest rates again. Prices are climbing faster than three percent a year, pushed by the oil shock, and two governors already dissented last month in favour of a quarter-point hike. When American rates rise, governments and companies from Lagos to Jakarta that borrowed in dollars pay more... and they'll get no vote on the decision.
<long pause>
China now settles nearly thirty percent of its goods trade in its own currency, the People's Bank of China says. In trade with Russia, the yuan's share hit sixty-two percent in June, up from just seventeen, after Western sanctions and the freezing of Russian reserves in twenty twenty-two made dollar payments a risk. Countries selling to China under American sanctions now have a way around the dollar, though exporters pay a price as the yuan's risen four point six percent this year.
<long pause>
Qatar's economy will shrink by more than fourteen percent this year, the International Monetary Fund says. The war with Iran has disrupted production and exports of liquefied natural gas, the fuel that built one of the world's richest states per person. The fund says Doha's reserves can absorb the shock and expects growth to resume in twenty twenty-seven, in sha'Allah... but that depends on a war no one in Doha controls.
<long pause>
That same war has soured Pakistan's mango trade. Exports to the Middle East fell from thirty-six thousand tonnes to fourteen and a half thousand, earnings dropped from a hundred million dollars to sixty million, and freight costs jumped from around a thousand dollars a container to eight thousand. Growers in Punjab and Sindh can't hold fruit that rots in days, so every week of near-closure in Hormuz lands directly on their farms.
<long pause>
On South Africa's west coast, small-scale fishers are waiting for a High Court ruling on TotalEnergies' plan to drill a well a hundred and eighty-eight kilometres offshore. The Aukotowa fishing co-operative argues the French company's environmental assessment covered fishers in only one stretch of coast. In twenty twenty-two, a court halted Shell's seismic survey off the Wild Coast for failing to consult communities... and this case will show whether that precedent holds.

In science. <short pause>
In Liberia's Nimba County, the Beoyoolar clinic's only midwife has never been trained to spot obstetric fistula. The injury comes from obstructed labour, which cuts off blood to the tissue and leaves women leaking urine or faeces, and Liberia's health ministry estimates more than a hundred new cases a year, mostly among young women. Fistula all but vanished in wealthy countries once caesarean sections became routine, so its survival here is a measure of who gets emergency care, not of medicine's limits.

In technology. <short pause>
Google's released Gemini Neon, its fourth Gemini four model in just seven weeks. It ranks third on the Artificial Analysis index, behind Anthropic's Claude Fable and OpenAI's latest model, and costs three dollars per million input tokens and fifteen per million output. Frontier capability is getting cheaper faster than labs can tell their products apart, and Google's opened a cyber variant only to vetted defenders, a sign of how dangerous these tools are becoming.
<long pause>
Rockstar Games fired thirty-one British staff who were organising a union, ahead of the release of Grand Theft Auto six. The Independent Workers' Union of Great Britain called it the most unscrupulous union busting the games industry has seen. Crunch, weeks of forced extra hours before a launch, remains common practice... and whether game workers can bargain over it may be decided in a British employment tribunal.
