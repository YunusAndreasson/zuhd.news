This is your briefing for the thirtieth of September, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
One thousand four hundred and seventy-six people sailed from the Malaysian port of Lumut on navy ships this week... bound for Myanmar. They're the first of about five thousand that Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim's government plans to send back, and neither government will say how many are Rohingya. Malaysia never signed the Refugee Convention, and the UN refugee agency says it had no role. So no one independent is checking who's being returned to a country whose military still bombs its own markets from the air.

In politics. <short pause>
The US Supreme Court has let the Trump administration resume deporting migrants to countries where they have no family, no language and no legal status. An unsigned emergency order, with no explanation given, suspended a judge's rule that migrants must first get a chance to object. That restarts a programme that's already sent an estimated twenty-five thousand people to more than two dozen countries. On this emergency docket, the policy is settled in practice long before the justices rule on whether it's lawful.
<long pause>
South Korea's demanding an apology after landmines wounded three of its soldiers inside the Demilitarized Zone, the strip that's divided the peninsula since the armistice of nineteen fifty-three. The UN Command says its investigators found an active North Korean anti-personnel mine at the site... yet Pyongyang calls the whole account a farce. With the hotlines between the two Koreas cut, there's no channel left to establish what happened, and Seoul has promised countermeasures in stages.
<long pause>
Across France, unions say three hundred thousand public workers went on strike as police detained four hundred and forty people at school protests. What began as a student movement has widened into a public-sector revolt. The twenty twenty-seven budget freezes state salaries for a fourth straight year to save two billion euros, and unions say earlier freezes have already cost workers a double-digit share of their purchasing power. President Macron's government has no parliamentary majority to fall back on, and that leaves both sides waiting to see who gives way first.
<long pause>
In Peshawar, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa's chief minister, Sohail Afridi, says he'll march on Islamabad on the fourth of October. That's despite the federal government threatening to remove his elected provincial government. Federal adviser Rana Sanaullah says the centre can impose governor's rule or an emergency, and Pakistan's federal governments have used those constitutional tools against provincial rivals before. Imran Khan's party, Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf, is now staking its stronghold on the streets... and an elected provincial leader is leading a march on his own capital.
<long pause>
Off the Horn of Africa, Puntland's own maritime police, not a foreign naval task force, have retaken two hijacked oil tankers. Five crew members of one of them, the Honour Twenty-Five, died, and the force blames the pirates. Officials say the pirates were moving fuel ashore in small boats for sale on the black market. Piracy off Somalia peaked more than a decade ago before international navies pushed it back, but warships are now drawn toward the Gulf, and a semi-autonomous region is filling that gap on its own.
<long pause>
A court in Rome has sentenced three Egyptian security officers to ten years each for the twenty sixteen kidnapping of Giulio Regeni. He was a Cambridge researcher whose tortured body was found beside a highway outside Cairo. They were tried in absentia, and Major Magdy Sherif escaped the murder conspiracy charge for which prosecutors had sought a life sentence. Amnesty International in Italy says the verdict scratches the wall of impunity Egypt has built... but a sentence that no one will enforce tests how far a European court can reach.
<long pause>
Morocco's king has named El Mansouri prime minister. She's only the second woman to lead a government in the Arab world, after Tunisia's Najla Bouden in twenty twenty-one. Her Authenticity and Modernity Party won ninety-seven seats, but it still needs a coalition partner to govern. The constitution obliges the king to choose the premier from the leading party, yet he keeps control of security and religion, so how much power she'll actually hold remains the open question.

On the economy. <short pause>
Saudi Arabia has resumed crude loadings at Yanbu on the Red Sea after an eleven-day shutdown of the pipeline that feeds the port. Drone attacks that Riyadh blames on the Houthis closed the line on the eleventh of September. It's the route that carries Saudi oil around the Strait of Hormuz, where the war with Iran has throttled shipments. The East-West pipeline was built in the nineteen eighties, during the tanker war between Iran and Iraq, as the kingdom's escape hatch... and now it's become a target too.
<long pause>
On Kenya's coast at Lamu, Africa's richest man, Aliko Dangote, is offering East African governments thirty percent of a sixteen billion dollar refinery. His Lagos plant turned his home country from a fuel importer into an exporter. East Africa, by contrast, still buys almost all its refined fuel through Gulf traders, a dependence exposed this year when price surges set off deadly protests in Kenya. The plant is due in twenty thirty, in sha'Allah. If it's built, the margin on twenty to thirty million tonnes of regional demand a year would finally stay on the continent.
<long pause>
Australia's annual inflation rose to four percent in August, up from three and a half, even though the Reserve Bank has raised rates a full percentage point this year to four point six percent. Fuel costs jumped nearly fifteen percent in a single month as oil climbed and government tax relief came to an end. Rate hikes cool spending at home, but they can't reach an oil shock that comes from abroad... so mortgage holders are paying more to fight inflation their central bank can't touch.
<long pause>
In Durban, South Africa's main container terminal has handled thirty percent less cargo since a software switch in mid-August. Freight forwarders say the changeover to the new system was planned to take twelve hours and ran on for more than a month, at a terminal that handles over sixty percent of the country's containers. Transnet, the state logistics company, has spent years fighting a reputation for congestion, and exporters are now asking who'll pay for the backlog.

In science. <short pause>
A survey published in Nature finds that nearly nine in ten British parents lean towards giving their newborns an antibody against respiratory syncytial virus, the infection known as RSV. Unlike a vaccine, the antibody gives babies ready-made protection for a whole season, but only fifty-four percent of parents are firm in that intent. RSV remains one of the leading reasons infants end up in hospital, and whether a new immunisation programme works may depend on the gap between leaning towards it and deciding.
<long pause>
Shredding lithium-ion batteries for recycling can release carbon nanotubes into the air above the recommended exposure limit, a new study has found. Manufacturers add nanotubes to many batteries to boost performance, and in laboratory studies, long thin fibres like these have been compared with asbestos. As the world's pile of spent batteries grows, the workers who break these cells apart face a hazard that safety rules haven't caught up with.

In technology. <short pause>
Ahead of Nigeria's twenty twenty-seven general election, the Abuja analytics firm Mundx says its artificial intelligence tallied results from three thousand seven hundred and fifty-four of the three thousand seven hundred and sixty-three polling units in Osun State's latest vote. The Independent National Electoral Commission posts handwritten result sheets as photographs. Mundx's software reads and checks each one, then links every total back to its source sheet so anyone can verify it. Rigging used to happen in transit, between the polling unit and the collation centre... and digitisation moves the weak points to uploads, servers and code.
<long pause>
Over Ukraine, seventy-nine of the hundred and sixty-five drones Russia launched in a single night were jet-powered. A teardown of a captured Geran Four found it can fly up to eight kilometres high, twice the altitude of the propeller-driven Shaheds that filled earlier waves. Ukraine built much of its air defence around machine guns and cheap interceptors, and faster, higher drones slip past them. That could force it to spend scarce missiles on targets that cost a fraction as much.
