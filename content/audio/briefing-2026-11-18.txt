This is your briefing for the eighteenth of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Russian strikes hit two bridges and the subway in Kyiv... partly cutting the city's water and power. Ukraine answered by striking the Kerch Bridge, the only link between occupied Crimea and southern Russia, and the peninsula's Moscow-installed governor says a drone attack killed at least two people there. It comes as Germany and Britain publicly rejected a senior Pentagon official's claim that Ukraine's deep strikes have failed, opening a split among Kyiv's backers.

In politics. <short pause>
Britain froze the assets of eight Israeli companies that build settlements in the occupied West Bank, among them Har Hevron Development, Hemdat Shomron and SAG Construction, and sanctioned Tzav Nine, a settler group that blocks food, water and fuel trucks bound for Gaza. Until now, Western sanctions mostly targeted individual violent settlers, even after the International Court of Justice ruled in twenty twenty-four that the occupation itself is unlawful. The freeze reaches only British assets and business... so its bite depends on whether other capitals follow.
<long pause>
In Belgrade, the Student List now holds a hundred and forty of the two hundred and fifty seats in Serbia's new parliament, built on two point three million votes. The movement grew out of protests over the Novi Sad railway station canopy collapse in November twenty twenty-four, which killed sixteen people. President Aleksandar Vučić's Progressive Party, in power since twenty twelve, falls to a hundred and four seats... and the first test of the new majority is the speaker lawmakers elect.
<long pause>
The UN mission in Western Sahara expired on the thirty-first of October, after Russia vetoed an American draft that dropped the promised Sahrawi referendum, and Moscow's own one-year rollover then failed to win support. Since nineteen ninety-one, the mission has monitored the ceasefire between Morocco and the Polisario Front, but the vote on self-determination it was created to hold never took place. With the monitors gone, the Sahrawi people have lost the last international body charged with asking them what they want.
<long pause>
Saudi-led coalition strikes killed at least nine people in Yemen, Russian state news agency TASS reported, seven of them at a foam-rubber factory in northern Hodeidah, according to the Houthi health ministry. The coalition entered Yemen's war in March twenty fifteen, and Hodeidah's port remains the main gateway for food and fuel into the country's north. Another strike killed two people in Saada's Kitaf district... and no independent account of either attack has yet emerged.
<long pause>
In Mocoa, in Colombia's Putumayo department, a copper company sent the mayor's office a letter linking opponents of its mine to armed groups. Libero Cobre, a subsidiary of Canada's Copper Giant Resources, wrote it in twenty twenty-five, and Global Witness says it saw no evidence for the claim and calls it part of a campaign to silence critics. Colombia has for years ranked among the deadliest countries in the world for land and environmental defenders... so being named beside armed groups there is no small accusation.

On the economy. <short pause>
In Washington, Federal Reserve governor Lisa Cook has refused to appear before a White House panel weighing her removal, which may now recommend firing her without her. The law lets a president dismiss a Fed governor only for cause, and no president has removed one in the central bank's more than hundred-year history. Kevin Hassett, Andrea Lucas and Keith Sonderling will weigh mortgage-fraud allegations from twenty twenty-one that Cook denies... and their verdict tests who sets the price of money worldwide.
<long pause>
A thirty-five point nine million dollar transmission contract at Panama's state grid company, Etesa, now costs a hundred and fifty-four point six million. Three addenda, all approved under President José Raúl Mulino's administration and endorsed by the comptroller, raised the price by three hundred and thirty-one percent, the largest of them ninety-five million dollars this May. Panama's public works sat at the centre of the Odebrecht bribery scandal that swept Latin America in the twenty-tens, and Honduran outlet Contracorriente's reporting now asks who answers for this one.
<long pause>
In Bacolod, more than two hundred young protesters rallied against a proposed copper mine in Hinoba-an that regulators say they have no record of. They tie the project to Pax Silica, Washington's initiative to secure critical minerals away from China, and activists say a firm called Cooper Metals is after the Don Jose deposit. The Philippines still remembers the Marcopper disaster of nineteen ninety-six, when mine tailings poured into Marinduque's Boac River... and a mine nobody admits to is its own warning.
<long pause>
Pakistan's power distributors want twenty-four billion rupees more from consumers, filed with the National Electric Power Regulatory Authority. Eleven companies claim thirty-nine billion in capacity, maintenance and loss costs, and industrial users say the adjustment adds about one rupee thirty a unit for three months. Capacity payments, owed to private power producers whether or not their electricity is used, have weighed on Pakistani bills since the nineteen nineties... and there's little room left to absorb another rise.

In science. <short pause>
Cyclone Akvan is forecast to cross the Andhra Pradesh coast near Nellore on the twentieth of November, with sustained winds of up to a hundred and thirty kilometres an hour and gusts to a hundred and forty-five. It's the third Bay of Bengal cyclone in under a month, after Arnab and Murasu, in a basin that produced the nineteen seventy Bhola cyclone, among the deadliest storms ever recorded. India's weather department forecasts more than twenty-one centimetres of rain for the north coast... on communities that haven't recovered from the last two storms.
<long pause>
Researchers at Stanford found that the people who confided most in AI companions felt the worst... and the effect was strongest among those with small social networks offline. Diyi Yang's team analysed four hundred and sixty thousand Character AI messages donated by two hundred and forty-four users, publishing the results in Nature Human Behaviour. It echoes a worry first raised about social media: an always-available companion can displace the human contact the lonely most lack, while the business model rewards more engagement.
<long pause>
A team led by Ke Zhang in Nanjing tracked roughly two thousand lakes since the year two thousand, and found them increasingly moving in step. Heatwaves, droughts and urban development push them the same way, a pattern the study confirmed in sediment records from a hundred and nineteen lakes. Ecologists see the diversity among lakes as a buffer against local shocks, much like a varied portfolio... and when it's gone, algal blooms could strike whole regions at once.

In technology. <short pause>
Nvidia doubled its quarterly profit to sixty-five point three billion dollars in its first results since Jensen Huang's departure, presented by interim chief executive Colette Kress. Revenue reached a hundred and fifteen point eight billion against a hundred and nine billion expected, and the company guided a hundred and thirty billion for the next quarter. That forecast excludes data-centre sales to China, where a spy inquiry and new US export rules loom... so the question is how much of the boom survives Washington's next move.
<long pause>
In Shenzhen, Huawei unveils a new AI-focused phone brand, OSTA, on the twentieth of November, its first handset reportedly running the Kirin ninety thirty S chip. Huawei was cut off from Google's services in twenty nineteen and from TSMC's foundries a year later, yet its Mate Sixty surprised the industry in twenty twenty-three with an advanced chip made in China. Leaked specs show a seven thousand milliamp-hour battery at a starting price near four thousand yuan... and what's still unknown is how far the new chip pushes past the equipment bans.
<long pause>
For four straight weekdays, outages have hit major American systems, from Gmail, Apple Pay and iCloud to thousands of grounded flights and NSA Net, the National Security Agency's classified internal network. The Federal Aviation Administration, United Airlines and Microsoft had all cut staff before their failures, though no evidence links the job losses to the outages. Officials say the failures are unconnected and no cause has been established... while the agency staggers its staff's arrival times and investigates.
