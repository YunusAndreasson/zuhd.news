This is your Jumu'ah briefing for the thirteenth of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Donald Trump says he'd consider military force against Cuba... an island his government has already cut off from the world's oil. Since January, a US blockade has kept fuel from reaching Cuban shores, and the island's power grid has collapsed again and again. Cuba's foreign minister says the Group of Seven is now repeating Washington's falsehoods, and in Havana, people are left to wonder how far this goes.

In politics. <short pause>
Iran's Revolutionary Guards say they've destroyed an American early-warning radar in Bahrain, according to the state news agency Mehr. The Guards call it retaliation for a US air strike on a telecommunications tower in Sirik, on Iran's southern coast, and they threaten a limitless response to any repeat. Bahrain has hosted the US Navy's Fifth Fleet for three decades... and with Washington pulling its negotiator from ceasefire talks, the small kingdom now sits on the front line of a confrontation it didn't start.
<long pause>
In Venezuela, a UN fact-finding mission has documented how former governor Alfredo Díaz died after medical neglect in state custody. Its report rests on two hundred and thirteen confidential interviews with a hundred and twenty-one victims, relatives and witnesses, and it finds at least eighteen officials it implicates in abuses still in power. Washington's new oil dealings with Caracas have changed the diplomacy, but the report suggests they haven't changed what happens inside the cells.
<long pause>
From Tapachula, near Mexico's border with Guatemala, about fifteen hundred migrants set out walking north this week, and Donald Trump answered with a threat to double tariffs on Mexican goods unless Mexico stops them. It echoes twenty nineteen, when a similar tariff threat pushed Mexico to deploy its new National Guard against migrants on its own soil. The cost of this kind of leverage falls on Mexican exporters... and on the people being turned back south.
<long pause>
South Africa's acting intelligence coordinator, Ntandazo Sifolo, has told the Madlanga Commission that four years of national intelligence estimates warned criminal networks were infiltrating the justice system. Each estimate was approved by the National Security Council, yet the infiltration of police and prosecutors persists, an echo of the state capture years laid bare by the Zondo Commission. The question now is who read those warnings, and why nothing changed.
<long pause>
More than forty European leaders met in Dublin as bomb hoaxes hit three thousand seven hundred Italian schools, part of a wave of hybrid attacks that European officials blame on Russia. Europe has carried much of the cost of Ukraine's war, yet it has no seat at the US-brokered peace talks, and its leaders want to name a single envoy to change that. Meanwhile, the European Commission has demanded that SpaceX restore its Starshield service, after Volodymyr Zelenskyy said a speed cap of a hundred and sixty-one kilometres an hour leaves Ukraine's interceptor drones too slow to catch jet-powered Shaheds, a restriction SpaceX denies.
<long pause>
London's Privy Council has struck down Trinidad and Tobago's colonial-era sodomy law, eight years after activist Jason Jones first won his case in the country's High Court. The case turned on the savings clause, a provision written at independence that shields laws inherited from British rule from constitutional challenge. Several other Caribbean states keep both those laws and the same clause... and campaigners there will be reading this ruling closely.

On the economy. <short pause>
India's retail inflation hit six point oh nine percent in October, a two-year high that breaches the Reserve Bank of India's six percent ceiling. Food led the climb, with food inflation jumping to eight point nine four percent from seven point seven four in September, while costlier oil imports, squeezed by the closed Strait of Hormuz, feed through to every kitchen. Economists now expect the central bank to raise rates, making credit dearer for a country of one point four billion people.
<long pause>
Italy's government wants five point nine billion euros from its banks next year, and Carlo Messina, chief of Intesa Sanpaolo, says that could provoke the European Central Bank. Italian banks are among the biggest holders of Italian government bonds, the tie that turned the eurozone debt crisis of twenty eleven into a doom loop between lenders and the state. Squeeze the banks too hard, Messina warns, and Frankfurt could order them to shed those bonds... weakening the very state that taxed them.
<long pause>
Japan's yen has found an unlikely buyer in Washington, which sold five hundred million dollars' worth of euros for yen in its first such intervention since nineteen ninety-eight, during the Asian financial crisis. The New York Federal Reserve bought seventy-nine point four billion yen on the thirty-first of July, alongside Tokyo's own record purchase of fifteen point four trillion yen. The yen still touched a hundred and sixty point oh three to the dollar on Friday... a sign that even two governments acting together can't easily turn a currency.
<long pause>
In Nigeria, presidential candidate Seyi Makinde's campaign says about three hundred and forty million barrels of future crude have reportedly been pledged against eleven point two trillion naira in loans, with the lenders, rates and fees undisclosed. Oil-for-loan deals have a costly record in Africa, where Angola spent years shipping crude to repay Chinese credit while its own budget went short. Fixed repayments under four deals, named Gazelle and Leopard, could cap what Nigeria earns if prices rise, and Makinde's camp wants a public inquiry.
<long pause>
Mercedes-Benz has halted investment in Germany after four months of talks with workers collapsed, and its labour director, Britta Seeger, says plant closures are now an option. The company wants forty-hour weeks without extra pay, against the thirty-five-hour standard that German metalworkers began winning through strikes in the nineteen-eighties. That standard covers about a fifth of German employees, so what's settled at Mercedes may set the terms for many more.

In science. <short pause>
Researchers at Flinders University in Adelaide have found that microbes living on the skin of young largetooth sawfish may help them survive the harsh conditions of Australia's dry season. The species is critically endangered, once found along tropical coasts on four continents and now gone from much of that range, largely lost to fishing nets. If a living film of bacteria can buffer these fish, protecting their nurseries may mean protecting what we can't even see.

In technology. <short pause>
Hackers working for North Korea stole more than four hundred million dollars by joining video calls from hijacked accounts and playing recorded clips of earlier victims, faces their targets knew and trusted. It's the newest turn for an operation the US says funds Pyongyang's weapons programmes, the same state blamed for last year's one and a half billion dollar theft from the exchange Bybit. American prosecutors have seized servers behind the scheme and moved to recover one point two billion dollars taken from Binance... but a familiar face on a screen can no longer be taken at face value.
<long pause>
Brazil's big-tech lobby is branding a digital markets bill as censorship, though the bill is about competition, not speech. It would give the competition authority, the Administrative Council for Economic Defence, a dedicated unit to police digital platforms, modelled on Europe's Digital Markets Act. Civil-society groups convened by the Presidency are rallying behind it, mindful that in twenty twenty-three a big-tech campaign helped shelve Brazil's last attempt to regulate the platforms.
<long pause>
Jensen Huang has quit Nvidia, stepping down as chief executive, president and director after more than three decades, and finance chief Colette Kress now runs the company. Reports have linked Huang to a businesswoman whom US authorities reportedly suspect of ties to Chinese intelligence. His exit leaves the company whose chips power much of the world's artificial intelligence without its founder, just as Washington and Beijing fight over who gets to buy those chips.
