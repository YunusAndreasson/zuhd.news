Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, has promised hell if American strikes resume... even as an Iraqi delegation spent eight hours in Tehran handing over a message from Washington, according to sources cited by Russia's state news agency. Donald Trump says three aircraft carriers stand ready and thirty-eight Iranian bridges are in his sights, yet calls fresh strikes highly unlikely if Tehran signs a nuclear deal and reopens the Strait of Hormuz. Iran's own plan asks for something larger: an end to the war on every front.

In politics. <short pause>
In northwestern Nigeria, gunmen set fire around a room in Gidan Taba village, in Zamfara State's Maradun area, killing twelve members of one family, among them women and children. Banditry across the northwest has outlasted every federal security campaign for more than a decade, fed by disputes over land, cattle and gold that the state has never settled. The killings come as thirty state assemblies approve state police... and the residents taken in the same raid haven't yet been counted.
<long pause>
Donald Trump spent his first weekend after losing Congress promising to double down on immigration enforcement, while conceding to CBS that deportations may have been too tough. Democrats now hold both chambers, with fifty-four Senate seats, which gives them subpoenas, hearings, control of spending and, in the House, the power to impeach. Hakeem Jeffries has already backed impeaching Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth, and Stephen Miller, the architect of the crackdown, becomes White House chief of staff on Monday.
<long pause>
Pope Leo the Fourteenth has arrived in Argentina, the visit his Argentine predecessor Francis never made, in a country where nearly a third of people live in poverty. Argentina's Church has openly criticised Javier Milei's austerity, which has cut health spending by forty-eight percent in real terms, and Brazil has recalled its ambassador after Milei refused to recognise Lula's election win. His call for South American unity now has to reach a president who's picked a fight with his country's largest trading partner.
<long pause>
In Jerusalem, Gadi Eisenkot has twenty-eight days to form Israel's first government after Benjamin Netanyahu... and he doesn't yet have a majority. The Joint List, the Palestinian citizens' alliance with nineteen seats, will back him from outside to keep Netanyahu from returning but won't join a cabinet, its leader Yousef Jabareen told the Russian agency TASS. Only once before, in twenty twenty-one, has an Arab party sat in an Israeli coalition, and that government lasted barely a year.
<long pause>
Near Giza, a train bound for Fayoum slammed into the rear of a Cairo-to-Aswan service at al-Ayyat, killing seven people and injuring a hundred and twelve, Egypt's Health Ministry says. It's the same district where, in two thousand and two, a fire swept through a crowded train and killed more than three hundred and seventy people... the country's worst rail disaster. A hundred and ten million Egyptians lean on this network, and decades of upgrade promises still haven't stopped the crashes.
<long pause>
Russian strikes have cut power to about eight hundred and fifty thousand Ukrainians, more than six hundred thousand of them in Dnipropetrovsk region, and the energy company DTEK says repeated attacks are slowing repairs as winter arrives. Moscow's defence ministry also says it hit three ships at Odesa and nearby Chornomorsk, claiming they carried weapons. Ukraine forged its own Black Sea shipping corridor in twenty twenty-three after Russia quit the grain deal, and that lifeline is again under fire.

On the economy. <short pause>
Iraq says the Iran war has cost it sixty billion dollars, and it's now secured American understandings for an oil export lane through the Strait of Hormuz that hugs the coast of Oman. Nearly all of Iraq's budget rides on crude shipped by sea, yet an official of Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy says no unauthorised vessel crossed the strait overnight... so who authorises passage remains open. Further north, a drone strike on the Khor Mor gasfield has left more than seventy percent of Iraqi Kurdistan without power, and no one's been held to account for the repeated attacks.
<long pause>
Pakistan's new auto policy cleared the International Monetary Fund in just two days, placing a levy on conventional cars to fund electric vehicle and export incentives. For decades the industry's mostly assembled foreign models for a protected home market, while more than twenty IMF bailouts have given the fund a quiet veto over Islamabad's industrial choices. The target is six billion dollars of exports in five years, from a sector that's rarely sold abroad.
<long pause>
In Newcastle, in South Africa's KwaZulu-Natal province, labour inspectors found just eight percent of seventy clothing factories complying with the law, and in one raid they found garments for the Foschini group's Jet brand. The town's clothing trade grew from apartheid-era incentives that pulled factories toward cheap labour on the edges of the old homelands, and the low wages outlived the system that created them. Retailers set the price per garment, a union official says, so the question is whether enforcement can reach the brands at the top of the chain.

In science. <short pause>
In northern Peru's Lambayeque Valley, archaeologists have found that violence stayed extremely rare, even through catastrophic El Niño floods. That coast is known for mass human sacrifice, and researchers long assumed those killings belonged to wars over food when floods ruined the fields; the new survey found little sign of it. Ritual killing, it seems, wasn't the same as a society turning on itself... a distinction worth understanding as climate shocks return.
<long pause>
Off the Cocos Keeling Islands in the Indian Ocean, researchers have mapped a single rose coral colony stretching one point five six kilometres, about a hundred buses end to end. Lead researcher JP Hobbs needed twelve dives, between fourteen and sixty-five metres deep, to trace its nearly fifty-seven thousand square metres along the outer reef. Much about it is still unknown, at a time when warming seas have bleached reefs across the world.
<long pause>
At Sungkyunkwan University in South Korea, Seong Sik Shin's team has grown a single perovskite film that works as both a solar cell and a light-emitting diode, with dramatically fewer defects. Perovskites have promised cheap solar power since two thousand and nine, when Japanese researchers first tried them, but tiny crystal flaws make them degrade too fast for rooftops. Built with Korea's chemical technology institute, UC San Diego and Princeton, the film's next test is whether it lasts outside the lab.

In technology. <short pause>
Anthropic has paid three point four billion dollars for one point seven gigawatts of generators, the largest known power purchase of its kind by an AI lab. It took over a Caterpillar order that OnePWR placed two years ago, before prices soared, because generator lead times now stretch to years... so labs, like xAI and Alphabet before them, are buying their own power rather than waiting on utilities. Blackstone has warned investors the company could carry a junk credit rating ahead of its stock market listing.
<long pause>
ByteDance is weighing a second, larger data centre phase at Brazil's Port of Pecém in Ceará state, where the first three hundred megawatts are already under construction, people familiar with the plans say. The project earns tax incentives by serving customers abroad, turning Brazil's semi-arid northeast, a region long marked by drought and migration south, into an export hub for computing. Who gets the water, the power and the jurisdiction over the data is still unsettled.
<long pause>
Nvidia's board has opened an independent investigation into Jensen Huang, who stays on as chief executive after a letter to staff about an affair. Separately, anonymous American intelligence officials reportedly flagged Alice Huang of Megaspeed, an Nvidia cloud partner in Singapore, as an asset of Beijing... an allegation no court has tested, and House committees want a closed briefing on the eighteenth of November. The stakes reach beyond one man, because Jensen Huang has been the loudest voice for easing chip export limits on China.
