This is your briefing for the twenty-third of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Drones struck Khartoum at dawn... and five children are dead, with at least ten people wounded. Four of the dead came from a single family that had already fled the fighting in West Kordofan, and a drone believed to belong to the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces hit their house in Omdurman. Another killed a ten-year-old in Jabal Awliya, a reminder that Sudan's displaced find no safe ground, not even in the capital.

In politics. <short pause>
Two Russian ships have been hit in two days, in waters a continent apart. A burning military cargo ship, the Sparta Two, owned by a logistics arm of Russia's defence ministry and under US sanctions, drifted into Finnish waters after Saturday's Baltic strike, and Prime Minister Petteri Orpo blamed Ukraine, though Kyiv hasn't claimed it. Then, at dawn on Sunday, a Russian corvette exploded in Benghazi, where it had stopped for fuel under a deal with Libya's eastern government... and nobody has yet said who sent the drone the authorities there suspect.
<long pause>
Coalition talks between Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud and Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar have collapsed, split on whether Itamar Ben-Gvir keeps his place in government. As national security minister, Ben-Gvir commands Israel's police and the prisons holding Palestinians, while Eisenkot's bloc of forty-eight seats sits thirteen short of a majority, so any government needs Likud, the ultra-Orthodox or Arab parties. Until someone bridges that gap, a caretaker government keeps deciding the fate of Gaza and the West Bank with no new mandate.
<long pause>
From Djibouti, twenty-two countries' coastguards, drawn from the Gulf and East Africa, will jointly patrol the Red Sea from next year, under a task force endorsed by the UN's International Maritime Organization. Houthi attacks on Saudi shipping left a security vacuum, and piracy returned within weeks, echoing the Somali piracy wave that regional states confronted under the Djibouti Code of Conduct, signed in two thousand nine. Still, the force has no legal framework yet, and members have until September twenty twenty-seven to settle who prosecutes the pirates they catch.
<long pause>
In Hong Kong, five thousand six hundred and forty of roughly five thousand seven hundred eligible electors voted in Sunday's Election Committee poll, and Beijing hailed a record turnout. Those electors are government-appointed district representatives, corporate bodies named by the authorities and subsidised non-governmental organisations... not the public, which has had no say over this body since Beijing's twenty twenty-one overhaul admitted only candidates vetted as patriots. The fifteen-hundred-member committee will pick the city's next leader, so the real contest was settled long before anyone cast a ballot.
<long pause>
Air traffic control across Belgium stopped for fourteen hours, as controllers joined a national strike against Prime Minister Bart De Wever's ten billion euro savings plan. Skeyes, the air navigation service, said staffing was too thin to run safely, so Brussels and Charleroi went dark, along with thousands of passengers' plans and a lost day for Charleroi's three thousand four hundred airport workers. Unions are fighting cuts to pensions and public services, and the question now is whether a government built on austerity bends before the next walkout.

On the economy. <short pause>
Oman is lending Pakistan State Oil two hundred million dollars to buy fuel, as the US-Iran conflict chokes the Strait of Hormuz into its ninth month. Oman's state trader, OQ Trading, has already sold the company more than a million tonnes of gasoline this year, according to Pakistan's Petroleum Division. A country that's leaned on Gulf credit through repeated balance-of-payments crises is now relying on a neighbour just to keep its pumps full.
<long pause>
Shares in APL Apollo fell more than fifteen percent on Monday after Tata Steel agreed to pay up to fifteen thousand five hundred and ten crore rupees, about one point seven billion dollars, for a twenty-six point four percent stake. A planned open offer for another twenty-six percent could hand one of India's oldest industrial names control of the tube maker... a steelmaker buying its own customer to capture the margins downstream. Analysts at Chola Securities point out that APL Apollo's margins of seven to eight percent trail Tata Steel's own, and investors are asking which side gets the better deal.
<long pause>
Ships waited nine days at anchor off Durban, while Cape Town had no queue at all. South Africa's health department listed pharmaceutical cargo on sixteen vessels needing urgent evacuation, after an August software update triggered a backlog the terminal operator blames on years of underinvestment. The government's betting on opening Transnet's debt-laden rail network to private operators... but miners and farmers can't wait while ore and citrus sit short of the coast.
<long pause>
Across the continent in Abuja, a twelve-and-a-half kilogram cylinder of cooking gas now costs up to twenty-five thousand naira. Prices have climbed from as little as one thousand three hundred and sixty naira a kilo in October to as much as two thousand, and Nigeria's regulator cites low import volumes and weak distribution, in a country holding Africa's largest gas reserves. Households are rationing gas and buying charcoal stoves, a shift that costs forests and lungs as much as wallets.
<long pause>
Gold fell nine point two percent in a week, to three thousand five hundred and fifteen dollars an ounce, and buyers who held off at record prices are back in Dubai's Gold Souq. A hawkish Federal Reserve, a stronger dollar and oil-driven inflation pushed it down, even as the Nasdaq had its worst week since March twenty twenty and US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned the Fed against overtightening. Markets still price another rate increase next month, so the souq's bargain hunters are wagering the dip won't last.

In science. <short pause>
A remotely operated robot has found the world's deepest known explosive volcanic eruptions, near Challenger Deep, almost eleven thousand metres down in the Mariana Trench. Water pressure at that depth was thought to smother such explosions, and the trench itself began forming fifty-two million years ago, when the Pacific plate started sliding beneath the Philippine Sea plate. Scientists hope the discovery helps answer a question that's long puzzled them... why this one trench plunges so far.
<long pause>
Egyptian archaeologists have uncovered a necropolis at Tarrana, in the western Nile Delta, holding hundreds of coffins and gold. The graves span Egypt's Late Period into early Roman rule, beside Wadi el-Natrun, source of the natron salt that embalmers used to mummify the dead. Preliminary studies suggest these were middle-class burials, and further fieldwork may show how ordinary Egyptians, not only pharaohs, prepared for eternity.
<long pause>
Researchers in Hokkaido have watched silver atoms briefly take on the arrangement used by radioactive polonium, a structure silver doesn't normally adopt. Arrangement is everything in chemistry: the same carbon atoms make diamond or graphite, with vastly different properties. The new form, reported in the journal Nano Letters, shows unusually high chemical reactivity, which could open new uses, if it can be made to last.

In technology. <short pause>
A Deloitte China survey found that nearly half of Chinese firms, forty-eight and a half percent, say returns from artificial intelligence fall short of expectations. In one company, AI made two thousand engineers twenty to forty percent faster, yet the company itself moved no faster, because requirements, review, testing and release still queue for approval at every gate. It's the productivity paradox economists saw with computers in the nineteen-eighties, and in what China's tech press calls the year of silicon-based employees, firms now have to redesign the process, not just buy the model.
<long pause>
Software has verified a claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis before human mathematicians have even read it, according to China's Zhidx. The proof assistant Lean and independent checkers accepted it, and its statement matches the standard definition in Mathlib, the shared library of formal mathematics, while the proof itself was built by Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge working with the AI model Claude. That hypothesis has stood open since Bernhard Riemann posed it in eighteen fifty-nine... and it now awaits the slower verdict of human readers.
