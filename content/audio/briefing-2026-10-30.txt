This is your Jumu'ah briefing for the thirtieth of October, twenty twenty-six.

In the southern Syrian village of Al-Taff, a Jordanian airstrike killed Lamis, who was eight, Osama, nine, and Yaseen, eleven... along with their uncle and their grandmother. Their father vanished into Assad's prisons years ago, and now the children he left behind are gone too. Amman says Monday's strikes avenged an attack on a church funeral in Karak and killed more than twenty Islamic State fighters, but nobody has said who investigates a cross-border strike in post-Assad Syria.

In politics. <short pause>
More than two hundred and eighty thousand Yemenis have fled their homes since August, as the war between Saudi Arabia and Ansarallah has reignited. Nearly seventeen hundred people have died in that time, shattering the uneasy calm that had largely held since the truce of twenty twenty-two. Ansarallah counts a hundred and one airstrikes by Saudi-backed forces, while Human Rights Watch accuses Ansarallah of depriving civilians in Taiz of food... and the displaced are caught between both.
<long pause>
Ukraine's drone strikes on Russian oil refineries stopped in mid-October, according to Ukrainian military officials. The campaign was one of Kyiv's most effective weapons against Moscow's war revenue, but Ukraine depends on American Starlink access, intelligence sharing and Patriot interceptors. Washington, worried about oil prices during the Gulf war, had leverage to spare... while Russia hasn't paused its own strikes on Ukraine's power grid, with winter approaching.
<long pause>
Venezuela's opposition leader, María Corina Machado, says the United States has proposed presidential elections in twenty twenty-seven. Interim president Delcy Rodríguez has promised a vote but set no date, and Washington is backing talks on overhauling the electoral system that leave Machado's own party out. It's an unusual transition, one where the timetable for Venezuelan democracy seems to be drafted in Washington rather than in Caracas.
<long pause>
Between fifty-seven and sixty people were killed when gunmen attacked a market in Karim Lamido, in Nigeria's Taraba State. Lawmaker Mohammed Audu told the House of Representatives that ten died at Binnari market on the twenty-third of October, while others leapt into a river to escape. The district has no access road... so security forces arrive only after the attackers have gone.
<long pause>
Along the Lawa River in Suriname, Wayana villagers are mapping their ancestral land to hold off wildcat gold miners. Suriname is the only country in South America that still doesn't legally recognise Indigenous land rights, and twenty-five thousand hectares near the French Guiana border were lost to mining between twenty twenty-one and twenty twenty-four. Miners now outnumber the Wayana themselves, and the government in Paramaribo sometimes hands out concessions on land the Wayana call home.
<long pause>
Four people were killed and eleven injured when an Italian Chinook helicopter crashed on a training flight in Bosnia's Brčko district. Italy's defence ministry says the dead were two soldiers and two Red Cross volunteers serving with the European Union force that's kept the peace there since two thousand and four. Three decades after the Dayton accords, up to fifteen hundred European troops remain, as secessionist pressure from Republika Srpska tests the settlement.

On the economy. <short pause>
Investigators at Chainalysis have confirmed at least five point nine billion dollars stolen from Binance, likely the largest crypto theft ever, and blockchain sleuths point to North Korea's Lazarus Group. Stablecoin issuers Tether and Circle froze more than two point three billion... but bitcoin has no issuer, so so-called decentralised money could only be stopped where a company sat in the middle. Binance held about a hundred and forty-four billion dollars before the breach and says it'll cover every loss, though deposits and withdrawals remain suspended.
<long pause>
Intel beat its own revenue forecast and still lost twenty-three percent of its value in after-hours trading. Chief executive Lip-Bu Tan expects the personal computer chip market to shrink twelve percent next year, as soaring memory prices push computer makers to cut orders. Meanwhile the company lacks the wafers and substrates to meet booming server demand, and it's the American government's largest stake in a chipmaker... so the question is whether it can build capacity before the AI boom passes it by.
<long pause>
Diesel in Pakistan now costs more than four hundred rupees a litre, while petrol holds just under three hundred and seventy-eight. Taxes and duties take about a hundred rupees from every litre of diesel, the fuel that moves trucks and tractors and runs power plants and generators. With Gulf supply squeezed by the Iran war, the farmer and the transporter pay first, and the rise spreads into what every household pays for food.
<long pause>
Brazil's flagship fund to pay countries for keeping tropical forests standing aimed for a hundred and twenty-five billion dollars... it has seven point eight billion pledged. The UN Environment Programme says tropical forest nations need sixty-seven billion dollars a year by twenty thirty, yet only eight of more than sixty-five endorsing countries have put money down. Even Norway's three billion depends on the fund reaching ten billion, and the forests aren't waiting for the pledges to arrive.

In science. <short pause>
Researchers in Borneo found that fruit production collapsed for two and a half years after the great fires of twenty fifteen. Orangutans lost lean body mass as females rested more, travelled less and fell back on poorer food, a slow famine long after the smoke cleared. Indonesia now faces one of its worst wildfire seasons in more than a decade, with Kalimantan's peatlands smouldering again.
<long pause>
As delegates prepare for the UN climate summit in Turkey, COP thirty-one, they're watching what China asks for next. Last year in Brazil, Beijing helped secure a two-year work programme on climate finance, won a dialogue on unilateral trade measures like Europe's carbon border tax, and co-led a methane summit with Brazil and the UK. For decades China negotiated from behind the line that separated developing from developed nations... now it's setting the agenda, and whether it'll also carry more of the bill remains open.

In technology. <short pause>
Free images of Gaza and parts of the West Bank from Europe's Sentinel satellites now arrive twenty-four hours late, the investigative group Bellingcat has found. The EU says member states unanimously ordered the delay in late March over threats linked to Iran's attacks, echoing an American rule that degraded commercial imagery of Israel for more than two decades. Researchers documenting Israeli army demolitions in Hebron have already waited a day for pictures... and nobody has said when the delay will end.
<long pause>
President Trump has set a hundred-and-eighty-day clock on foreign robot imports, a possible prelude to tariffs on national security grounds. The big four makers of industrial robotic arms, FANUC, ABB, Yaskawa and KUKA, are based in Japan, Switzerland and Germany, and America has no domestic source of heavy-duty arms. Any tariff would land on American factories trying to automate, the very plants the policy claims to protect.
<long pause>
Internal Slack messages reported by Four Oh Four Media show Proton's chief executive, Andy Yen, rejecting security testing before release. People chose the Swiss company's encrypted email precisely for privacy, yet a growing share of its code now comes from AI, and Yen says this is the last year it'll hire engineers. For a firm whose whole promise is trust, the question is who's checking the machine's work.
