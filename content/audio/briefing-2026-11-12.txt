This is your briefing for the twelfth of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Mississippi hasn't sent a Democrat to the Senate since nineteen eighty-two... and now Scott Colom has beaten Cindy Hyde-Smith by only a few thousand votes. He'll be the state's first Black senator in more than a hundred and forty years, taking the seat once held by Hiram Revels, the first Black American ever to serve in the Senate. Black residents make up thirty-seven percent of Mississippi, the highest share of any state, and white voters angry about prices joined them... a coalition Democrats will test again in next year's race for governor.

In politics. <short pause>
In Indian-administered Kashmir, two Special Police Officers were found shot dead in Kishtwar, their weapons missing. They'd vanished on Wednesday in the Dachhan forest, a zone with no mobile coverage, after villagers heard gunshots, and police call it the third attack in Jammu and Kashmir since July. These officers are locally recruited, poorly paid auxiliaries who carry the front line of a decades-old insurgency, and the security sweeps that follow fall hardest on the forest villages around them.
<long pause>
Malaysia's convicted former prime minister, Najib Razak, is now serving his graft sentence at home after a royal pardon, and he can't use a phone without prison approval. Home Minister Saifuddin Nasution Ismail told parliament that any breach sends Najib straight back to Kajang Prison. It's a striking precedent: billions vanished from the One Malaysia Development Berhad fund under his watch, and voters threw out his coalition over it in twenty eighteen... yet the man convicted for it now serves his time in his own house.
<long pause>
In Israel, sixty-seven lawmakers backed Gadi Eisenkot to form the next government, against forty-six for Benjamin Netanyahu. Eisenkot has written to the caretaker premier, warning him not to order troops into action without an immediate threat, because Netanyahu keeps command of the army until a new coalition wins the Knesset's confidence. That warning comes from a former army chief whose own name is attached to a doctrine of overwhelming force against Lebanese neighbourhoods, and for Gaza and Lebanon, where tens of thousands have been killed, the handover offers no certain relief.
<long pause>
Armenia has lost its voting rights in the Collective Security Treaty Organization, the Russia-led military bloc, over unpaid dues. Yerevan froze its participation in February twenty twenty-four, saying the alliance offered no meaningful support when it needed it, and neither Russia nor any other member sent troops in twenty twenty, though the charter envisages mutual defence. As Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan hedge toward China and Turkey, the bloc meeting in Moscow looks less like an alliance and more like a question.
<long pause>
Brazil's São Paulo state has a new justice secretary... Ricardo Salles, a defendant in a timber-smuggling case, appointed by governor Tarcísio de Freitas in the very week of the COP thirty-one climate summit. As environment minister from twenty nineteen to twenty twenty-one, Salles cut federal enforcement while Amazon deforestation climbed, and since twenty twenty-three he's been accused of ties to a network that smuggled illegal timber. The appointment is part of an electoral bargain, and it puts a man charged over the forest in charge of justice in Brazil's richest state.
<long pause>
Palestinians won't vote before September twenty twenty-seven, under a decree issued by President Mahmoud Abbas on the tenth of October. The delay stretches a gap of more than twenty years without national elections, since the twenty oh-six vote whose result was never allowed to govern. Abbas cites occupation obstacles and insists on voting in Jerusalem, the West Bank and Gaza alike, but a generation of Palestinians has now come of age without ever choosing its own leaders.

On the economy. <short pause>
Sudan has earned three and a half billion dollars from gold exports, and ordinary families haven't felt any of it. The Sudanese pound keeps falling while war spending takes priority, and economist Haitham Mohamed Fathi says gold supplies close to half of all exports. Smuggling is so widespread that nobody can say how much is really mined, which means the country's richest resource is fuelling the war rather than feeding the people living through it.
<long pause>
Sanctioned Russian crude is now selling for more than Brent, the global benchmark, with eastern Siberian oil topping a hundred and forty-one dollars a barrel. Western sanctions and the Group of Seven price cap were built to force Moscow to sell at a discount, but with Gulf barrels cut off by the Hormuz closure, China and India are bidding for whatever Russia can ship. Asian buyers are already hunting for supplies closer to home, and the longer the strait stays shut, the more Moscow becomes Asia's supplier of last resort.
<long pause>
Pakistan has cut diesel to three hundred and sixty-four rupees a litre, down from a wartime peak above five hundred and twenty. That's still well above the pre-war price of two hundred and eighty-one, and taxes alone take a hundred rupees on every litre of diesel and a hundred and fourteen on petrol. Diesel runs the trucks and power plants that set the price of nearly everything, so households will judge this relief by what it does to their bills in the coming weeks.
<long pause>
El Salvador's vice president, Félix Ulloa, has hailed an incredible boom... but foreign investment actually fell thirty-seven point two percent. The fact-checker Voz Pública tested twelve of his economic claims and found six false or misleading, noting that construction grew twenty-four point four percent largely on state-backed building. Under President Nayib Bukele, the government's security record has drawn global attention, and now its economic story is being measured against its own official figures.

In science. <short pause>
Researchers have linked summer droughts stretching from Romania to Kazakhstan to shrinking winter ice in two rapidly warming seas. This summer brought one of Europe's most severe droughts since fifteen-forty, deepening the humanitarian crisis in Ukraine and parching wheat belts that feed much of the world. If winter ice really does foretell a dry summer, farmers could get months of warning... but only if forecasters learn to read the signal in time.
<long pause>
Kenya has discharged its last Ebola patient, after the virus killed two of nine confirmed cases. A single traveller from the Democratic Republic of the Congo infected four relatives and four health workers, and the health ministry traced a hundred and thirty-five contacts. Saudi Arabia's visa suspension on the thirtieth of October has already stranded hundreds of Kenyan Gulf workers, and the outbreak can only be declared over after forty-two days without a new case, in sha'Allah.
<long pause>
Two remote atolls in the Marshall Islands, Bikar and Bokak, may be among the most intact ecosystems left on Earth. That's remarkable for a nation where the United States detonated sixty-seven nuclear weapons between nineteen forty-six and nineteen fifty-eight, and where humans have marked nearly every place from the deep sea to the highest peaks. The study in Frontiers in Marine Science offers a baseline of what an untouched reef looks like, just as rising seas threaten the low-lying islands themselves.

In technology. <short pause>
The Chinese artificial intelligence company MiniMax has released an image model that renders sixteen-megapixel pictures in about ten seconds, and it's published the full weights for anyone to use. Testers found it took a fifth of the time of mainstream models, could fuse up to sixteen reference images, and set dense Chinese text accurately, a task that's long defeated systems trained mostly on English. Chinese labs keep giving away what Western rivals sell, though complex work like exploded technical diagrams still trips it up.
