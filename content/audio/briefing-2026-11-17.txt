This is your briefing for the seventeenth of November, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Three American aircraft carriers and thirteen destroyers now face Iran... as the USS Theodore Roosevelt joins a blockade that's redirected a hundred and fifty merchant ships since June. Iran says mines struck four tankers in a single day, and a drone set off sirens over Iraq's Khor Mor gas field. Tehran has also cut its plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz from seven days to two, Pakistan's foreign minister says, and Washington hasn't replied.

In politics. <short pause>
Paraguay's ousted president, Santiago Peña, is being moved to a military prison after reports that his army-base cell was a suite with a private gym. He fell this month over the collapse of Ueno Bank, where prosecutors say he steered public money while insiders borrowed heavily, and more than three quarters of the two point eight billion dollars deposited there still hasn't been returned. Peña denies the charges... and the savers who lost everything are watching to see whether a prison wall means accountability or just a change of address.
<long pause>
Bahrain has sentenced a group of religious scholars to life in prison and confiscated their assets, according to Iran's state-run Mehr News Agency, the only outlet reporting it so far. The kingdom has prosecuted its Shia clergy repeatedly since the twenty eleven uprising, when a largely Shia population rose against the Sunni monarchy, and five years later it stripped the country's most senior Shia cleric, Sheikh Isa Qassim, of his citizenship. Tehran's foreign ministry calls the trials collective punishment built on baseless claims of Iranian links, while the names and the charges remain unconfirmed by any independent source.
<long pause>
In a basement cell in Damascus, thirty-three Somali women and children are being held without heating, sunlight or clean water. Syrian officials moved them out of Roj camp in the northeast on the eighth of November, after years of detention that began with the fall of the Islamic State group... and none of the women has ever been charged. Officials have told them they'll likely be deported to Somalia, with no court, no lawyer, and no say in where they're sent.
<long pause>
Pope Leo the Fourteenth ended his South American tour in the Peruvian Amazon, demanding justice before a largely Indigenous crowd for the land defenders who've been murdered there. Rights groups count between thirty-five and fifty-seven of them killed, as illegal loggers and miners push deeper into the forest, and Peru lost about two hundred thousand hectares of it last year alone. A pope who once served as a bishop in Peru can name the dead, but convictions for their killers remain rare.
<long pause>
Off Bulgaria's Black Sea coast, the wheat ship Betul Eight sank after a reported sea-drone strike... and all twelve crew survived. It's the seventeenth vessel attacked off Bulgaria and Romania since mid-July, the security firm Diaplous says, and this one was carrying three thousand tonnes of Romanian wheat to Egypt. Egypt is among the world's largest wheat buyers, so every attack on this grain route reaches the price of bread far from the war.

On the economy. <short pause>
In the Nigerian city of Kano, two hundred and twenty-two gas-powered vehicles unveiled in May still haven't carried a single passenger. The fleet was meant to cushion the end of petrol subsidies, which pushed pump prices up more than five hundred percent, yet seven months on the buses sit parked. Meanwhile a pregnant woman, Aisha Bashir, pays eleven hundred naira to reach hospital... and she's missed two antenatal appointments when she couldn't find the fare.
<long pause>
The Asian Development Bank has given Karachi's Red Line bus project ten more months, with strings attached. Since construction began in March twenty twenty-two, the cost has climbed to seven hundred and twenty-five million dollars, and the lender now demands milestones, including a new contract for a twelve point eight kilometre stretch. Pakistan repays the loan whether or not the buses ever run, while commuters on one of the city's busiest corridors keep picking their way around torn-up roads.
<long pause>
South Africa's sugar industry is campaigning to scrap the health levy on sugary drinks, citing sixteen thousand six hundred and twenty-one lost jobs. That figure, the Treasury says, is a model's estimate for twenty nineteen against an imagined world with no levy... nobody actually counted those jobs. Taxes like this one spread from Mexico to dozens of countries as a weapon against diabetes, and lawmakers in Pretoria now have to decide how much weight a projection deserves.
<long pause>
A court in Milan has placed Revolut's Italian branch under two court-appointed administrators for a year. Rival banks filed two hundred and sixty-five suspicious transaction reports on twenty-four suspects, the court found, while Revolut, with more than five million Italian customers, filed none. Its branch runs on a licence from Lithuania, a reminder that Europe's banking passport lets a fintech grow fast across borders while its supervisor sits in another country.

In science. <short pause>
Vampire bats drink about half their body weight in blood, and researchers in Frankfurt have now decoded the genomes that let all three species live on it. Blood holds little sugar or fat and a toxic load of iron, so their stomachs became stretchy, pH-neutral storage sacs, and one water-shedding gene runs more than nine hundred times higher to lighten them for the flight home. Published in Nature, the work shows how far evolution will rebuild a mammal for a diet almost nothing else could survive.
<long pause>
At City University of Hong Kong, researchers have made a vividly coloured coating that still reflects ninety-seven percent of light. Until now, cooling a surface in daylight without electricity meant painting it white or making it mirror-like, but this one makes its colour through thin-film interference, the same trick that tints a soap bubble, layered above a thick reflective base. Whether these ethyl cellulose films can be made cheaply at scale will decide if cities can cool their rooftops without turning them all white.

In technology. <short pause>
South Korea will ground every flight for thirty-five minutes on Thursday so its national university entrance exam can run in silence. This year proctors face a new threat, after a thirty-four-year-old who topped a tax accountant exam confessed to photographing questions with a miniature camera and reading answers from an AI on his phone. A state that can empty its skies for students now has to stop machines whispering in their ears.
<long pause>
United Airlines' departures stalled for ninety minutes on Monday, days after a nationwide air traffic ground stop and alongside outages at Chase and other big American banks. Ordinary faults, not attackers, appear to lie behind the run... a software bug at Chase, a network failure at the Federal Aviation Administration, and at United, the system that calculates a plane's takeoff weight. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy now wants fifty billion dollars for air traffic control, but Senate Republican leader John Thune wants to know why, when Democrats say only one billion of last year's twelve and a half billion has been spent.
<long pause>
Germany's draft budget trims its digital sovereignty lines to a hundred and twenty-nine point seven million euros, down from nearly a hundred and thirty-eight million. That money funds openDesk, a public open source workplace meant to replace commercial office software, while the federal administration spent four hundred and eighty-one million euros on Microsoft licences last year alone. Berlin says it wants independence from foreign tech giants... yet it's paying them nearly four times what it spends on the alternative.
