This is your Jumu'ah briefing for the ninth of October, twenty twenty-six.
<long pause>
Eighty schoolchildren from Borno State, in Nigeria's northeast, are still in captivity... nearly six months after insurgents took them. Forty-four were seized at Mussa in May, by the governor's count, and thirty-six more at Lassa two months later. Since the Chibok abduction of twenty fourteen, Nigeria has seen roughly thirty mass school kidnappings and more than two thousand students taken, and the families in Borno are asking why this one has slipped from the country's attention.

In politics. <short pause>
In Sanaa, a fourteen-year-old girl named Athir al-Amri was killed on her school bus, on the way home from her last exam. Witnesses and relatives say shrapnel from a Saudi-led coalition airstrike hit the bus and wounded two of her classmates. The coalition says a hundred jets destroyed three hundred and twenty-four military targets on the fifth of October, and it hasn't yet accounted for the bus.
<long pause>
A federal immigration agent in New York fired seven rounds into a car, hitting the driver in the neck, while a five-year-old boy sat inside, unharmed. Mayor Zohran Mamdani called the shooting unconscionable, but a city government has no command over federal agents working its streets. That leaves the inquiry with the Department of Homeland Security... the same department that employs the agent.
<long pause>
Across the border in Mexico, a fight inside El Castillo prison in Mazatlán killed nine inmates and a minor who was visiting, and state authorities say sixteen more were hurt. It's two years since one faction of the Sinaloa Cartel handed Ismael Zambada to the United States, splitting the organisation into the Chapitos and the Mayiza. Reporters from InSight Crime found that in Culiacán the open shootouts have given way to targeted killings, and the fighting's moved south, toward the very city where the prison stands.
<long pause>
South Africa's parliament has heard that sixty-three officials in the Ekurhuleni metro police were hired with criminal convictions already on record, murder among them. Another fifty-five collected seventy-two convictions while serving, including attempted murder and firearm offences, in a force that polices four million people east of Johannesburg. Lawmakers still don't know whether any of those officers have been removed from duty.
<long pause>
Azerbaijan says four thousand and ten of its citizens are still missing from three decades of war with Armenia. None of the fifty DNA profiles that Armenia sent matched the remains Azerbaijan has recovered, and Baku's handed over a list of eight hundred and thirty-four people it believes were taken prisoner in the nineteen nineties. Two governments have initialled a peace deal... but it's the families who keep the count, and they're still waiting.
<long pause>
In Vilnius, the European Union withdrew its sponsorship of Europe's annual counter-disinformation conference on the day it opened, over a panel that examined interference by the United States. Reporters quote internal State Department documents in which American officials thank Lithuania, the host country. Europe built its defences against information manipulation with Russia and China in mind, and it's now unclear whether those defences can be pointed at an ally.

On the economy. <short pause>
Houthi missiles have hit Riyadh's airport again, killing at least three people, and oil prices surged on fears that the war's spreading from the Strait of Hormuz into Saudi Arabia's heartland. Iran's stepped up its attacks on shipping in the strait, and refined fuel is running short, with European diesel up eight percent in a single day. For countries that import their fuel, the shortage is already showing up in the price of transport and food.
<long pause>
Australia has lost what would've been its largest stock market listing since Telstra in nineteen ninety-seven. Firmus, a data-centre company backed by Nvidia, sought a valuation of about forty-four billion dollars, six hundred times its revenue, while only forty-six of the nine hundred and twelve megawatts it's contracted are actually running. Companies like Firmus need public money to finish what they've promised to build, and that market's closing to them.
<long pause>
India's biggest software firm, Tata Consultancy Services, reported quarterly revenue up eleven percent, to more than seventy-three thousand crore rupees, with net profit up fifteen percent. Its artificial intelligence work now passes a tenth of revenue, at an annual rate of three point one billion dollars, in an industry many expected that same technology to hollow out. The tension's elsewhere... this week Washington suspended green card sponsorship for firms including Tata, restricting the very people the industry sends to its largest market.
<long pause>
From Geneva, the World Trade Organization has doubled its forecast for growth in goods trade this year. Goods that enable artificial intelligence made up forty-seven percent of that growth by value, and Pakistan's sea freight exports rose seventy-three percent in the first half as its operators absorbed traffic diverted from Hormuz. So the same war that's choking one route is enriching another, and nobody can say how long either lasts.

In science. <short pause>
Chemists at Ulm University in Germany have found that nickel electrodes do their work as nickel dioxide, and not as the oxyhydroxide that textbooks assumed. Nickel's the cheap, robust catalyst in alkaline water electrolysis, one of the main routes to climate-neutral hydrogen, and the team determined its structure under real reaction conditions. If the surface was misidentified all along, then designs built on the old picture may need rethinking.
<long pause>
Mathematicians are reeling after OpenAI released more than three hundred and fifty mathematical findings in one day, including a claimed proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, a conjecture related to one of the Millennium Prize Problems. By Thursday the company had withdrawn three of the papers and corrected several others in its public repository. Peer review was built for a world where proofs arrive one at a time, and no one's yet said who'll check three hundred and fifty.

In technology. <short pause>
Alibaba is suing the Pentagon, denying any link to the Chinese military. The listing it's fighting was created in twenty twenty-one, and while it isn't a sanction in itself, it bars a company from Pentagon contracts and signals that Commerce or Treasury penalties could follow. Xiaomi won removal from an earlier version of the list that same year, so the question's whether a court will again ask Washington to show its evidence.
<long pause>
South Korean police are investigating attacks on at least nine banks since late September, and the security firm CrowdStrike says the likely culprit is a twenty-six-year-old based in China. The tool involved, called ARTEX, automates penetration testing by calling on outside models such as ChatGPT, Claude and DeepSeek, and its developer has now closed the source code. Closing a repository doesn't recall the copies already downloaded... and that's the problem with releasing dual-use tools in the open.
<long pause>
In the desert of the United Arab Emirates, a one-tonne test vehicle called ONEIROS is due to fly next year, in sha'Allah. It's a privately funded hopper from the Exploration Company, with a throttleable methane engine, autonomous navigation and hazard avoidance, meant to prove controlled landings. The aim is cheaper access to the Moon, and it's being tested far from the old launch sites of the space age.
