A Kenyan court has convicted the pastor Paul Mackenzie and twenty-eight other leaders of his movement of murdering a hundred and ninety-one people... and a hundred and ninety of them were children. He ordered his followers in the Shakahola forest, near Malindi, to fast to death, and armed guards strangled or clubbed those who refused. Kenya's own rights commission says that if police had properly investigated his arrest back in twenty seventeen, lives would have been saved.

In politics. <short pause>
South Korea's Defence Ministry has vowed resolute punishment after Pyongyang threatened to deploy tactical strike weapons and loudspeakers along the border. North Korea's spent two and a half years mining and fencing that line, and its state media chose the same day to show Kim Jong Un at a clothing expo, ending days of rumours that he'd died. Loudspeakers alone could unravel the thaw President Lee Jae Myung counts among his first achievements.
<long pause>
Fourteen people have died in a landslide at an unlicensed gold pit in Santa Bárbara de Iscuandé, in Colombia's southwestern department of Nariño. Armed groups control the gold trade there, and the governor's office says they hampered rescuers, while informal pits like this one have no structural reinforcement at all. With gold at record prices, the people who dig it keep dying... and the people who profit from it are rarely the ones counted.
<long pause>
Four Salvadoran soldiers are missing after gangs bombed an armoured vehicle of Haiti's UN-backed force near Croix-des-Bouquets, then fired on the helicopters sent to rescue them. It's the first major attack on the Gang Suppression Force since it began operating on the first of June, in a capital where gangs control an estimated seventy percent of the territory. As the Security Council meets on Haiti, the question is who protects Haitians if the force can't protect itself.
<long pause>
In Pakistan's National Assembly, opposition leader Mehmood Khan Achakzai has demanded to know where Sohail Afridi is... the elected chief minister of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, unseen since the eleventh of October. Afridi's government was sidelined when governor's rule and an emergency took effect, and a day after he vanished, a federal minister said Afridi and some of his cabinet were in preventive custody. Nobody in Islamabad has yet said where that custody is, or on what legal basis a sitting head of government is being held.
<long pause>
Storm Adam has killed three people on Spain's Mediterranean coast, and eighty-seven thousand residents are still under orders to shelter in place. In the town of Pego, six hundred and thirty-two millimetres of rain fell in thirty-six hours, and helicopters winched people from rooftops in towns that military vehicles couldn't reach. Two years ago, on this same coast, more than two hundred and twenty people died after flood alerts reached phones only once the water had arrived... whether the rebuilt system worked this time is still unanswered.
<long pause>
Benjamin Netanyahu, who's wanted by the International Criminal Court, has published a three-page security doctrine that makes preemptive war official Israeli policy. It names Iran, Hezbollah and the Houthis as threats, and it licenses buffer zones along Israel's borders without saying where they'd be. Released just a week before the twenty-seventh of October election, it's been dismissed by Gadi Eisenkot and Yair Lapid as empty slogans... but for Israel's neighbours, a doctrine without borders is no slogan.

On the economy. <short pause>
Saudi Arabia has postponed the Future Investment Initiative, the summit known as Davos in the Desert, from the twenty-sixth of October to twenty twenty-seven. Houthi strikes across the kingdom, including on Riyadh's airport, have killed at least eighteen people, and agencies in the capital have ordered staff to work from home. Vision twenty-thirty depends on billions in foreign capital to move the economy beyond oil, and the postponement concedes that investors may not come to a country under fire.
<long pause>
Iran's new Persian Gulf Strait Authority says it's put a hundred and twenty-two ships on a blacklist, according to the state-run Mehr agency. The authority says it's dealt with more than forty vessels, while nineteen are negotiating and two have been removed... Tehran is now running the Strait of Hormuz, through which a fifth of the world's oil normally passes, as a permit regime. Brent crude settled at a hundred and nine dollars on Monday, and every new rule in the strait is priced at pumps worldwide.
<long pause>
A union of Micron workers in Taoyuan will strike on Thursday over bonuses, timed to a mediation meeting for the company's staff in Taichung. Micron employs about fifteen thousand people in Taiwan, where it makes the memory chips that feed AI servers, the very boom that's swelling its profits. Workers want a concrete profit-sharing proposal, and in a market this tight, even a short stoppage tests who really holds the leverage.
<long pause>
Five hundred and fifty thousand Ukrainian households lost power overnight after Russia fired sixteen ballistic missiles and more than two hundred drones at the grid. President Zelensky says twenty-eight substations and other energy sites were hit across eleven regions, and the Energy Ministry says air raid alerts are slowing repair crews. With winter approaching, Ukraine's engineers are rebuilding under fire... and the question is whether they can outpace the next salvo.

In science. <short pause>
New Zealand's temperatures have risen one point three six degrees Celsius since nineteen-oh-nine, according to the government's Our Atmosphere and Climate report for twenty twenty-six. The seas around the country have warmed thirty-four percent faster than the global average, the Tasman Sea is in a marine heatwave, and glaciers are expected to lose between half and eighty-five percent of their remaining ice this century. A record-strength El Niño is now forming on top of that baseline, and what it does to an already drier, windier north and east is still unwritten.
<long pause>
At an undersea volcano off the Pacific Northwest called Axial Seamount, a prototype geothermal plant built by Endurance Energy produced more power than it used... for eight point seven seconds. Fluid venting at two hundred and eighty-one degrees spun its turbine to twenty-six thousand revolutions a minute, before a battery software fault and a leaking cable ended the run. For island nations like Tonga, which runs its grid on imported diesel whose price has soared since the Iran war, seafloor heat could one day mean power that no strait can close.

In technology. <short pause>
ByteDance has split Seed, its large-model lab, into five divisions under Wu Yonghui, covering pretraining data, reinforcement learning, enterprise models, consumer chatbots and alignment. Giving safety a division of its own is reportedly a first among China's frontier labs, according to the mainland outlet TMTPost, and the shake-up comes alongside the departure of a co-founder. China's biggest-spending AI lab is betting that building models like an army is the way to keep pace... and that alignment deserves its own command.
<long pause>
Seventy fake news sites published three hundred and eighteen articles about the war in Congo, mimicking real outlets and rewriting stories for different audiences. Coordinated accounts on X amplified the network, and Congolese fact-checkers say they missed it entirely until Anthropic, the AI company, flagged it in a September report. In a war where information already moves faster than aid, flooding a conflict with fiction has become cheap... and the people doing the checking are a step behind.
<long pause>
Meta has admitted that its own AI tools generated images favouring Flávio Bolsonaro during Brazil's election campaign, according to the investigative outlet Agência Pública. The company earned two hundred and eighty-two million reais from boosted campaign posts, more than any other platform, and it shut down the Lula campaign's Instagram ad account on the ninth of October for what it calls a mistake lasting just over an hour. Brazilians are now asking what democracies everywhere will have to answer: what happens when the platform selling the ads also makes the images?
