## Top Stories This Week

### 1. The Iran War and Hormuz Endgame (~134 articles, ~32% of total output)
The defining story of the week — and perhaps the year. Coverage tracked the full arc: US-Israel strikes on Iranian energy infrastructure in late March, the IRGC taking de facto control of government as Khamenei recovered from severe injuries, ceasefire talks opening in Islamabad (the first direct US-Iran contact in years), and then Sunday's rupture as Trump announced a naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. The Quincy Institute analysis published Sunday crystallised what we'd been tracking all week: a blockade designed to pressure Iran inflicts more economic damage on US allies and the US domestic economy than on Tehran itself. The UK and France are now quietly coordinating to resume shipping through the Strait — America's closest allies working to undo what Washington just announced. This is the story of a war that was supposed to demonstrate American power and is instead revealing its limits.

### 2. Iran War Economic Cascade (~18 articles)
The downstream economic coverage was among the week's strongest editorial work. The cascade ran from the macro (high oil + high rates = global debt trap) to the visceral (a Haitian factory worker walking 2 hours each way to work because fuel is unaffordable). Ireland declared a fuel emergency. Bangladesh faces energy rationing. South Africa's largest food NGO warned of structural hunger driven partly by fertiliser supply chain disruption through Hormuz. The thread connecting these: a war fought 10,000km from the poorest people is costing them the most.

### 3. Nigeria's Compound Security Crisis (2 articles — but should be many more)
Two stories published Sunday captured the scale of Nigeria's security collapse: ~200 civilians killed by a Nigerian military airstrike on a Yobe market (Africa's deadliest single airstrike in years), and an Amnesty International report documenting 1,100+ kidnappings in northern Nigeria over four months. Neither story received the coverage volume it warranted. We ran them — but only once, and only on Sunday. This is a continent-scale human rights crisis that our pipeline is systematically under-resourced to cover.

### 4. Orbán Falls, Hungary Turns (1 article, but 16 years of arc)
Viktor Orbán's 16-year government ended in Sunday's election, with the centre-right Tisza party winning on a consolidating opposition vote. The editorial significance: the precedent that authoritarian-adjacent leaders backed by both Trump and Putin can lose when opposition unites around a single credible challenger. The mechanism matters for other countries watching — consolidation, not ideology, was decisive.

### 5. Artemis II at the Moon (7 articles across the week)
Consistent coverage of the first crewed lunar mission since Apollo. The 'Earthset' photo — Earth setting behind the Moon, 58 years after Earthrise — was the strongest single image of the week and a rare positive landmark in a relentlessly grim news cycle.

## Recommendations for Next Week

1. **Build a dedicated Africa coverage habit.** Nigeria's airstrike and kidnapping data ran the same day as throwaway auto-backfill. These deserve advance research: pre-identify one significant Africa story per cycle that isn't war-reactive (not just Boko Haram). Sudan, Sahel, East Africa should each appear at least once per week.

2. **Track Hormuz-dependent nations as a recurring beat.** The Philippines, Japan, South Korea, and Bangladesh each face existential energy exposure from the Hormuz blockade. Weekly check-ins on their diplomatic responses, energy price impacts, and government actions would give the Iran war coverage genuine global scale rather than its current Washington-Tehran bilateral framing.

3. **Elevate the slow climate story alongside the fast energy crisis story.** War-driven energy coverage is crowding out climate policy. The IPCC AR7 deadlock, climate finance gaps, and adaptation spending in the Global South are stories that compound the Iran war's damage — a deliberate pairing would strengthen both.

4. **Create a ceasefire/diplomacy tracker.** The Islamabad talks generated 20+ articles but without a consistent tracking framework: what was agreed, what collapsed, who holds leverage, what comes next. A recurring 'state of negotiations' piece — even 200 words per cycle — would anchor the coverage and prevent readers from losing the thread across days.
